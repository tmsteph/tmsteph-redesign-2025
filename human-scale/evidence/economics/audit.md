# Evidence Audit 07: Economics

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> An economy is a coordination system, a production system, and a distribution system. No ownership label tells us by itself whether ordinary people are flourishing inside it.

This audit puts Chapter 7 — *Economics: What Is the Economy For?* — under pressure.

The chapter currently blends claims about:

- markets;
- competition;
- entrepreneurship;
- material security;
- ownership;
- small business;
- cooperatives;
- public goods;
- monopoly;
- housing;
- automation;
- cash support;
- employee ownership;
- wealth;
- practical freedom.

Some are well-established economic mechanisms.

Others are normative choices or institution-specific hypotheses.

---

# Summary Judgment

**Keep practical freedom, material security, competition, broad opportunity to build ownership, and institutional pluralism near the center. Stop treating small/local/private/cooperative/public ownership as though any one form is intrinsically Human Scale.**

The strongest foundations are:

1. Extreme material insecurity restricts practical choice even when formal rights exist.
2. Markets can coordinate decentralized information and incentives effectively in many domains.
3. Markets can also produce monopoly power, externalities, information problems, under-provision of public goods, and unequal bargaining power.
4. Competition and entry can matter for innovation, prices, quality, and economic dynamism.
5. Cash-transfer and social-protection programs can reduce poverty and improve consumption/security in many contexts; design, targeting, administration, incentives, and fiscal sustainability still matter.
6. Ownership can create wealth, resilience, control, and upside; concentration of ownership can also concentrate power and risk.
7. Employee ownership, profit sharing, cooperatives, small enterprise, public ownership, and conventional investor-owned firms all have contexts in which they can work well or badly.
8. Productivity gains do not determine their own distribution.

The weaker or normative claims are:

- local business is inherently better than large business;
- small firms are inherently better employers;
- cooperatives reliably outperform investor-owned firms;
- employee ownership automatically creates worker power;
- universal basic income is proven superior to other security systems;
- public ownership is inherently more accountable;
- private ownership is inherently more efficient;
- inequality itself has one scientifically determined “correct” level;
- entrepreneurship is a moral requirement for everyone;
- homeownership is the best asset-building mechanism for every household.

# Claim Register

## Claim 1 — Material security affects practical freedom

**Grade: Strong as a conceptual and empirical constraint**

A person with no financial buffer may technically be free to:

- leave a job;
- move;
- care for a child;
- start a business;
- refuse an abusive landlord;
- retrain;
- survive an emergency.

But each option becomes harder when losing one paycheck means losing food, housing, medicine, transportation, or childcare.

Human Scale therefore uses **practical freedom** rather than formal choice alone.

This does not tell us which security mechanism is optimal.

Possible mechanisms include:

- wages;
- savings;
- insurance;
- family support;
- public benefits;
- cash transfers;
- universal services;
- portable benefits;
- ownership;
- credit;
- mutual aid.

**Doctrine implication:** Material security is an outcome/value. Programs should compete on how effectively and simply they create it.

Official reference:
- World Bank, Social Protection & Jobs: https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/socialprotection

---

## Claim 2 — Cash-transfer evidence is not the same as proof of UBI

**Grade: Strong corrective principle**

A large literature finds that cash-transfer programs can improve consumption, reduce poverty, support nutrition, schooling, assets, or resilience in many populations.

But “cash transfer” can mean:

- targeted poverty benefits;
- child allowances;
- pensions;
- emergency cash;
- conditional transfers;
- unconditional transfers;
- temporary pilots;
- universal payments.

Evidence from one design cannot automatically prove the effects of a permanent national universal basic income at a very different scale and tax structure.

**Doctrine implication:** Human Scale can support simple cash mechanisms where they work without claiming that UBI is scientifically settled.

Useful official overview:
- World Bank social protection work: https://www.worldbank.org/en/topic/socialprotection

---

## Claim 3 — Markets are powerful coordination tools, not moral verdicts

**Grade: Foundational economic mechanism**

Prices, contracts, entry, profit/loss, and decentralized choice can coordinate enormous amounts of distributed information.

Markets can be particularly effective where:

- buyers can compare alternatives;
- sellers can enter;
- harms fall mostly on the parties choosing;
- information is adequate;
- property/contract rules are clear;
- fraud is constrained;
- switching is realistic.

Markets can perform poorly where there are:

- monopoly or oligopoly;
- severe externalities;
- asymmetric information;
- public goods;
- network effects;
- natural monopolies;
- coercive bargaining conditions;
- high switching costs;
- systemic risk.

**Doctrine implication:** “Market” and “government” are institutional tools, not identities.

---

## Claim 4 — Competition deserves separate attention from private ownership

**Grade: Strong economic mechanism / context-dependent implementation**

An economy can contain private firms while still becoming highly concentrated.

Competition can affect:

- prices;
- quality;
- innovation;
- wages and labor-market options;
- supplier power;
- entrepreneurship;
- resilience.

Competition policy has to distinguish:

- productive scale economies;
- genuine innovation;
- network effects;
- anticompetitive conduct;
- regulatory barriers;
- acquisitions that reduce entry;
- natural-monopoly conditions.

**Doctrine implication:** Human Scale should care about **contestability and credible alternatives**, not simply the number of firms.

Official reference:
- OECD competition policy: https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/competition.html

---

## Claim 5 — Small businesses are not automatically more humane

**Grade: Corrective principle**

Small firms can provide:

- entrepreneurship;
- local knowledge;
- direct accountability;
- niche services;
- experimentation;
- community ties;
- pathways to ownership.

They can also have:

- lower wages;
- less formal HR capacity;
- weaker benefits;
- owner dependency;
- poor succession planning;
- low productivity;
- unsafe practices;
- little bargaining power with suppliers or landlords.

Large firms can create bureaucracy and concentration while also providing:

- scale;
- benefits;
- training;
- compliance capacity;
- capital;
- research;
- logistics;
- stable processes.

**Doctrine implication:** Preserve room for small enterprise without pretending “small” proves a better workplace or product.

---

## Claim 6 — Ownership can expand freedom and concentrate risk

**Grade: Strong structural mechanism**

Ownership can provide:

- wealth accumulation;
- income;
- voting/control rights;
- collateral;
- inheritance;
- business upside;
- housing stability;
- bargaining power;
- the ability to say no.

Ownership can also concentrate a household's risk in:

- one home;
- one employer;
- one small business;
- one local market;
- one stock or asset.

**Doctrine implication:** Human Scale should favor wider access to productive assets while also valuing diversification, portability, liquidity, and consumer/worker protection.

“Everyone should own a business” is not the goal.

**More people should have credible paths to assets and upside.**

---

## Claim 7 — Employee ownership is promising and heterogeneous

**Grade: Moderate / design-dependent**

Employee ownership can take many forms:

- ESOPs;
- worker cooperatives;
- stock grants;
- options;
- profit sharing;
- partnership stakes.

Potential benefits can include:

- wealth building;
- alignment;
- retention;
- participation;
- broader distribution of capital gains.

Potential problems include:

- concentration of retirement wealth in the employer;
- symbolic ownership without meaningful voice;
- illiquidity;
- governance complexity;
- unequal grants;
- workers bearing both employment and investment risk.

**Doctrine implication:** Keep employee ownership as an experiment class. Measure wealth, risk, voice, wages, productivity, turnover, governance, and diversification.

---

## Claim 8 — Cooperatives are an institutional option, not a universal answer

**Grade: Context-dependent institutional form**

Cooperatives can align governance with members who are:

- workers;
- consumers;
- producers;
- residents;
- borrowers.

They can be effective where member interests are sufficiently aligned and governance remains workable.

They can also face:

- capital constraints;
- slow decision-making;
- member conflict;
- free-rider problems;
- governance fatigue;
- professional-management challenges.

**Doctrine implication:** Human Scale should protect room for cooperatives without treating democratic ownership as proof of efficient or fair outcomes.

---

## Claim 9 — Public goods require institutions beyond ordinary consumer markets

**Grade: Foundational economic principle**

Some goods are difficult to fund through ordinary individual purchase because benefits are non-excludable, shared, or spill across users.

Examples can include aspects of:

- basic research;
- public health surveillance;
- flood protection;
- national defense;
- clean air;
- some infrastructure;
- public information.

Possible mechanisms include:

- taxation;
- public provision;
- regulated utilities;
- procurement;
- philanthropy;
- commons governance;
- hybrid models.

**Doctrine implication:** Human Scale should ask what institution fits the good rather than force every activity into market or state categories.

---

## Claim 10 — Public ownership is not automatically public accountability

**Grade: Corrective principle**

Public ownership can support:

- universal access;
- long time horizons;
- infrastructure investment;
- public-good goals;
- natural-monopoly governance.

It can also create:

- political patronage;
- weak performance incentives;
- opaque bureaucracy;
- capture;
- underinvestment;
- difficulty exiting poor service.

**Doctrine implication:** Public systems need performance measures, transparency, independent oversight, user voice, and clear accountability just as private systems need competition, regulation, or exit.

---

## Claim 11 — Inequality is not one variable

**Grade: Strong corrective principle + normative debate**

Different questions include:

- poverty;
- income inequality;
- wealth inequality;
- consumption inequality;
- mobility;
- opportunity;
- political influence;
- regional inequality;
- intergenerational persistence.

Human Scale should avoid treating a single inequality ratio as the complete measure of justice.

A society could have equal poverty.

A society could have unequal wealth with high mobility and broad prosperity.

A society could have high average income with extreme political concentration.

**Doctrine implication:** Measure the problem we actually care about.

The Human Scale values are:

- nobody trapped in severe deprivation;
- credible paths to capability and ownership;
- limits on forms of concentration that destroy competition, rights, or democratic accountability;
- practical freedom for ordinary people.

The exact distribution remains a political question.

---

## Claim 12 — Entrepreneurship is valuable and should not become compulsory identity

**Grade: Value + economic mechanism**

Entrepreneurs can create:

- firms;
- products;
- jobs;
- experiments;
- local services;
- new technologies;
- ownership pathways.

But many people reasonably prefer:

- stable employment;
- public service;
- skilled trades;
- caregiving;
- art;
- research;
- teaching;
- retirement;
- part-time work.

A healthy economy should make entrepreneurship possible without treating everyone who wants employment rather than ownership as insufficiently independent.

---

## Claim 13 — Housing is both shelter and asset, creating policy tension

**Grade: Strong structural observation**

When housing functions simultaneously as:

- essential shelter;
- household savings;
- speculative asset;
- retirement strategy;
- collateral;
- tax base;
- local political interest,

policies that increase affordability can conflict with incumbent expectations of asset appreciation.

**Doctrine implication:** Human Scale should separate the goals:

- stable housing;
- housing supply;
- asset building;
- neighborhood quality;
- mobility;
- tenant security;
- homeowner security.

No single tenure model satisfies all goals.

---

## Claim 14 — Automation creates a distribution question, not an automatic post-work future

**Grade: Strong political-economy principle**

Automation can increase productivity and change labor demand.

The gains can flow through:

- wages;
- prices;
- profits;
- capital gains;
- tax revenue;
- public benefits;
- shorter hours;
- new jobs;
- reduced employment in affected tasks.

**Doctrine implication:** Human Scale should make distribution explicit without pretending technology determines the moral answer.

Broad ownership, competition, education, safety nets, wage institutions, taxation, and work-time policy are possible mechanisms.

# Recommended Chapter Changes

## Change 1 — Practical freedom

Keep practical freedom as the central economic outcome:

> people should be able to survive disruption, refuse some bad bargains, build capability, and have credible paths to assets and upside.

Do not imply this requires one welfare or ownership architecture.

## Change 2 — Institutional pluralism

Use:

> markets, public systems, cooperatives, nonprofits, households, commons, and hybrid institutions should be matched to the problem they solve.

## Change 3 — Ownership

Replace romantic “ownership society” language with:

> broaden realistic access to assets, capital gains, housing stability, business ownership, retirement savings, and other forms of economic upside while managing concentration and household risk.

## Change 4 — Security

Treat cash support, services, insurance, family support, savings, and employment protections as a portfolio of possible security mechanisms.

Measure:

- poverty;
- take-up;
- administrative burden;
- work incentives;
- income volatility;
- health;
- housing stability;
- fiscal cost;
- political durability.

## Change 5 — Competition

Make contestability a stronger Human Scale concern:

> practical exit requires credible alternatives. Monopoly can make nominal consumer or worker choice largely symbolic.

## Change 6 — Small/local business

Use:

> preserve low barriers to entry and room for small firms because experimentation and ownership matter, while applying health, safety, labor, environmental, and consumer standards according to real risk rather than romantic scale.

---

# What Chapter 7 Gets Right

The chapter is strongest when it says:

- economics should serve human life;
- material security can expand freedom;
- ownership matters;
- markets are useful and limited;
- entrepreneurship needs room;
- public goods require other institutions;
- automation gains create distribution choices;
- monopoly and concentrated power matter;
- no single ownership model should become doctrine.

It is weakest wherever local/small/cooperative ownership can be read as automatically more humane.

---

# What Would Change Our Minds?

Human Scale should revise favored economic proposals if stronger evidence shows that:

- simple security programs materially reduce employment, investment, or fiscal capacity more than they improve practical freedom;
- targeted systems outperform universal systems without unacceptable administrative burden or exclusion;
- universal systems outperform targeted systems once take-up, stigma, error, and bureaucracy are counted;
- employee ownership consistently concentrates risk without meaningful wealth or voice benefits;
- cooperative forms underperform conventional ownership in important domains without compensating benefits;
- large firms consistently deliver better wages, benefits, innovation, accessibility, or resilience than local alternatives;
- competition policy reduces useful scale economies or innovation more than it improves contestability;
- public ownership outperforms regulated private provision in one domain or vice versa;
- broad ownership policies inflate asset prices without expanding genuine household wealth.

Human Scale should be willing to find that a large corporation, public utility, cooperative, family business, or cash program is the best institution for a particular problem.

---

# Result of Audit 07

**Chapter 7 survives, but “human-scale economics” becomes institutional pluralism organized around practical freedom rather than a preferred ownership aesthetic.**

The stronger principle is:

> An economy should produce useful goods and services, preserve competition and innovation, protect people from catastrophic insecurity, give ordinary people credible paths to assets and upside, and prevent either public or private power from becoming effectively unchallengeable.

Markets are tools.

Government is a tool.

Ownership is a tool.

Security systems are tools.

**The measure is whether ordinary people gain real capability, real alternatives, and a fair chance to build a life.**
