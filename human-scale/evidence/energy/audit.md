# Evidence Audit 06: Energy

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> Energy abundance is a value. Reliability, affordability, emissions, health effects, land use, and resilience are outcomes to measure.

This audit puts Chapter 6 — *Energy: Power Without Dependence* — under pressure.

The chapter tries to hold several ideas together:

- modern energy is liberating;
- fossil-fuel combustion creates serious environmental and health costs;
- low-carbon electricity should expand;
- nuclear and renewables should be judged empirically rather than tribally;
- grids matter;
- local generation can increase resilience;
- efficiency matters;
- communities should retain some energy agency;
- abundance is preferable to enforced scarcity.

Most of these survive.

Several need sharper definitions.

---

# Summary Judgment

**Keep abundant, reliable, lower-pollution energy as a central Human Scale goal. Stop treating “local,” “distributed,” “renewable,” “nuclear,” or “grid-scale” as proxies for resilience or virtue. Energy performance is system performance.**

The strongest foundations are:

1. Modern energy access supports health, communication, refrigeration, water systems, education, industry, transportation, and material living standards.
2. Fossil-fuel combustion contributes substantially to greenhouse-gas emissions and harmful air pollution.
3. Wind, solar, hydro, and nuclear generally have much lower lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions than unabated fossil generation.
4. Variable wind and solar require system integration through combinations of grids, flexible demand, storage, geographic diversity, firm generation, and other resources.
5. Transmission and distribution networks are critical infrastructure, not merely passive wires.
6. Distributed energy can improve some resilience objectives, but only if the system is designed to operate during the relevant outage or disruption.
7. Nuclear can provide low-carbon firm electricity and energy security benefits while carrying real capital-cost, project-delivery, waste, safety, regulatory, and institutional demands.
8. No generation technology is impact-free.

The weaker or explicitly normative claims are:

- society should maximize energy consumption;
- local ownership automatically improves energy outcomes;
- microgrids are always more resilient than large networks;
- renewable-only or nuclear-heavy systems are universally optimal;
- energy independence is always preferable to interdependence;
- efficiency alone can solve demand growth;
- the lowest monetary cost is always the correct energy objective.

# Claim Register

## Claim 1 — Modern energy access is foundational to modern capability

**Grade: Strong / development and infrastructure foundation**

Reliable electricity enables or supports:

- refrigeration;
- lighting;
- communications;
- healthcare;
- pumping and water treatment;
- sanitation;
- education;
- computing;
- industrial production;
- cooling and heating;
- mobility and charging;
- emergency response.

Global SDG 7 tracking treats access to electricity and clean cooking as basic development goals.

Human Scale therefore rejects romantic energy poverty.

**Doctrine implication:** The project should seek abundant useful energy, not aesthetic austerity.

Official reference:
- Tracking SDG 7: https://trackingsdg7.esmap.org/

---

## Claim 2 — Fossil combustion creates serious climate and health externalities

**Grade: Strong**

Coal, oil, and natural gas combustion contribute to greenhouse-gas emissions. Combustion-related air pollution also creates major health burdens.

The exact impact depends on fuel, controls, upstream methane, plant efficiency, geography, exposure, and other factors.

Human Scale can confidently say:

> **energy systems should internalize rather than hide major pollution and climate costs.**

Official sources:
- IPCC AR6 WGIII: https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/
- WHO ambient air pollution: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ambient-(outdoor)-air-quality-and-health
- WHO household air pollution: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/household-air-pollution-and-health

---

## Claim 3 — Low-carbon generation technologies differ greatly from unabated fossil generation in lifecycle emissions

**Grade: Strong**

Lifecycle assessment includes construction, materials, fuel cycles, operations, and decommissioning rather than only direct smokestack emissions.

Across major assessments, wind, solar, hydro, and nuclear are generally far below unabated coal and natural gas in lifecycle greenhouse-gas intensity.

Exact values vary by technology, location, supply chain, capacity factor, methane leakage, manufacturing energy, and methodological assumptions.

**Doctrine implication:** Avoid arguments based only on operational emissions or only on construction impacts. Use lifecycle and system-level accounting.

Primary/official references:
- IPCC AR6 WGIII Chapter 6: https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-6/
- NREL Life Cycle Assessment Harmonization: https://www.nrel.gov/analysis/life-cycle-assessment.html

---

## Claim 4 — Reliability is a system property

**Grade: Strong engineering principle**

No single generator provides “the grid.”

Reliability emerges from combinations of:

- generation;
- transmission;
- distribution;
- reserves;
- frequency and voltage control;
- fuel availability;
- storage;
- demand response;
- forecasting;
- maintenance;
- cybersecurity;
- weatherization;
- interconnection;
- operator practices.

A system with many renewable generators can be reliable if integration is strong.

A system with dispatchable generators can still fail because of fuel, weather, transmission, maintenance, or common-mode problems.

**Doctrine implication:** Stop debating technologies as isolated talismans. Evaluate portfolios and failure modes.

Official reference:
- IEA, *Electricity Grids and Secure Energy Transitions*: https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-grids-and-secure-energy-transitions

---

## Claim 5 — Variable renewables create integration requirements, not an automatic reliability verdict

**Grade: Strong**

Wind and solar output vary with weather and time.

That creates system needs that can be met through different combinations of:

- geographic diversity;
- stronger transmission;
- short- and long-duration storage;
- flexible demand;
- dispatchable generation;
- hydro;
- nuclear;
- geothermal;
- interconnection;
- overbuilding and curtailment;
- forecasting;
- sector coupling.

The cost and practicality of high shares depend heavily on system context.

**Doctrine implication:** Neither “renewables are unreliable” nor “storage solves everything” is a sufficient system analysis.

Official reference:
- IEA grid-integration work and grid report: https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-grids-and-secure-energy-transitions

---

## Claim 6 — Grids are enabling infrastructure and can become a bottleneck

**Grade: Strong / current infrastructure consensus**

Transmission and distribution allow:

- geographic diversity;
- power sharing;
- balancing;
- access to generation resources;
- resilience through multiple supply paths;
- electrification of new loads.

They can also become congested, slow to build, vulnerable to weather, cyberattack, physical attack, wildfire, or institutional delay.

The IEA has highlighted grid expansion and modernization as critical to electricity-system transitions.

**Doctrine implication:** Human Scale should not confuse decentralization with avoiding grid investment. Strong shared networks and local capability can complement one another.

---

## Claim 7 — Distributed generation is not automatically backup power

**Grade: Strong engineering correction**

A grid-connected rooftop solar system may shut down during an outage for safety unless it has appropriate inverter/islanding capability and system design.

A battery, generator, controllable load, microgrid controller, or other resources may be required depending on the resilience objective.

Distributed resources can improve resilience when they are designed around the relevant failure mode.

**Doctrine implication:** Replace “local generation = resilience” with:

> **local capability can improve resilience when it can actually serve critical loads during the disruption being planned for.**

---

## Claim 8 — Microgrids can improve some resilience outcomes and add complexity/cost

**Grade: Context-dependent engineering strategy**

Microgrids can isolate from a larger grid and support critical loads.

They can be valuable for:

- hospitals;
- military facilities;
- campuses;
- remote communities;
- shelters;
- emergency services;
- critical infrastructure.

They can also require:

- controls;
- protection coordination;
- storage or firm generation;
- maintenance;
- fuel;
- cybersecurity;
- capital investment;
- skilled operation.

Human Scale should keep microgrids as a resilience tool, not a universal architecture.

---

## Claim 9 — Nuclear power can provide low-carbon firm generation

**Grade: Strong for low lifecycle emissions and firm output; economics/institutional performance vary**

Nuclear plants can produce large quantities of low-carbon electricity with high capacity factors and comparatively small direct land footprints.

They also involve:

- high capital requirements;
- long development and construction timelines in many jurisdictions;
- project-delivery risk;
- regulatory capacity;
- safety systems;
- radioactive waste management;
- decommissioning;
- security and proliferation concerns;
- cooling-water and siting constraints.

The IEA has argued that nuclear can contribute to secure low-emissions energy transitions while also recognizing investment and policy challenges.

**Doctrine implication:** Human Scale should remain technologically neutral enough to support nuclear where it performs well and reject it where costs, institutions, risks, or alternatives make it poor fit.

Official reference:
- IEA, *Nuclear Power and Secure Energy Transitions*: https://www.iea.org/reports/nuclear-power-and-secure-energy-transitions

---

## Claim 10 — Renewable energy also has material, land, ecological, and infrastructure impacts

**Grade: Strong**

Solar, wind, hydro, geothermal, biomass, transmission, batteries, and other low-carbon technologies require land, materials, manufacturing, mines, roads, grid connections, and maintenance.

Impacts vary greatly.

Possible concerns include:

- habitat;
- bird/bat mortality;
- river ecology;
- land competition;
- mining;
- community consent;
- waste and recycling;
- transmission corridors;
- visual and noise impacts.

**Doctrine implication:** “Renewable” does not mean impact-free. Compare impacts per unit of useful system output and over full lifecycles.

---

## Claim 11 — Energy efficiency is valuable but not a complete abundance strategy

**Grade: Strong for reducing energy required for a given service; rebound/context matter**

Efficient buildings, motors, appliances, industrial systems, vehicles, heat pumps, and controls can reduce energy demand for the same service.

But societies may use some savings to consume more services.

Human Scale should value efficiency because waste is expensive and often polluting.

It should not rely on efficiency as a substitute for building enough supply, networks, and capacity.

The principle is:

> **use energy efficiently and build enough clean capability for legitimate human needs and future growth.**

---

## Claim 12 — Energy affordability matters alongside decarbonization

**Grade: Strong social/economic constraint**

Electricity and fuels are inputs into nearly every other system.

High or volatile prices affect:

- households;
- heating/cooling;
- transportation;
- industry;
- food;
- housing;
- public services;
- competitiveness.

A policy that lowers emissions while making essential energy inaccessible can create serious human costs.

A policy that keeps energy cheap by externalizing pollution also creates costs.

**Doctrine implication:** Evaluate total system cost, distribution, health, environmental effects, reliability, and long-run investment rather than one headline price.

---

## Claim 13 — Interdependence can increase or reduce resilience

**Grade: Structural tradeoff**

Energy independence sounds resilient because it reduces reliance on external suppliers.

Interconnection can also increase resilience by allowing regions to share power, fuels, equipment, expertise, and reserves.

Dependency becomes dangerous when:

- suppliers are concentrated;
- routes are fragile;
- inventories are inadequate;
- alternatives are unavailable;
- political coercion is plausible;
- correlated failures spread through the network.

**Doctrine implication:** The goal is not isolation. It is diversified dependency with credible fallback.

# Recommended Chapter Changes

## Change 1 — Abundance

Keep energy abundance as an explicit value, but define it as:

> enough reliable, affordable, lower-pollution energy to support health, comfort, mobility, industry, science, computation, food systems, water, and future capability without treating waste as progress.

## Change 2 — Local resilience

Replace any implication that distributed generation is automatically resilient with:

> local resources improve resilience when they can serve critical loads during the relevant failure and are maintained, fueled, controlled, and tested accordingly.

## Change 3 — Technology neutrality

Strengthen:

> nuclear, wind, solar, hydro, geothermal, storage, transmission, demand flexibility, efficiency, fossil fuels with or without carbon controls, and emerging technologies should be evaluated as parts of systems rather than political identities.

## Change 4 — Grids

Make explicit that large grids can be a Human Scale technology when they:

- increase reliability;
- lower costs;
- share diverse resources;
- support decarbonization;
- remain governable and resilient.

Scale justifies itself when the network effect is the benefit.

## Change 5 — Resilience

Define resilience through scenarios:

- outage duration;
- critical loads;
- fuel disruption;
- heat/cold;
- wildfire;
- storm;
- cyberattack;
- equipment failure;
- transmission loss;
- regional shortage.

Then choose architecture.

---

# What Chapter 6 Gets Right

The chapter is strongest when it says:

- energy poverty is not virtuous;
- reliable power enables human flourishing;
- pollution should not be hidden;
- nuclear and renewables should not become tribal identities;
- local capability and regional grids can complement each other;
- efficiency and abundance are compatible;
- communities and households benefit from understanding critical dependencies;
- energy systems should be evaluated by actual failure modes.

It is weakest where “local” or “distributed” can be read as inherently resilient.

---

# What Would Change Our Minds?

Human Scale should revise preferred energy directions if evidence shows that:

- a favored generation mix cannot provide reliability at acceptable total system cost;
- local resilience systems are rarely maintained or fail during actual emergencies;
- transmission expansion creates environmental or social costs larger than alternatives in specific corridors;
- nuclear projects consistently fail cost/time/safety/institutional tests in a jurisdiction;
- renewable-heavy portfolios produce persistent reliability or material constraints that cannot be economically mitigated;
- fossil generation with credible emissions controls outperforms lower-carbon alternatives on total health/climate/system outcomes in a specific use case;
- efficiency investments cost more than building cleaner supply for the same outcome;
- energy policies materially worsen poverty or industrial capacity without compensating benefits.

Human Scale should also strengthen technologies that repeatedly outperform expectations.

The doctrine does not owe loyalty to a fuel.

---

# Result of Audit 06

**Chapter 6 survives with a stronger system-level definition of abundance and resilience.**

The revised principle is:

> A Human Scale energy system should provide abundant useful energy while minimizing pollution and catastrophic risk, maintaining affordability, and surviving credible failures. Local capability, large grids, nuclear, renewables, storage, efficiency, and other technologies should justify themselves through system performance rather than identity.

Energy is one of the clearest examples of the doctrine's scale rule.

Sometimes the human-scale answer is a rooftop battery.

Sometimes it is a continental transmission network.

**Scale should have to justify itself — and in energy, network scale often can.**
