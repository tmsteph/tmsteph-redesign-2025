# Cross-Cutting Audit 05 — Auditing the Human Scale Audit

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> A self-reflection tool can be useful before it is scientifically validated. The dangerous moment is when a convenient score begins to look more objective than the questions underneath it.

The Human Scale Audit is one of the project's best public entry points because it asks people to look at their actual environment rather than agree with a doctrine.

It is also one of the easiest places for Human Scale to accidentally manufacture false precision.

The tool is currently presented as provisional, private, no-signup, and not a scientific or medical diagnostic. Those disclaimers are important but not sufficient.

The next step is not to make the score sound more scientific.

It is to test whether the questions are understandable, distinct, fair across different people, responsive to meaningful change, and useful without implying a universal formula for a good life.

---

# 1. Define the purpose before validating the score

The Audit can serve at least four different purposes:

1. **reflection** — help a person notice friction in their environment;
2. **conversation** — give households or groups common language for tradeoffs;
3. **experiment design** — identify one dimension to change and observe;
4. **measurement** — estimate a construct reliably enough to compare people, places, or time.

The first three require less psychometric evidence than the fourth.

Human Scale should not drift from “reflection tool” into “measurement instrument” merely because the interface produces a number.

The public page should continue to say clearly:

**This is a reflection tool, not a diagnostic or scientifically validated index.**

If the project later wants comparative measurement, that should be treated as a separate research program.

---

# 2. A total score may be the weakest part of the tool

Human flourishing is multidimensional.

Two people can receive the same total score while living radically different lives:

- one has strong relationships and terrible sleep;
- another sleeps well and is profoundly isolated;
- one lacks money but has abundant time and support;
- another has high material security but no control over time;
- one lives with disability and excellent assistive support;
- another has no disability but an inaccessible environment relative to their actual needs.

Adding unlike dimensions together creates three risks:

- **compensation:** a high score in one area appears to cancel a serious problem in another;
- **hidden values:** the weighting implies how much nature, money, community, time, or meaning ought to matter relative to each other;
- **false ranking:** a 73 can look objectively better than a 68 even when the difference is arbitrary.

Human Scale should test whether the total adds value beyond a profile of dimension scores.

A serious possibility is to keep the total only as a playful summary — or remove it entirely if users overinterpret it.

---

# 3. Content-validity audit

Before statistical calibration, ask whether the questions represent the intended domain.

For every item, document:

- which Human Scale construct it is intended to measure;
- why that construct belongs in the Audit;
- whether it is evidence-backed, value-based, or exploratory;
- what populations might interpret it differently;
- what important situations it misses;
- whether it accidentally measures income, geography, disability, household form, or culture instead of the intended concept;
- whether a different item already measures nearly the same thing.

Outside reviewers should include people who disagree with Human Scale, not only supporters.

The question is not “does this item sound good?”

It is:

**what exactly does an answer to this question tell us?**

---

# 4. Cognitive interviews before large samples

A small number of careful interviews can reveal more than hundreds of unexamined scores.

Ask people to think aloud while answering:

- What do you think this question means?
- What examples are you considering?
- Which answer options feel impossible to map onto your life?
- Does the question assume something about family, housing, work, disability, money, religion, transportation, or culture?
- Are you answering what happens, what you prefer, or what you believe should happen?
- Would your answer change depending on the week?
- Is the question asking two things at once?

Record misunderstanding patterns and rewrite before doing formal reliability analysis.

---

# 5. Separate environment from preference

Some Human Scale dimensions concern environmental opportunity. Others concern personal desire.

Example:

A person may have abundant opportunities for social interaction and genuinely prefer solitude.

Another may desperately want connection and lack access.

If the Audit asks only “how social is your life?” it can confuse:

- opportunity;
- behavior;
- preference;
- satisfaction;
- need.

Where useful, questions should distinguish:

**Can you? Do you? Do you want to? Is the current amount working for you?**

This correction matters for community, nature, movement, spirituality, technology, work, and place.

---

# 6. Disability and accessibility stress test

The Audit should be reviewed against the first cross-cutting audit.

Questions about walking, exercise, cooking, driving, nature, independence, work, community, and technology can accidentally grade disability rather than environmental support.

A better item asks whether the person's environment provides realistic capability **given their body, tools, assistance, and preferences**.

Test for cases such as:

- wheelchair users;
- blind and low-vision users;
- Deaf and hard-of-hearing users;
- chronic pain and fatigue;
- autism and sensory differences;
- ADHD and executive-function support;
- cognitive or intellectual disability;
- temporary injury;
- medically necessary technology;
- people using caregivers or personal assistance.

A person should not receive a lower “human” score merely because their independence is supported rather than solitary.

---

# 7. Age and life-stage stress test

Questions can function differently for:

- adolescents;
- young adults;
- parents of infants;
- caregivers;
- adults working multiple jobs;
- retirees;
- older adults with changing mobility;
- people near end-of-life;
- students;
- unemployed people;
- people in institutions or temporary housing.

A score may reflect life stage rather than environmental quality.

Human Scale should either:

- build different modules;
- allow items to be marked not applicable;
- or explicitly limit which populations the general Audit is meant to describe.

Do not hide population limits inside a universal-looking score.

---

# 8. Cultural and household-form stress test

Review items for assumptions about:

- individualism versus interdependence;
- nuclear versus extended family;
- religion and spirituality;
- urban versus rural life;
- home ownership;
- paid versus unpaid work;
- food preparation;
- privacy;
- leisure;
- transportation;
- household crowding;
- multigenerational living;
- gender roles;
- norms around children and elders.

The objective is not a culturally empty instrument.

Every instrument embodies concepts.

The objective is to know which concepts are being measured and avoid mistaking one cultural arrangement for universal flourishing.

---

# 9. Check for double-counting

Human Scale concepts are causally entangled.

For example:

- a long commute can reduce time, movement, sleep, family contact, and community;
- low income can reduce food access, housing quality, time, nature access, transport, and agency;
- disability can affect work, transport, time, place, and technology depending on support;
- neighborhood design can affect mobility, social contact, heat, nature, and time.

If the Audit scores each downstream effect independently, one upstream problem can be counted several times.

That may be acceptable for a lived-experience profile but misleading for a causal index.

The methodology should state which interpretation is intended.

---

# 10. Reliability comes after comprehension

If the questions survive qualitative review, test:

## Test–retest stability

Have people repeat the Audit after a short period when no major change is expected.

If scores swing wildly, identify whether:

- items are ambiguous;
- the construct genuinely varies day to day;
- the response scale is too sensitive;
- recent mood dominates the answer.

## Internal structure

Do questions claimed to belong to the same dimension actually move together?

Do supposedly different dimensions collapse into one factor such as income, housing security, or general life satisfaction?

Do some items correlate so strongly that they are redundant?

Do not optimize statistical neatness at the expense of substantive meaning.

---

# 11. Sensitivity to meaningful change

A useful self-audit should change when life meaningfully changes.

Potential small experiments:

- commute reduction;
- a schedule becoming predictable;
- improved sleep opportunity;
- a mobility accommodation;
- a new recurring community connection;
- removing disruptive notifications;
- gaining safe nearby outdoor access;
- moving into more accessible housing;
- reducing an administrative burden;
- obtaining needed caregiving support.

Ask whether the relevant dimension moves in the expected direction without unrelated dimensions changing for no reason.

This does not prove causality.

It tests whether the tool can at least notice the kinds of changes it claims to reflect.

---

# 12. Differential item functioning and fairness

If sample size eventually permits, test whether people with similar underlying circumstances answer particular items differently because of:

- disability;
- age;
- gender;
- language;
- culture;
- country;
- urban/rural setting;
- household form;
- income;
- employment status;
- parent/caregiver status;
- religion/nonreligion.

Statistical fairness analysis should follow substantive review, not replace it.

A biased concept cannot be repaired merely by adjusting coefficients.

---

# 13. Weighting must be transparent

If a total score exists, publish exactly how it is calculated.

For each weight, state whether it comes from:

- equal weighting for simplicity;
- empirical prediction of an outcome;
- expert judgment;
- user preference;
- democratic/public deliberation;
- explicit Human Scale values.

Do not allow weights to look empirical when they are normative.

A strong future design could let users view:

1. **unweighted profile**;
2. **Human Scale default weighting** — explicitly normative;
3. **personal weighting** — what matters most to this user;
4. no total score at all.

This would make disagreement visible rather than hide it in arithmetic.

---

# 14. Do not invent clinical thresholds

Avoid labels such as:

- unhealthy;
- normal;
- deficient;
- at risk;
- optimal;

unless a validated instrument and appropriate evidence justify them for that specific construct.

Safer result language is descriptive:

- “This area appears to create more friction for you.”
- “You reported less control over time than over other dimensions.”
- “This might be a useful area to investigate.”
- “Your priorities may differ from the default weighting.”

The Audit should invite inquiry rather than pronounce a diagnosis.

---

# 15. Privacy should remain a product feature

The current local/no-signup design is philosophically aligned with the project's data-minimization values.

Calibration work should avoid destroying that advantage.

Possible models:

- opt-in anonymous research mode;
- clearly separated research consent;
- local score by default;
- explicit data fields shown before submission;
- no sensitive data collection unless needed for a defined fairness analysis;
- short retention;
- published data dictionary;
- aggregate reporting with disclosure safeguards;
- easy withdrawal where identifiable data is retained.

Research convenience is not permission to silently convert a private reflection tool into behavioral surveillance.

---

# 16. Minimum calibration sequence

## Phase 1 — Expert and adversarial review

- Evidence reviewers
- Accessibility reviewers
- Psychometrics / survey-method review
- Critics of Human Scale

## Phase 2 — Cognitive interviews

Small, diverse sample. Rewrite aggressively.

## Phase 3 — Usability/accessibility testing

Mobile, screen reader, keyboard, low vision, cognitive load, plain language, slow connection.

## Phase 4 — Pilot sample

Test missingness, distributions, completion, test–retest, item redundancy, and dimension structure.

## Phase 5 — Fairness stress test

Look for systematic item problems across populations.

## Phase 6 — Intervention sensitivity

Use Lab experiments to see whether targeted real-life changes register sensibly.

## Phase 7 — Decide whether a total score deserves to survive

Do not assume the answer is yes.

---

# 17. Public success criteria

The Audit becomes stronger when:

- users understand the questions similarly enough;
- people can mark irrelevant items without being punished;
- disability and support needs are not graded as personal failure;
- dimension scores represent meaningfully distinct constructs;
- important changes move the relevant dimensions;
- weights are transparent;
- results do not imply clinical validity;
- users find at least one useful question or experiment;
- critics can explain how scoring works and challenge it;
- the tool remains privacy-respecting.

The most important success metric may not be the score.

It may be:

**Did the tool help someone notice a real source of friction and choose a sensible next experiment?**

---

# Result of Cross-Cutting Audit 05

**The Human Scale Audit should be treated as a hypothesis-generating reflection tool until its questions, dimensions, weighting, accessibility, reliability, and fairness survive real testing.**

A number is useful only if it clarifies reality.

If the total score creates more false certainty than insight, Human Scale should be willing to delete it.
