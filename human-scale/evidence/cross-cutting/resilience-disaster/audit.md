# Cross-Cutting Audit 06 — Resilience & Disaster

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> Resilience is not self-sufficiency. It is the ability of people and systems to keep essential functions working, adapt when something fails, recover without abandoning the most vulnerable, and learn before the next disruption.

Resilience appears across Energy, Food, Water, Technology, Transportation, Community, Governance, Health, Disability, Housing, Economics, and Localism.

Human Scale is attracted to local capability because concentrated systems can create large failure domains.

The audit keeps the value of redundancy, distributed capability, local knowledge, and mutual aid while rejecting the idea that smaller systems are automatically more resilient.

**Resilience is a property of a network, not a synonym for decentralization.**

---

# 1. Start with the functions society must preserve

**Grade: Strong resilience-planning framework**

NIST's Community Resilience Planning Guide focuses on how buildings and infrastructure systems support social and economic functions and how communities can plan for recovery of those functions after hazards.

Source:
- NIST, Community Resilience Planning Guide: https://www.nist.gov/community-resilience/planning-guide

FEMA's Community Lifelines similarly organizes response and stabilization around essential functions rather than individual assets in isolation.

Source:
- FEMA, Community Lifelines: https://www.fema.gov/emergency-managers/practitioners/lifelines

Human Scale resilience should therefore begin with questions such as:

- Can people obtain safe water?
- Can critical medical care continue?
- Can people remain at safe temperatures?
- Can emergency information reach them?
- Can responders move?
- Can food and medication reach communities?
- Can sanitation continue?
- Can people communicate?
- Can essential payments and identity systems function?
- Can disabled and medically dependent people maintain life-sustaining equipment and support?

The specific infrastructure matters because of the function it supports.

---

# 2. Redundancy is useful and costly

Resilient systems often benefit from multiple ways to perform an essential function.

Examples can include:

- multiple transmission paths;
- backup generation;
- local batteries or microgrids;
- multiple water sources or storage;
- multiple communication channels;
- road and transit alternatives;
- inventory buffers;
- multiple suppliers;
- distributed medical capability;
- mutual-aid agreements;
- redundant data and recovery systems.

But redundancy costs money, land, maintenance, attention, inventory, and operational complexity.

Unused backup systems can quietly fail.

Human Scale should therefore reject “more redundancy is always better.”

The question is:

**Which function is critical enough, and which failure is plausible enough, to justify the cost of another path?**

---

# 3. Local capability and large-system capacity are complements

A neighborhood can check on residents, share tools, distribute information, cool food temporarily, operate a shelter, or coordinate rides.

It cannot independently replace:

- a regional electric grid;
- trauma care;
- wildfire suppression;
- weather forecasting;
- national logistics;
- large-scale water treatment;
- telecommunications backbones;
- mass evacuation capacity;
- specialized search and rescue;
- public finance for catastrophic recovery.

Human Scale should favor **nested resilience**:

- household capability;
- neighborhood/community response;
- city/county capacity;
- regional mutual aid;
- state and national logistics;
- international support when necessary.

The right question is not “local or centralized?”

It is:

**what must still work at each scale when another scale is degraded?**

---

# 4. Community relationships can improve response — but social capital is uneven

Neighbors can provide:

- wellness checks;
- rides;
- meals;
- translation;
- childcare;
- information;
- evacuation help;
- tools;
- temporary shelter;
- knowledge of who may need help.

But relying on informal networks can disadvantage people who are:

- socially isolated;
- new to the area;
- stigmatized;
- disabled;
- poor;
- undocumented;
- estranged from family;
- outside dominant religious or cultural groups;
- unable to communicate easily.

Mutual aid is valuable.

It must complement public obligations and professional response rather than determine who deserves rescue based on social connectedness.

---

# 5. Disability and medical dependence change resilience design

CDC emergency-preparedness guidance highlights that people with disabilities may require additional planning around communication, transportation, medical care, power, supplies, and support networks.

Source:
- CDC, Emergency Preparedness and Disability Inclusion: https://www.cdc.gov/disability-emergency-preparedness/about/index.html

A power outage can be an inconvenience for one household and a medical emergency for another.

Human Scale resilience planning should account for:

- electricity-dependent medical equipment;
- refrigerated medication;
- mobility devices;
- accessible evacuation;
- communication access;
- paratransit;
- caregivers and personal assistants;
- service animals;
- backup batteries;
- accessible shelters;
- continuity of dialysis, oxygen, medication, and other care.

The system should do this without requiring people to surrender excessive privacy merely to receive protection.

---

# 6. Preparedness should be proportional, not obsessive

Preparedness can reduce avoidable harm.

It can also become expensive, anxiety-producing, performative, or detached from realistic hazards.

Human Scale should favor a practical hierarchy:

1. understand local hazards;
2. know emergency communication channels;
3. protect critical medication, documents, and contacts;
4. maintain reasonable household supplies;
5. make plans for children, pets, disability, and caregiving needs;
6. know evacuation and shelter options;
7. participate in community preparedness where useful;
8. invest in larger infrastructure resilience where individual preparation cannot solve the problem.

Do not turn ordinary life into permanent emergency posture.

---

# 7. Efficiency and resilience trade off — but not always

Highly optimized systems may reduce slack, inventory, spare capacity, or alternative routes.

That can create fragility.

But inefficiency is not automatically resilience either.

Old, poorly maintained, duplicated, or fragmented systems can fail more often.

Some technologies improve both efficiency and resilience through:

- predictive maintenance;
- distributed sensing;
- flexible routing;
- modular design;
- standardized interfaces;
- automated fault isolation;
- improved forecasting;
- better inventory visibility;
- interoperable communications.

Human Scale should measure actual failure and recovery performance rather than romanticize slack.

---

# 8. Energy resilience is service continuity, not off-grid identity

Local solar, batteries, generators, microgrids, efficiency, and islandable systems can improve resilience for specific loads and failure scenarios.

Strong regional grids, diverse generation, transmission, fuel supply, maintenance crews, and interconnection can also improve resilience.

A home that can run a refrigerator during an outage is more resilient in one dimension.

A hospital, water plant, data center, city, and region require much larger coordinated systems.

The goal is **critical-service continuity**, not proving independence from the grid.

---

# 9. Food resilience is not maximum localization

Local and regional production can provide redundancy and shorter supply chains for some foods.

Wider trade can protect a region from local drought, fire, flood, crop disease, or seasonal failure.

Human Scale should favor diversified supply rather than one geographic ideology.

Useful questions:

- How many independent suppliers exist?
- What happens if one region fails?
- Is storage adequate?
- Are processing and distribution bottlenecks concentrated?
- Are low-income households protected from shortage-driven price spikes?
- Which foods are critical and which are substitutable?

---

# 10. Communication should have multiple channels

A resilient information system may combine:

- mobile alerts;
- radio;
- websites;
- social media;
- local television;
- sirens;
- amateur or community radio where appropriate;
- physical signage;
- door-to-door outreach;
- trusted local organizations;
- accessible formats and languages.

No channel reaches everyone.

Digital tools can spread information quickly and also spread false information quickly.

Human Scale should prioritize authoritative, accessible, redundant communication and clear correction of errors.

---

# 11. Recovery is part of resilience

A community is not resilient merely because people survive the first 72 hours.

Recovery can involve:

- housing;
- insurance;
- employment;
- school continuity;
- healthcare;
- mental health;
- infrastructure repair;
- small businesses;
- documentation;
- benefits;
- rebuilding decisions;
- displacement;
- rebuilding standards;
- long administrative processes.

Administrative burden therefore becomes disaster policy.

A recovery system that requires exhausted people to navigate dozens of disconnected institutions can turn the disaster into a second disaster of paperwork and uncertainty.

---

# 12. Recommended doctrine-wide changes

1. **Define resilience by essential functions and recovery, not self-sufficiency.**
2. **Use nested scales.** Household, local, regional, state, national, and international capacities should back one another up.
3. **Invest in redundancy selectively.** Criticality and plausible failure should justify the cost.
4. **Keep mutual aid and formal response complementary.**
5. **Make disability and medical dependence central to planning.**
6. **Prepare proportionally to realistic local hazards.**
7. **Measure actual downtime, failure, recovery, and service continuity.**
8. **Treat strong grids and local energy resilience as complements.**
9. **Diversify food supply geographically and institutionally rather than maximize localization.**
10. **Use redundant, accessible communication channels.**
11. **Treat administrative recovery systems as critical infrastructure.**
12. **Publish failure receipts and after-action learning, not only preparedness plans.**

---

# 13. What would change our minds?

Human Scale should reduce investment in a backup or local system when maintenance cost, low reliability, complexity, or opportunity cost exceeds the resilience benefit.

It should strengthen centralized systems where scale, expertise, interconnection, and pooled resources clearly produce better reliability and recovery.

It should strengthen distributed systems where they measurably reduce outage duration, service loss, bottlenecks, or catastrophic single points of failure.

It should revise preparedness advice when hazard data show that a different risk deserves priority.

It should reject community-resilience programs that systematically leave socially isolated, disabled, poor, or marginalized people with worse protection.

And it should publish what failed after real disruptions so resilience becomes a learning system rather than a branding word.

---

# Result of Cross-Cutting Audit 06

**Human Scale resilience is not the ability to live without larger systems. It is the ability of people, households, communities, infrastructure, and larger institutions to keep essential functions working together when parts of the system fail.**

The resilient future is not fully centralized or fully decentralized.

It has enough overlapping capability that one failure does not become total dependence, and enough shared capacity that local people are not abandoned to handle catastrophe alone.
