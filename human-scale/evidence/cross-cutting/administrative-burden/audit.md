# Cross-Cutting Audit 04 — Administrative Burden

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> A form can take ten minutes to complete and still sit inside a process that consumes ten hours, three trips, five phone calls, two logins, a missed work shift, and weeks of uncertainty.

Administrative burden cuts across Government, Health, Work, Education, Disability, Family Care, Justice, Economics, Housing, Disaster Response, and Technology.

Human Scale has repeatedly argued that institutions should become legible and that technology should remove bureaucracy.

The audit keeps that direction while adding an important correction:

**not every procedural step is waste. Some friction protects accuracy, safety, rights, public money, privacy, due process, or appeal. The goal is not zero bureaucracy. It is justified procedure with the lowest human burden compatible with the function.**

---

# 1. Burden is larger than form-filling time

**Grade: Strong federal measurement framework / oversight finding**

The U.S. Government Accountability Office describes administrative burden in benefit programs through learning, compliance, and psychological costs. GAO's 2025 review found that federal documentation can miss burdens such as the time required to figure out eligibility and how to apply.

Source:
- GAO, Administrative Burden: OMB Should Update Instructions to Help Agency Assessment Efforts (2025): https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-107239

Federal customer-experience work similarly defines administrative burden across the full customer journey: learning about a service, assembling documents, completing requirements, scheduling or traveling to offices, waiting, and managing psychological stress.

Source:
- Performance.gov, Calculating a More Holistic Burden Estimate: https://www.performance.gov/cx/life-experiences/recovering-from-a-disaster/outputs/2/

Human Scale should therefore measure **the whole process**, not only the official transaction.

---

# 2. The Bureaucracy Receipt becomes a serious measurement tool

A Human Scale Bureaucracy Receipt should record at least:

- number of websites or portals;
- number of accounts and logins;
- time spent learning eligibility or requirements;
- pages of instructions;
- forms;
- fields completed;
- duplicate information requests;
- documents that must be found, scanned, copied, or certified;
- phone calls;
- transfers;
- hold time;
- in-person trips;
- transportation time and cost;
- appointment availability;
- active completion time;
- waiting days between steps;
- deadlines and expiration windows;
- accessibility barriers;
- translation or interpretation needs;
- uncertainty about status;
- number of times the person must re-explain the situation;
- error correction;
- appeal steps;
- psychological strain where the user reports it;
- lost wages or caregiving time;
- whether help from another person was required.

The receipt should also record **why each burdensome step exists** when that can be determined.

A painful step may still be justified.

The point is to make the trade visible.

---

# 3. Burden can block access to benefits and rights

**Grade: Strong administrative evidence / distributional concern**

GAO notes that eligible people can fail to claim benefits partly because of the time and resources required to obtain or retain them.

Source:
- GAO-25-107239: https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-25-107239

Administrative burden is therefore not merely bad customer service.

It can affect:

- food assistance;
- healthcare;
- disability support;
- unemployment benefits;
- housing assistance;
- veterans' services;
- disaster aid;
- immigration processes;
- licensing;
- education aid;
- taxes;
- legal rights;
- business formation;
- permits;
- professional credentials.

When burden is high, people with more time, money, literacy, language access, legal help, stable housing, internet access, transportation, executive function, or institutional familiarity can navigate the same nominal rule more easily.

That makes administrative design an equity issue.

---

# 4. Simplification can also create harm

Human Scale should not convert “bureaucracy bad” into an ideology.

Procedures can exist to:

- verify identity;
- prevent fraud;
- establish eligibility;
- protect privacy;
- document informed consent;
- create an evidentiary record;
- ensure public notice;
- protect environmental or safety requirements;
- allow affected people to object;
- support audit and accountability;
- preserve due process;
- prevent arbitrary decision-making;
- create appeal rights;
- coordinate scarce resources fairly.

Removing a step can:

- increase errors;
- shift burden to frontline workers;
- increase fraud or abuse;
- make decisions less transparent;
- eliminate records needed for appeal;
- create safety risk;
- favor people who know how to exploit ambiguity;
- make automated decisions harder to audit.

The Human Scale test is therefore:

**What function does this step perform, and can the same function be achieved with less human burden?**

---

# 5. Digitalization is not automatically simplification

Federal customer-experience programs explicitly pursue digital services, mobile access, self-service channels, case-status tracking, and human-centered design.

Sources:
- Performance.gov Federal Customer Experience: https://www.performance.gov/cx/
- Performance.gov A-11 Section 280 Explainer: https://www.performance.gov/cx/a-11/

Digitalization can remove travel, handwriting, mailing, duplicate data entry, and waiting.

It can also add:

- account creation;
- password recovery;
- identity verification failures;
- inaccessible interfaces;
- device or browser incompatibility;
- upload limits;
- camera/scanner requirements;
- confusing dashboards;
- automated chat dead ends;
- loss of human escalation;
- cybersecurity exposure;
- exclusion of people without reliable internet or digital skill.

A PDF form uploaded to a website is not necessarily digital transformation.

Human Scale should require **channel pluralism** where stakes are high:

- good self-service;
- phone support;
- accessible in-person help where necessary;
- authorized helpers;
- clear escalation.

---

# 6. Status uncertainty is a form of burden

A process can be easy to submit and still be punishing if the person cannot tell:

- whether the submission arrived;
- what stage it is in;
- what is missing;
- who owns the case;
- how long the next step normally takes;
- whether a deadline is approaching;
- whether silence means approval, denial, or nothing;
- how to correct an error.

Performance.gov explicitly treats case-status tracking and accurate processing-time information as important service expectations.

Source:
- Performance.gov Federal Customer Experience: https://www.performance.gov/cx/

Human Scale should therefore treat **state visibility** as institutional infrastructure.

---

# 7. Psychological cost is real but difficult to standardize

Processes involving disability, poverty, immigration, taxes, child welfare, illness, death, unemployment, disaster, legal conflict, or accusations can create fear, shame, confusion, and repeated re-exposure to stressful events.

Performance.gov's disaster-recovery burden work explicitly includes psychological costs alongside learning and compliance costs.

Source:
- Performance.gov Recovering from a Disaster: https://www.performance.gov/cx/life-experiences/recovering-from-a-disaster/

Human Scale should measure self-reported psychological burden where useful without pretending it can always be converted into one objective minute-equivalent.

Important design questions include:

- Must a person retell traumatic details repeatedly?
- Are notices written as threats when clarification would work?
- Does the process presume fraud or bad faith without evidence?
- Can someone ask a question without risking the case?
- Are errors distinguishable from misconduct?
- Is there a safe way to ask for accommodation or help?

---

# 8. Administrative burden exists outside government

Private institutions create their own bureaucracies:

- insurance prior authorization;
- bank disputes;
- subscription cancellation;
- warranties;
- workplace benefits;
- HR systems;
- university enrollment;
- medical billing;
- landlord applications;
- credentialing;
- platform appeals;
- utility service;
- customer support;
- tax preparation;
- travel claims.

Human Scale should therefore define bureaucracy functionally:

**institutional procedures people must navigate to exercise a right, receive a service, comply with a rule, correct an error, or exit a relationship.**

Government is one important case, not the only one.

---

# 9. AI can remove burden — and create unappealable bureaucracy at machine speed

AI can help:

- explain eligibility;
- prefill forms;
- translate;
- classify documents;
- summarize cases;
- detect missing information;
- route requests;
- schedule appointments;
- answer routine questions;
- draft notices;
- identify duplicate requirements;
- search regulations and case files.

AI can also:

- produce incorrect instructions;
- create opaque eligibility scores;
- deny requests without understandable reasons;
- hallucinate policy;
- make escalation harder;
- encourage staff to defer to machine output;
- increase surveillance;
- make bad rules operate more efficiently rather than remove them.

Human Scale should prioritize **automation of friction before automation of rights-critical judgment**, while allowing high-stakes automation where evidence and safeguards eventually justify it.

---

# 10. Administrative simplicity should be measured on both sides of the desk

A reform can reduce burden for the public while dramatically increasing workload for workers — or vice versa.

Human Scale should count:

- user time;
- employee time;
- training;
- error correction;
- exception handling;
- maintenance;
- technical support;
- audit burden;
- fraud investigation;
- accessibility support;
- downstream litigation or appeal;
- total processing time;
- staffing and cost.

The objective is **system burden**, with special attention to burdens imposed on people with less power or fewer alternatives.

---

# 11. Recommended doctrine-wide changes

1. **Define administrative burden across the full journey.** Learning, compliance, money, waiting, status uncertainty, travel, psychological strain, and correction all count.
2. **Make the Bureaucracy Receipt a standard Human Scale Lab instrument.**
3. **Require a reason for each burdensome step.** What function does it protect?
4. **Separate protective procedure from administrative waste.**
5. **Treat burden as an access and equity issue, not merely customer satisfaction.**
6. **Digitize functions, not paper.** A bad process online is still a bad process.
7. **Preserve channel pluralism and human escalation for consequential services.**
8. **Make case status visible.** People should know where they are in a process.
9. **Measure burden on workers and institutions as well as users.**
10. **Use AI first on repetitive friction, translation, explanation, routing, search, and prefill — with stronger safeguards as judgment becomes more consequential.**
11. **Test simplification for error, fraud, safety, rights, and downstream workload.**
12. **Publish before/after receipts so simplification can be evaluated rather than celebrated rhetorically.**

---

# 12. What would change our minds?

Human Scale should preserve a burdensome procedure when evidence shows that removing it substantially increases fraud, error, arbitrary treatment, rights violations, safety problems, or downstream costs and no lower-burden alternative provides equivalent protection.

It should reverse digital-only reforms that reduce agency cost while excluding substantial numbers of users or eliminating meaningful recourse.

It should expand automation where real-world evaluation shows reduced total burden, equal or better accuracy, accessible service, clear reasons, and reliable escalation.

It should reject “simplification” that merely moves labor from the institution onto citizens, families, clinicians, teachers, workers, or small businesses.

And it should publish processes that already work well, because Human Scale needs examples of successful institutional design, not only receipts of failure.

---

# Result of Cross-Cutting Audit 04

**A humane institution should ask people for no more time, attention, money, disclosure, travel, repetition, or uncertainty than the legitimate function of the process requires.**

Bureaucracy becomes Human Scale when procedure is justified, legible, accessible, measurable, and open to redesign — without deleting the protections that procedure exists to provide.
