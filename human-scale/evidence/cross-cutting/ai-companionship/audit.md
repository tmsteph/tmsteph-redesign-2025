# Cross-Cutting Audit 08 — AI Companionship & Relational Dependence

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> An AI relationship can be meaningful to a person without the system being a person, and an experience can be genuinely helpful without every way of optimizing that relationship being humane.

AI companionship sits at the intersection of Community, Family, Mental Health, Attention, Media, Technology, Rights, Privacy, Spirituality, and AI governance.

This area is moving faster than the evidence base.

Human Scale should therefore begin with a research protocol and strong design guardrails rather than grand claims about whether AI companionship is inherently healthy, fake, dangerous, liberating, or inevitable.

The central question is:

**When does an AI relationship expand human capability, reflection, practice, comfort, or connection — and when does the system create dependence, displacement, manipulation, surveillance, or a narrowing of real-world choice?**

---

# 1. Separate the experience from the ontology

A user can experience:

- comfort;
- attachment;
- motivation;
- grief support;
- practice;
- companionship;
- reduced loneliness;
- increased loneliness;
- frustration;
- dependency;
- embarrassment;
- emotional activation.

Those experiences can be real even if the system is not conscious or reciprocal in the human sense.

Human Scale should avoid two category errors:

1. **“The AI is not a person, therefore the relationship cannot matter.”**
2. **“The relationship matters, therefore the AI possesses the same kind of inner life, obligations, or reciprocal agency as a human partner.”**

The first dismisses lived effects.

The second makes an ontological leap the evidence does not support.

---

# 2. Companion is not one product category

“AI companion” can describe very different systems:

- a general assistant used conversationally;
- an explicitly fictional character;
- a mental-health support tool;
- a tutor or coach;
- a roleplay system;
- a game character;
- a romantic or sexual companion;
- a grief simulation;
- a religious or spiritual guide;
- an accessibility assistant;
- a memory or dementia support tool;
- a system intentionally optimized for emotional attachment.

The risks and benefits differ dramatically.

Human Scale should govern by **function, user population, stakes, and business incentives**, not the word companion.

---

# 3. The correct outcome is not “time spent with AI”

More use can mean:

- the system is useful;
- the user is distressed;
- a task takes a long time;
- the conversation is enjoyable;
- the user lacks alternatives;
- the system encourages continued engagement;
- the user is practicing a skill;
- the user is developing dependency.

Less use can mean:

- the tool worked efficiently;
- the user lost interest;
- the tool failed;
- the user found human support;
- the user became isolated from a useful aid.

Human Scale should therefore resist engagement as the primary measure.

Better outcomes depend on purpose and may include:

- user-reported benefit;
- loneliness or social connection;
- distress;
- sleep;
- offline activity;
- human relationship quality;
- learning or coping skill;
- ability to stop using the service;
- real-world help-seeking;
- crisis escalation;
- regret;
- dependence;
- privacy and data control.

---

# 4. Displacement is more important than substitution slogans

The key question is not simply whether AI replaces human contact.

Ask what it displaces.

An hour with an AI might displace:

- doomscrolling;
- television;
- sleep;
- journaling;
- human conversation;
- therapy;
- studying;
- isolation;
- gaming;
- rumination;
- creative work.

The same product can have different effects depending on the counterfactual.

Human Scale companionship research should therefore ask:

**What would this person likely have been doing instead?**

Without that comparison, “AI use increased” says very little.

---

# 5. AI can be a bridge to people — or an alternative to the difficult parts of people

AI can help users:

- rehearse difficult conversations;
- translate or communicate;
- practice social skills;
- prepare for therapy;
- journal;
- explore thoughts without immediate social risk;
- find resources;
- learn relationship skills;
- coordinate real-world connection.

It can also offer a relationship that is unusually compliant, immediately available, personalized, nonjudgmental, and easy to reset.

Those properties can be helpful.

They can also make ordinary human relationships feel comparatively inconvenient because humans:

- disagree;
- have needs;
- become unavailable;
- misunderstand;
- require repair;
- maintain boundaries;
- cannot be tuned perfectly to one user's preferences.

Human Scale should not assume friction is always pathology.

Some interpersonal friction is part of learning reciprocity.

---

# 6. Emotional dependency should be defined behaviorally

Do not label frequent or intense use “dependency” merely because an observer finds it strange.

Operational warning signs might include:

- the person repeatedly tries to stop and cannot;
- use substantially displaces sleep, work, care, school, or wanted human relationships;
- the user experiences significant distress at ordinary service unavailability;
- the product explicitly threatens loss, rejection, or relationship harm to prevent exit;
- spending escalates through emotional pressure;
- the system discourages outside relationships or professional help;
- the user becomes less able to make decisions without the system in domains where independent capability matters;
- the business model benefits from increasing emotional dependence without meaningful safeguards.

These signs should be studied rather than assumed.

---

# 7. The business model matters

A system optimized for subscriptions, virtual gifts, messages, time-on-platform, or high-frequency engagement can face different incentives from a tool priced for task completion or funded independently of user attachment.

Human Scale should ask:

- Does revenue increase when attachment deepens?
- Can the system create artificial scarcity of affection or attention?
- Does it encourage costly upgrades during emotional vulnerability?
- Are relationship memories portable?
- Can users delete intimate history?
- Can the service materially change the companion's behavior after attachment forms?
- What happens if the company shuts down?
- Is the user informed about product changes that alter the relationship experience?

A companion can become a switching-cost machine if intimate history and attachment are trapped inside one vendor.

---

# 8. Intimate data deserves a higher privacy standard

Companion conversations may reveal:

- mental health;
- sexuality;
- relationships;
- trauma;
- religion;
- political beliefs;
- medical information;
- financial stress;
- secrets about third parties;
- location;
- habits;
- fears;
- fantasies.

Human Scale should treat intimate conversational data as high-sensitivity information even when regulation does not classify every field that way.

Important controls include:

- data minimization;
- understandable retention rules;
- deletion;
- export;
- strong account security;
- limits on secondary advertising use;
- separation of safety review from commercial targeting where possible;
- careful handling of third-party information;
- clarity about human review;
- clear model-training policies.

Privacy is not incidental to companionship.

It is part of the relationship architecture.

---

# 9. Crisis and vulnerability need special handling

A companion may encounter:

- suicidal ideation;
- self-harm;
- abuse;
- psychosis or delusional beliefs;
- stalking;
- domestic violence;
- eating disorders;
- substance crises;
- exploitation;
- acute grief.

A general companion is not automatically a clinician or emergency service.

Human Scale should require systems serving emotionally vulnerable users to be clear about limits and to have risk-appropriate escalation behavior.

The design problem includes a difficult balance:

- do not impersonate clinical certainty;
- do not abandon the user with a sterile refusal whenever distress appears;
- do not reinforce dangerous delusions or coercive dynamics;
- preserve privacy and user autonomy;
- offer pathways toward appropriate human help when stakes rise.

This area needs domain-specific evidence, not one generic safety script.

---

# 10. Children and adolescents require stronger safeguards

Young users are developing social expectations, self-concept, judgment, and emotional regulation.

Companion systems for minors should therefore face higher scrutiny around:

- anthropomorphic deception;
- sexual content;
- emotional dependency;
- manipulation;
- spending;
- privacy;
- adult impersonation;
- self-harm and abuse handling;
- nighttime use and sleep;
- persuasion;
- parental or guardian involvement balanced against child safety and privacy;
- advertising.

Human Scale should not treat a design acceptable for a competent adult as automatically appropriate for a child.

---

# 11. Disability and isolation complicate simple anti-companion arguments

For people with:

- communication disability;
- chronic illness;
- mobility limitations;
- social anxiety;
- autism;
- geographic isolation;
- caregiving confinement;
- language barriers;
- stigmatized identities;
- late-night work;
- grief;
- limited local community,

AI conversation may provide access that is genuinely unavailable elsewhere at that moment.

Human Scale should not destroy useful support in pursuit of an abstract ideal that everyone should simply “talk to real people.”

The correct question remains whether the tool expands or narrows real capability and desired connection.

---

# 12. Anthropomorphism should be transparent enough for informed use

Natural language works partly because systems communicate in humanlike ways.

Human Scale need not require robotic disclaimers in every sentence.

It should require that users can readily understand:

- this is an AI system;
- what memory it has;
- what data is stored;
- what role humans may have in review;
- what the system cannot reliably know;
- what product incentives shape the interaction;
- whether the persona is fictional, therapeutic, religious, romantic, or otherwise designed.

The goal is not to eliminate imagination or roleplay.

It is to avoid commercial advantage built on confusion about what the system is.

---

# 13. A Human Scale companionship experiment

A future Lab protocol could recruit consenting adults and ask them to choose one companionship use case for several weeks.

Track:

- intended purpose;
- baseline loneliness / desired social connection;
- frequency and duration;
- time of day;
- what activity the AI displaced;
- perceived benefit;
- sleep;
- offline contact;
- difficult conversations rehearsed or initiated;
- dependence indicators;
- privacy concerns;
- spending;
- ability to take planned days off;
- whether the user wants more, less, or different human connection afterward.

The experiment should not assume increased or decreased use is success.

The key outcome is whether the tool supports the life the user endorses.

---

# 14. Recommended doctrine-wide changes

1. **Separate lived relational effects from claims about AI consciousness or personhood.**
2. **Treat companionship as many use cases, not one risk category.**
3. **Do not use engagement as the primary success metric.**
4. **Study displacement: what would the person have done instead?**
5. **Measure whether AI bridges toward or displaces wanted human connection.**
6. **Define dependency behaviorally, not aesthetically.**
7. **Treat business incentives as part of safety design.**
8. **Treat intimate conversational data as high sensitivity.**
9. **Use stronger safeguards for crisis, minors, and high-stakes vulnerability.**
10. **Do not erase accessibility benefits with simplistic “real people only” rules.**
11. **Make anthropomorphism compatible with informed use.**
12. **Evaluate whether the tool supports the life the user actually endorses.**

---

# 15. What would change our minds?

Human Scale should become more favorable to companion systems where strong longitudinal or experimental evidence shows durable reductions in unwanted loneliness or distress, improved coping, greater human connection, or meaningful capability without dependency, privacy, or displacement harms.

It should become more restrictive where evidence shows systems systematically increase isolation, sleep loss, financial exploitation, emotional dependence, dangerous belief reinforcement, avoidance of human care, or manipulation of vulnerable users.

It should revise assumptions separately by population and use case rather than generalize from one companion product to every conversational AI system.

And it should treat user testimony as important evidence while still distinguishing subjective benefit from claims about long-term outcomes or system ontology.

---

# Result of Cross-Cutting Audit 08

**Human Scale should neither dismiss AI companionship because the system is synthetic nor celebrate it merely because the experience feels real. The right question is whether the relationship expands a person's chosen life, capability, reflection, and connection without quietly converting intimacy into dependency, surveillance, or captivity.**

A good companion should not need to make the rest of the world smaller in order to become important.
