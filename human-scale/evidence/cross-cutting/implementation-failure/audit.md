# Cross-Cutting Audit 10 — Implementation Failure

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> A policy, tool, garden, cooperative, school reform, AI system, public space, social program, or community project does not succeed because its theory is attractive. It succeeds only if people can operate, maintain, fund, govern, repair, adapt, and eventually hand it to someone else.

Human Scale strongly prefers experiments before ideology.

That is useful but incomplete.

Pilots often benefit from unusually motivated founders, temporary funding, special attention, flexible rules, volunteer energy, or participants who selected themselves into the experiment.

The implementation question is harder:

**What survives when the founder leaves, novelty fades, the budget becomes ordinary, difficult cases arrive, maintenance accumulates, incentives shift, and the project must coexist with everything else?**

This audit turns implementation itself into an evidence category.

---

# 1. Efficacy, effectiveness, adoption, and maintenance are different

A project can work under ideal conditions and fail in ordinary use.

It can produce good outcomes for participants who use it and still fail because almost nobody adopts it.

It can be adopted quickly and disappear a year later.

It can persist while losing the mechanism that originally made it useful.

Human Scale should distinguish:

1. **Theory:** Why should this work?
2. **Efficacy:** Can it work under favorable conditions?
3. **Effectiveness:** Does it work in ordinary real-world conditions?
4. **Adoption:** Do relevant people or institutions actually use it?
5. **Implementation fidelity:** Are the important parts delivered as intended?
6. **Adaptation:** What changes are necessary for context?
7. **Maintenance:** Does it continue after initial attention or funding?
8. **Scale:** Do outcomes survive when reach increases?
9. **Equity:** Who participates, who benefits, and who gets excluded?
10. **Replacement:** What should happen when a better intervention appears?

The CDC's program-evaluation framework emphasizes useful, feasible, ethical, accurate evaluation and iterative program improvement rather than evaluation as a ceremonial final report.

Entry:
- CDC Program Evaluation: https://www.cdc.gov/policy/php/evaluation/index.html

# 2. Founder energy is not a business model

Many small projects function because one unusually committed person:

- remembers everything;
- answers messages instantly;
- fixes problems personally;
- works unpaid hours;
- knows every participant;
- absorbs conflict;
- carries institutional memory;
- subsidizes costs;
- bends rules;
- recruits friends;
- performs invisible maintenance.

This can make a pilot look more sustainable than it is.

Human Scale should document **founder load** explicitly:

- hours per week;
- unpaid labor;
- tasks only the founder can perform;
- relationships dependent on one person;
- undocumented decisions;
- emergency coverage;
- burnout risk;
- succession plan.

A project is not truly decentralized merely because it is small.

It can be radically centralized in one human being.

---

# 3. Maintenance is part of the intervention

A new garden is not only beds and plants.

It is:

- irrigation;
- soil testing;
- tools;
- weeds;
- vandalism;
- accessibility;
- harvesting;
- conflict over plots;
- insurance;
- leadership;
- seasons;
- cleanup;
- funding;
- replacement volunteers.

A new software tool is:

- dependencies;
- hosting;
- backups;
- security patches;
- user support;
- accessibility regressions;
- browser changes;
- domain renewals;
- documentation;
- analytics;
- privacy incidents;
- ownership when maintainers leave.

A public-space improvement is:

- cleaning;
- landscaping;
- repairs;
- lighting;
- toilets;
- trash;
- behavior rules;
- staffing;
- accessibility;
- emergency response.

Human Scale should require a **maintenance model** before treating construction or launch as success.

---

# 4. Good pilots can fail at scale

Scaling changes the environment.

At larger reach:

- participants become more heterogeneous;
- average motivation can fall;
- edge cases increase;
- staff become less specialized;
- oversight grows;
- fraud opportunities change;
- supply chains matter more;
- informal trust becomes harder;
- communication becomes formalized;
- infrastructure bottlenecks appear;
- local adaptations conflict with standardization;
- political opposition can increase;
- costs do not always scale linearly.

Human Scale should not infer:

**it worked for 30 willing participants → therefore it should become universal policy.**

The next experiment after a successful pilot may be a larger, messier pilot — not legislation for everyone.

---

# 5. Selection effects can make a project look better than it is

People who volunteer for:

- a four-day workweek trial;
- a community garden;
- a neighborhood meeting;
- a cooperative;
- a quiet-technology experiment;
- a new school;
- an AI tool;
- a local resilience program

may differ from people who do not volunteer.

They can have more motivation, time, trust, resources, political interest, technical skill, or compatibility with the intervention.

Human Scale experiments should record:

- who was invited;
- who joined;
- who declined;
- who dropped out;
- who could not participate;
- why;
- whether the intervention becomes more burdensome when participation is mandatory or default.

Nonparticipation is data.

---

# 6. Implementation can move burden rather than remove it

A reform may look simpler from one perspective while shifting work elsewhere.

Examples:

- self-service software reduces staff time but increases user burden;
- home care reduces institutional cost but increases family labor;
- remote work removes commuting but shifts space/energy/equipment costs home;
- community maintenance reduces public spending but relies on volunteer labor;
- automation reduces clerical work but creates exception-handling and monitoring burden;
- a new reporting requirement helps oversight but consumes teacher/clinician time.

Human Scale should ask:

**Whose burden fell, whose burden rose, and was the shift intentional?**

This is the implementation companion to the Administrative Burden audit.

---

# 7. Incentives can overpower the written design

Institutions respond to what is rewarded, punished, measured, funded, audited, and politically visible.

A program that says “prioritize quality” while paying only for volume may create volume.

A workplace that says “use AI to save time” while rewarding maximum output may convert every saved minute into more work.

A school that says “value curiosity” while stakes depend on narrow test metrics may teach to the test.

A social platform that says “support well-being” while revenue depends on engagement may face persistent tension.

Human Scale should therefore audit **incentive compatibility**:

- What does the institution say it wants?
- What actually determines money, promotion, survival, status, or punishment?
- Do those signals point in the same direction?

Policy text is not implementation reality.

---

# 8. Metrics can become targets and stop measuring the goal

Once people are rewarded for a measure, behavior can shift toward the measure rather than the underlying purpose.

Human Scale should therefore pair quantitative metrics with:

- qualitative feedback;
- outcome review;
- adverse-event monitoring;
- random case audits;
- user complaints;
- measures of gaming or displacement;
- periodic reconsideration of whether the metric still represents the goal.

The Audit Calibration protocol applies here too.

A “Human Scale score” used for funding or rankings would create different incentives than the same questions used privately for reflection.

Measurement context matters.

---

# 9. Standardization and adaptation must coexist

Too much standardization can ignore local context.

Too much adaptation can destroy the mechanism or make evaluation impossible.

Human Scale projects should distinguish:

## Core function

What must remain for the intervention to still be the same intervention?

## Adaptable form

What can vary by:

- culture;
- climate;
- disability;
- neighborhood;
- institution;
- law;
- population;
- available staff;
- budget;
- technology;
- language?

This is another form of scale discipline.

A good model travels by preserving function while allowing form to change.

---

# 10. Failure can be political even when the mechanism works

A technically effective intervention may fail because:

- costs and benefits fall on different groups;
- a constituency loses status or income;
- the process excluded affected people;
- trust is low;
- implementation appears illegitimate;
- benefits arrive later than costs;
- opponents can frame the program more effectively;
- rules conflict with existing law;
- a leadership change reverses it.

Human Scale should not call all resistance irrational.

Political durability is part of real-world implementation.

A proposal that requires permanent alignment of unusually favorable political conditions may be fragile even if technically excellent.

---

# 11. Equity can worsen during implementation

A universal-looking program can produce unequal access if participation requires:

- flexible time;
- internet;
- transportation;
- literacy;
- English proficiency;
- documentation;
- money upfront;
- physical ability;
- childcare;
- social networks;
- comfort with institutions.

Human Scale should evaluate not only average outcomes but:

- who reaches the program;
- who completes it;
- who benefits;
- who bears burden;
- who gets excluded;
- what adaptations improve access without destroying effectiveness.

This is why Accessibility and Administrative Burden are cross-cutting rather than niche concerns.

---

# 12. A project needs an exit and shutdown plan

Not every program should survive.

Human Scale projects should define:

- what counts as failure;
- when to stop;
- how users are notified;
- how data is returned or deleted;
- how assets are distributed;
- how obligations are fulfilled;
- how people dependent on the service transition;
- what learning is published.

Stopping a failed project can be responsible stewardship.

A program that exists only because no one knows how to end it is not resilient.

---

# 13. Implementation receipts

Every significant Human Scale pilot should eventually publish a receipt containing:

## Before

- problem;
- baseline;
- affected people;
- theory of change;
- expected benefits;
- expected harms/tradeoffs;
- budget;
- staff/volunteer time;
- maintenance assumptions;
- falsification conditions.

## During

- participation;
- dropout;
- exceptions;
- adaptations;
- incidents;
- staff burden;
- user burden;
- cost drift;
- maintenance problems;
- unexpected effects.

## After

- outcomes;
- distribution of benefits/costs;
- failures;
- whether the mechanism worked;
- whether the project should continue, change, scale, shrink, or stop;
- what another community should know before copying it.

**The receipt is more important than the success story.**

---

# 14. Recommended doctrine-wide changes

1. **Treat implementation as evidence, not an afterthought.**
2. **Separate efficacy, real-world effectiveness, adoption, maintenance, equity, and scale.**
3. **Measure founder load and succession risk.**
4. **Require maintenance models for physical, social, and digital projects.**
5. **Do not jump from a small willing pilot to universal policy.**
6. **Record nonparticipants and dropouts.**
7. **Track burden shifts across users, workers, families, institutions, and volunteers.**
8. **Audit incentives, not only stated goals.**
9. **Expect metrics to be gamed when stakes attach to them.**
10. **Specify core functions versus adaptable forms.**
11. **Treat political legitimacy and durability as implementation variables.**
12. **Measure equity through reach and burden as well as average outcome.**
13. **Define stop conditions and shutdown plans.**
14. **Publish implementation receipts, including failures.**

---

# 15. What would change our minds?

Human Scale should abandon or radically revise a favored intervention when real-world implementation repeatedly fails despite competent execution and reasonable adaptation.

It should not rescue every failure by claiming “the idea was good but people implemented it wrong.”

That explanation may be true sometimes.

But if an idea requires extraordinary people, permanent subsidies, perfect compliance, unusually favorable politics, or constant founder heroics to work, those requirements are part of the idea's real cost.

Human Scale should favor boring, durable systems over elegant fragile ones when the outcomes are better.

And it should treat negative implementation results as some of the most valuable evidence the project can produce.

---

# Result of Cross-Cutting Audit 10

**Human Scale should judge an idea not only by whether it is morally attractive or theoretically sound, but by whether ordinary people and institutions can operate, maintain, adapt, fund, govern, repair, and eventually replace it without heroic effort.**

A philosophy becomes real when it survives maintenance.
