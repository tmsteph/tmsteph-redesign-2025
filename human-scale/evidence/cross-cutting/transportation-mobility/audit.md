# Cross-Cutting Audit 03 — Transportation & Mobility

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> Transportation is not a contest between cars and walking. It is the system by which people reach care, work, food, school, relationships, nature, culture, and one another.

Transportation appears across Architecture & Place, Work & Time, Energy, Disability, Aging, Community, Health, Education, Economics, and Ecology.

Human Scale has often used walkability and proximity as shorthand for humane places.

The audit keeps the concern with time, safety, movement, and access while replacing the shorthand with a broader goal:

**realistic mobility with less compulsory time loss, less preventable injury, more choice, and fewer single-mode dependencies.**

---

# 1. Mobility is access, not movement for its own sake

Transportation has value because it connects people to destinations and opportunities.

WHO guidance explicitly recognizes that mobility systems affect access to education, decent work, healthcare, leisure, and other essentials.

Source:
- WHO, Guidance on safe environments and mobility: https://www.who.int/tools/compendium-on-health-and-environment/safe-environments-and-mobility

A Human Scale mobility metric should therefore ask:

- What essential destinations can a person realistically reach?
- How long does it take?
- How reliable is the trip?
- What does it cost?
- How dangerous is it?
- What physical or cognitive demand does it create?
- Is there a fallback when the preferred mode fails?
- Can children, older adults, disabled people, and people without cars participate?

**Access is the outcome. Transportation modes are mechanisms.**

---

# 2. Safe systems should assume human error

**Grade: Strong global road-safety framework**

WHO's Safe System approach begins from human fallibility and vulnerability. Road design, speeds, vehicles, users, and post-crash systems should work together so ordinary mistakes do not routinely become fatal or permanently disabling events.

Source:
- WHO, Road traffic injuries: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/road-traffic-injuries

This is profoundly Human Scale.

A system designed around actual humans should not require every driver, cyclist, pedestrian, child, or older adult to behave perfectly every second merely to remain alive.

The correction is:

**safety should be engineered into the system, not outsourced entirely to individual vigilance.**

---

# 3. Active mobility is valuable, but only when safe and feasible

**Grade: Strong health mechanism, conditional implementation**

WHO promotes walking, cycling, wheeling, and other active mobility because physical activity provides health benefits and because active modes can reduce noise, pollution, congestion, and emissions in appropriate contexts.

Sources:
- WHO, Promoting walking, cycling and other forms of active mobility: https://www.who.int/health-topics/physical-activity/promoting-walking-and-cycling
- WHO, Safer walking and cycling: https://www.who.int/news/item/09-05-2025-safer-walking-and-cycling-crucial-for-road-safety-and-better-health

But active transport is not universally feasible.

Barriers include:

- disability;
- age;
- distance;
- topography;
- heat, cold, rain, smoke, or extreme weather;
- unsafe traffic;
- caregiving;
- freight or equipment;
- time constraints;
- night work;
- personal security;
- inadequate infrastructure.

Human Scale should make active mobility **available and safe**, not compulsory.

---

# 4. Cars are tools with large benefits and large system costs

Cars can provide:

- door-to-door access;
- schedule flexibility;
- disability access for some users;
- emergency mobility;
- family and caregiving transport;
- freight capacity;
- access in rural and dispersed places;
- protection from weather;
- private space.

Car-dependent systems can also create:

- high household cost;
- traffic injury risk;
- congestion;
- pollution and noise;
- parking land consumption;
- isolation for non-drivers;
- large travel distances;
- barriers created by wide, fast roads;
- vulnerability when fuel, maintenance, insurance, licensing, or driving ability is lost.

Human Scale should not be anti-car.

It should be anti-**compulsory car dependence** where realistic alternatives can exist.

A society is more resilient when losing the ability to drive does not mean losing access to ordinary life.

---

# 5. Public transport is infrastructure, not a universal solution

Transit can move many people efficiently along strong demand corridors and can provide essential mobility to people who do not drive.

It can also fail through:

- low frequency;
- poor coverage;
- long transfers;
- weak accessibility;
- unreliable operations;
- personal safety concerns;
- insufficient late-night service;
- high operating cost in low-density areas;
- poor first/last-mile connections.

Human Scale should evaluate transit by actual door-to-door usefulness, not route-map existence.

Useful metrics include:

- frequency;
- reliability;
- travel time;
- transfer burden;
- accessibility;
- operating span;
- proximity to destinations;
- affordability;
- safety;
- comfort;
- ridership and capacity;
- alternatives during failure.

---

# 6. Transportation is time policy

A commute is part of the work footprint.

A 20-minute medical visit that requires two hours of transportation and waiting is not a 20-minute care experience.

A school that requires parents to spend hours driving children has transportation embedded inside family policy.

A grocery store, park, job, community center, or childcare provider may be geographically near and functionally far.

Human Scale should count **door-to-door elapsed human time**, not only miles or vehicle speed.

This is especially important for:

- transit transfers;
- unreliable service;
- parking searches;
- border crossings;
- school drop-off systems;
- disability paratransit windows;
- airport processes;
- rural healthcare trips;
- long commutes;
- congestion.

---

# 7. Mobility justice is not mode equality

Different people need different transportation options.

A wheelchair user, blind pedestrian, child, night-shift worker, delivery driver, parent with three children, older adult who no longer drives, rural resident, cyclist, and healthy downtown commuter do not have identical mobility needs.

Human Scale should therefore ask whether systems produce **usable access**, rather than whether every mode receives identical infrastructure or subsidy.

Equity can require:

- paratransit;
- accessible parking;
- curb ramps;
- audible signals;
- protected crossings;
- elevators;
- transit subsidies;
- school transport;
- rural demand-responsive service;
- safe cycling infrastructure;
- car access;
- delivery;
- remote service alternatives.

---

# 8. Remote access is sometimes transportation policy

Telehealth, remote work, online government, delivery, distance learning, and digital participation can eliminate some trips entirely.

This can return time and increase access for disabled, rural, caregiving, older, or time-constrained users.

It can also:

- exclude people without broadband or digital skill;
- reduce in-person service quality;
- create isolation;
- shift costs to households;
- fail when physical examination or presence is required.

Human Scale should count **the avoided trip** as a legitimate mobility improvement when the remote substitute genuinely works.

---

# 9. Transportation and land use cannot be separated completely

Long distances are often created upstream by where housing, jobs, schools, shops, healthcare, and services are allowed or economically able to locate.

Transportation systems then attempt to bridge those distances.

Human Scale should therefore evaluate transportation and land use together without pretending one urban form fits everywhere.

Questions include:

- Can more housing exist near jobs and services?
- Can daily destinations be closer without creating major nuisance or cost?
- Can specialized regional destinations be reached efficiently?
- Are lower-cost households pushed farther away from opportunity?
- Does new transportation capacity induce more distance or unlock needed access?
- Are freight and industrial functions given realistic routes?

Proximity can reduce transportation burden.

It is not the only way to solve it.

---

# 10. Energy and transport resilience

Transportation systems depend on energy, maintenance, roads, networks, spare parts, operators, and communications.

Electrification can reduce tailpipe pollution and change energy dependencies. It does not eliminate congestion, crash risk, tire/road impacts, parking, or land-use effects.

Internal-combustion vehicles offer mature refueling infrastructure and long range while creating fuel dependence and emissions.

Transit and cycling also require maintained infrastructure and operating capacity.

Human Scale should favor **redundant mobility portfolios** where practical rather than one brittle universal mode.

---

# 11. Recommended doctrine-wide changes

1. **Replace walkability as the master metric with usable access.**
2. **Keep walking, wheeling, and cycling safe and available without making them compulsory.**
3. **Adopt the Safe System principle: design for human error and physical vulnerability.**
4. **Reject both car ideology and anti-car ideology.** Cars solve real problems; compulsory car dependence creates real problems.
5. **Judge transit door-to-door.** Frequency, reliability, transfers, accessibility, operating span, and destination access matter.
6. **Count human time.** Travel, waiting, transfers, parking, and schedule uncertainty belong in the cost.
7. **Make disability and aging central to mobility design.**
8. **Treat remote access as a legitimate way to remove some travel demand.**
9. **Evaluate transportation with land use, housing, and destination geography.**
10. **Build resilience through multiple modes and fallbacks where feasible.**
11. **Measure safety by severe injury and death, not only traffic flow.**
12. **Treat freight, emergency service, caregiving, and rural access as first-class mobility needs too.**

---

# 12. What would change our minds?

Human Scale should reduce emphasis on active mobility where safety, climate, disability, distance, or user preference make other modes consistently better.

It should strengthen specific walking, cycling, transit, pricing, or land-use interventions where strong real-world evidence shows improvements in access, safety, health, time, affordability, or environmental outcomes without disproportionate burdens.

It should retain or expand car access where cars are the most effective way to preserve real mobility, especially when alternatives are infeasible.

It should challenge car dependence where people who cannot or do not drive lose access to basic participation.

It should reject transportation projects that move vehicles faster while making total human access, safety, or time worse.

And it should prefer actual trip data and user experience over ideology about which mode a good person ought to use.

---

# Result of Cross-Cutting Audit 03

**Human Scale transportation is not about choosing the morally correct vehicle. It is about giving more people safe, reliable, affordable, accessible ways to reach the people, places, services, and opportunities that make a life possible — without consuming unnecessary portions of that life in the process.**

The purpose of mobility is access.
