# Cross-Cutting Audit 01 — Disability & Accessibility

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> If a philosophy of “human scale” silently assumes a healthy, able-bodied, neurotypical adult who can walk, see, hear, speak, read dense text, tolerate crowds, manage executive function, drive when necessary, and live without assistive support, then it is not yet a philosophy scaled to humans. It is scaled to one subset of humans.

The first cross-cutting audit asks every chapter the same question:

**Does this idea expand real participation for people with disabilities — or does it merely make life pleasant for the default body imagined by the designer?**

The central correction is not “add an accessibility section.”

It is:

**accessibility changes the meaning of agency, technology, place, community, care, work, education, and localism across the entire doctrine.**

---

# 1. Disability is partly an interaction with environment

**Grade: Strong WHO framework**

WHO describes disability as part of the human experience arising from interaction between health conditions and environmental and personal factors. WHO estimates that about 1.3 billion people — roughly 16% of the global population — experience significant disability.

That framing is highly compatible with Human Scale because it refuses two errors:

- disability is only inside the individual;
- every limitation is socially constructed and can be designed away.

Bodies and health conditions are real.

So are stairs, inaccessible transit, unreadable information, stigma, inflexible work, missing assistive technology, unaffordable care, and systems built around one assumed user.

Sources:
- WHO, Disability: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/disability-and-health
- WHO, Global report on health equity for persons with disabilities: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240063600

---

# 2. Accessibility is infrastructure, not a special service

**Grade: Strong rights framework**

Article 9 of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities treats accessibility as access on an equal basis to the physical environment, transportation, information and communications, technology, and facilities and services open to the public.

This is much broader than ramps.

Human Scale accessibility includes:

- buildings;
- roads and crossings;
- housing;
- schools;
- workplaces;
- health facilities;
- public transportation;
- websites and apps;
- documents;
- emergency services;
- communication;
- signage;
- live assistance and interpretation;
- interfaces that can be perceived, understood, and operated by different users.

Source:
- UN CRPD Article 9 — Accessibility: https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with%20-disabilities/article-9-accessibility.html

The Human Scale correction is:

**accessibility belongs inside the default definition of good infrastructure.**

---

# 3. Independence does not mean doing everything alone

**Grade: Strong rights correction**

CRPD Article 19 protects living independently and being included in the community while explicitly recognizing personal assistance, in-home and residential supports, and accessible general services.

This matters because Human Scale often values competence, independence, local participation, and self-reliance.

Those values become exclusionary if “independence” is interpreted as needing no support.

A person can have more autonomy because they use:

- a wheelchair;
- a screen reader;
- medication;
- a personal care attendant;
- supported communication;
- paratransit;
- accessible housing;
- reminders and cognitive aids;
- family or community support;
- professional services;
- remote work;
- an AI assistant.

**Support can increase independence.**

Source:
- UN CRPD Article 19 — Living independently and being included in the community: https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-19-living-independently-and-being-included-in-the-community.html

---

# 4. Assistive technology is Human Scale technology

**Grade: Strong functional evidence / health-system framework**

WHO defines assistive technology broadly across mobility, vision, hearing, cognition, communication, and self-care. Products can include wheelchairs, hearing aids, prostheses, glasses, white canes, captioning, speech recognition, communication systems, and time-management software.

This creates an important correction to any “less technology” instinct inside Human Scale.

For many people, advanced technology is not what separates them from ordinary human life.

It is what gives ordinary life back.

Source:
- WHO, Assistive technology: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/assistive-technology

Human Scale should therefore judge technology by agency, capability, maintenance, affordability, compatibility, privacy, and reliability — not by how visually simple or “natural” the lifestyle appears.

---

# 5. Rehabilitation shows why capability is relational

**Grade: Strong health framework**

WHO defines rehabilitation as optimizing functioning and reducing disability in interaction with the environment. It can involve treatment, task adaptation, environmental changes, education, assistive products, and support for participation in work, family, education, recreation, and everyday life.

Source:
- WHO, Rehabilitation: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/rehabilitation

This is almost a clinical version of the Human Scale method:

**change the person where useful; change the environment where useful; use tools where useful; measure whether participation actually improves.**

There is no ideological prize for solving every problem at one layer.

---

# 6. “Nothing about us without us” becomes a design rule

Accessibility cannot be inferred reliably from outside observation alone.

A designer can meet a checklist and still create something difficult to use.

Disability is heterogeneous. Blindness, deafness, chronic pain, mobility impairment, intellectual disability, autism, ADHD, dementia, speech disability, fatigue, psychiatric disability, and combinations of conditions create different and sometimes conflicting needs.

The CRPD framework emphasizes participation and inclusion by persons with disabilities in decisions affecting them.

Human Scale should operationalize that as:

- involve users with disabilities before the design is fixed;
- pay people for specialized lived expertise when the work is substantial;
- test with more than one disability type;
- include disabled people in governance, not only user research;
- publish unresolved accessibility tradeoffs;
- keep an accessible feedback route open after deployment.

Source:
- UN CRPD implementation materials on accessibility and participation: https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/resources/handbook-for-parliamentarians-on-the-convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/chapter-six-from-provisions-to-practice-implementing-the-convention-2.html

# 7. Chapter-by-chapter pressure test

## Human Nature

Do not define “natural human functioning” by majority performance.

A recurring species-level tendency can be real without describing every individual. Human variation is part of human nature too.

The mismatch framework should ask whether environments create preventable barriers, not whether disabled bodies fail to match an imagined ancestral norm.

## Land & Life

Nature access should include:

- accessible routes and surfaces where feasible;
- seating and rest;
- shade;
- toilets;
- sensory considerations;
- accessible gardening methods;
- virtual or indoor alternatives when physical access is impossible;
- information about terrain and barriers before arrival.

A wilderness trail can remain wild without every meter being accessible. Human Scale should create a **portfolio of access**, not demand that every place serve every body identically.

## Community

Physical proximity is not the only valid community mechanism.

Online communities, text communication, gaming, video, asynchronous groups, and remote participation can be essential for people limited by mobility, fatigue, infection risk, geography, sensory overload, communication differences, or caregiving.

Digital belonging is not a consolation prize.

## Work & Time

A “good work” test must include:

- accessible recruitment;
- task accommodation;
- schedule control;
- remote/hybrid options where compatible with the work;
- assistive technology;
- accessible workplace design;
- predictable transportation;
- recovery time;
- protection from discrimination;
- whether productivity metrics punish disability-related variation.

Flexibility can be an accessibility tool rather than only a lifestyle perk.

## Technology

Accessibility is one of the strongest arguments **for** advanced technology.

Repairability and interoperability remain useful, but an assistive product that is theoretically open and practically unreliable may be worse than a proprietary device with dependable support.

The user needs functioning, not ideological purity.

## Energy

Energy resilience has disability-specific consequences.

Power supports:

- ventilators and respiratory equipment;
- powered mobility;
- refrigeration for medication;
- lifts and elevators;
- charging communication devices;
- heating and cooling;
- accessible transportation;
- home medical equipment.

Emergency planning should therefore identify people whose health or mobility depends on reliable power without creating invasive or stigmatizing surveillance systems.

## Economics

Disability can create additional costs while simultaneously limiting labor-market options.

Economic policy should evaluate real disposable resources and access, not assume equal income means equal capability.

Material security programs should avoid cliffs and rules that punish people for attempting work, saving, partnering, or increasing independence.

## Governance

Participation must be accessible before it can be called local democracy.

Meetings, documents, public comment, voting, hearings, appeals, emergency notices, and websites should account for communication, mobility, sensory, cognitive, and language access.

A city hall three blocks away is not “close to the people” if the process cannot be used.

## Food

Food systems must account for:

- swallowing and feeding needs;
- allergies and medically necessary diets;
- sensory differences;
- dexterity and cooking limitations;
- accessible packaging;
- kitchen design;
- delivery;
- caregiver burden;
- nutrition support.

“Cook more from scratch” is not a humane universal instruction.

## Architecture & Place

Accessibility is foundational, not a special add-on.

Human Scale place design should include route continuity, curb cuts, gradients, crossings, rest, shade, toilets, lighting, wayfinding, auditory/visual information, sensory load, transit, parking where needed, and safe evacuation.

Walkability is incomplete without rollability, restability, transit accessibility, and alternatives for people who cannot make the trip under their own power.

## Education & Childhood

A capability-focused education system should maintain ambitious learning while providing different routes to participation.

Accommodation should not automatically mean lower expectation.

Nor should one form of independence be treated as the goal for every learner.

Communication, assistive technology, support staff, sensory environment, accessible materials, alternative assessment where justified, and meaningful inclusion all matter.

## Health & Care

The WHO Global Report on Health Equity for Persons with Disabilities documents health inequities driven not only by underlying conditions but also by inaccessible systems, stigma, discrimination, poverty, and exclusion.

Source:
- WHO, Global report on health equity for persons with disabilities: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240063600

Human Scale health care must therefore treat disability inclusion as core quality and safety work, not a niche specialty.

## Family & Care

Support should increase autonomy of both the person receiving care and the caregiver.

Do not assume family knows best in every case.

Do not assume formal services know best in every case either.

Supported decision-making, communication access, respite, outside review, and safeguards against neglect or abuse all matter.

## Attention & Media

“Calm” interfaces can be disabling if they hide state, remove reminders, rely on subtle visual cues, eliminate captions, or make actions ambiguous.

Other users can be harmed by excessive motion, sound, animation, notifications, clutter, or cognitive load.

Human Scale should prefer **configurable clarity**.

## Justice & Rights

Rights procedures must be usable.

Accessibility belongs in courts, police interactions, legal documents, detention, voting, complaint systems, counsel, interpretation, evidence presentation, and appeals.

Formal equality is not meaningful if the process assumes abilities a person does not have.

## Spirituality & Meaning

Religious and secular communities should make participation possible without treating disability as moral failure, divine punishment, inspirational spectacle, or something that must be spiritually “fixed.”

Spiritual care should follow the person's beliefs and preferences rather than the caregiver's metaphysics.

## AI & Automation

AI can expand accessibility through:

- captioning;
- speech recognition;
- text simplification;
- translation;
- image description;
- communication assistance;
- memory and executive-function support;
- navigation;
- personalized interfaces.

It can also fail disabled users through biased data, inaccessible interfaces, hallucinated descriptions, surveillance, loss of human service channels, or automated decisions that are difficult to contest.

Human Scale should ask whether AI makes **capability and recourse** better, not merely whether it is innovative.

---

# 8. Universal design is a default, not the whole solution

Designing systems for wider ranges of people from the beginning can reduce the need for retrofits and individual exceptions.

But no universal design will eliminate every need for individualized accommodation.

Human Scale should therefore combine:

- inclusive default design;
- configurable interfaces;
- individual accommodation;
- assistive technology;
- human assistance;
- alternative channels;
- specialized services where needed.

The goal is participation, not ideological commitment to one accessibility mechanism.

---

# 9. Recommended doctrine-wide changes

1. **Replace the hidden “default adult” with explicit human variation.** Disability, age, neurotype, chronic illness, and temporary impairment belong in the baseline model.
2. **Define independence as control over one's life, not absence of support.**
3. **Treat assistive technology as paradigmatic Human Scale technology.**
4. **Replace walkability-only language with accessible mobility and realistic alternatives.**
5. **Replace minimalist calm with configurable clarity.**
6. **Treat digital participation as a legitimate accessibility layer, not second-best community.**
7. **Include energy-dependent disability needs in resilience planning.**
8. **Count disability-related costs and constraints in economic freedom.**
9. **Make accessible participation a precondition of legitimate local governance.**
10. **Design with disabled people, not merely for them.**
11. **Keep universal design and individual accommodation together.**
12. **Measure capability and participation rather than conformity to one idea of normal functioning.**

---

# 10. What would change our minds?

Human Scale should revise an accessibility intervention when disabled users show that it reduces real capability, creates new barriers, increases safety risk, or imposes disproportionate burdens compared with better alternatives.

It should not assume a feature is universally accessible because it meets a checklist.

It should accept that some accessibility needs conflict and test configurable or multiple-channel designs rather than seek a fictional single interface for everyone.

It should favor specialized or larger-scale services where local provision cannot maintain competence, reliability, affordability, or safety.

And it should treat criticism from disabled users as evidence about system performance — not as a request for kindness from the margins.

---

# Result of Cross-Cutting Audit 01

**Human Scale should not ask disabled people to become more compatible with a preferred human environment. It should ask how bodies, tools, support, institutions, and environments can be arranged so more people have real capability, participation, dignity, and choice.**

Accessibility is not a concession to exceptional humans.

Human variation is part of the scale.
