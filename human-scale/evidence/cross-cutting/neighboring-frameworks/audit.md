# Cross-Cutting Audit 09 — Human Scale & Neighboring Frameworks

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> A philosophy becomes easier to evaluate when it stops acting as though it invented every idea it uses.

Human Scale shares important intuitions with older political, economic, ecological, religious, urban, and philosophical traditions.

That is not a weakness.

The question is whether the project combines those ideas coherently, identifies where it disagrees, and contributes something testable rather than rebranding a familiar ideology.

This is a **comparative map**, not a claim that any thinker or movement can be reduced to a few paragraphs. Each tradition contains internal disagreement, historical evolution, and competing interpretations.

The purpose is to identify:

- family resemblance;
- genuine disagreement;
- ideas Human Scale should borrow explicitly;
- places where Human Scale may be reinventing existing work;
- questions that become sharper when another framework pushes back.

---

# 1. The Capability Approach

## Family resemblance

Human Scale is especially close to the capability tradition associated with Amartya Sen and Martha Nussbaum in one basic respect:

**resources are not the final outcome; what people are actually able to be and do matters.**

That aligns with Human Scale's repeated shift from inputs to practical capability:

- mobility rather than one transport mode;
- independence as control rather than absence of support;
- healthcare as function and access rather than service volume;
- education as capability rather than credential accumulation;
- technology as agency rather than feature count;
- rights as usable rather than merely written down.

UNDP's human-development tradition similarly treats expanding people's choices and capabilities as broader than economic output alone.

Useful entry:
- UNDP Human Development Reports: https://hdr.undp.org/

## Difference

Human Scale places more explicit emphasis on:

- evolutionary/biological mismatch;
- ecological participation;
- technological architecture and repairability;
- attention;
- scale and institutional legibility;
- local physical environments;
- versioned evidence audits and public falsification.

## What to borrow

Human Scale should use capability language more deliberately because it gives the doctrine a mature vocabulary for a concept it already uses constantly.

Important correction:

**do not measure freedom only by resources or formal options. Measure whether a person can realistically convert them into valued functioning.**

# 2. Liberalism

“Liberalism” contains many traditions, but a broad family resemblance includes individual liberty, rights, pluralism, rule of law, freedom of conscience, and limits on arbitrary power.

## Family resemblance

Human Scale's Justice & Rights audit is strongly liberal in this sense:

- equal dignity;
- freedom of belief and nonbelief;
- privacy;
- due process;
- practical exit;
- protection for dissenters and unpopular minorities;
- institutions constrained by rights;
- skepticism of compulsory ideological conformity.

## Difference

Human Scale is less satisfied with formal liberty alone.

It asks whether environmental design, material insecurity, inaccessible infrastructure, technology lock-in, administrative burden, or dependence make nominal freedom difficult to exercise.

It also treats ecology, care, embodied health, attention, and community as public design concerns more explicitly than some individual-rights-centered liberal traditions.

## Tension to preserve

Liberal critics should keep asking Human Scale:

**When does designing for flourishing become paternalism?**

Human Scale should never answer this once and for all. It should require justification whenever institutions shift from creating options to restricting peaceful choices.

---

# 3. Conservatism

Conservative traditions often emphasize inherited institutions, social continuity, family, local association, practical knowledge, limits of abstract planning, and skepticism toward rapid transformation whose consequences are poorly understood.

## Family resemblance

Human Scale shares:

- caution about remaking human environments faster than consequences are understood;
- interest in family, community, ritual, local knowledge, and continuity;
- suspicion of theories that treat people as abstract units;
- respect for evolved or inherited practices as sources of information;
- preference for experimentation over totalizing redesign.

## Difference

Human Scale does not grant tradition a presumption strong enough to override:

- rights;
- evidence;
- accessibility;
- pluralism;
- safe exit;
- technological possibility;
- new family or community forms that work better.

The Evidence Atlas explicitly says age does not make a belief or institution true.

## Tension to preserve

Conservative critics should ask:

**What important local knowledge or social function could a clean reform proposal destroy before reformers notice?**

Human Scale needs that question.

---

# 4. Socialism and Social Democracy

These traditions vary enormously, but commonly emphasize power in economic relationships, labor, ownership, inequality, social provision, collective action, and the limits of markets in meeting every human need.

## Family resemblance

Human Scale shares concern with:

- worker voice;
- care work;
- economic insecurity;
- concentrated ownership and monopoly;
- public goods;
- social insurance;
- bargaining power;
- who captures automation gains;
- material conditions that determine whether exit is realistic.

## Difference

Human Scale does not begin from one preferred ownership structure or from class conflict as the master explanation of society.

It retains strong roles for:

- markets;
- entrepreneurship;
- competition;
- private ownership;
- profit;
- distributed experimentation;
- consumer benefits through lower prices and better products.

It treats cooperatives, employee ownership, public ownership, regulation, social insurance, competitive markets, and direct transfers as mechanisms to compare rather than steps toward one predetermined economic form.

## Tension to preserve

Socialist critics should ask:

**Does “choice” mean much when bargaining power and ownership are extremely unequal?**

Human Scale should keep that challenge visible.

---

# 5. Libertarianism

Libertarian traditions place strong weight on voluntary exchange, property, freedom of association, decentralized decision-making, and limits on state coercion.

## Family resemblance

Human Scale shares:

- credible exit;
- skepticism of coercion;
- local experimentation;
- open systems;
- competition;
- entrepreneurship;
- user control;
- freedom of conscience;
- concern about bureaucratic burden.

## Difference

Human Scale treats several conditions as potentially requiring collective institutions:

- pollution/externalities;
- rights enforcement;
- public goods;
- catastrophic health and disability needs;
- infrastructure;
- monopoly;
- disaster response;
- safety regulation;
- material conditions that can make formal exit practically impossible.

## Tension to preserve

Libertarian critics should ask:

**Are we solving a genuine coordination or rights problem, or using a good outcome as an excuse for unnecessary coercion?**

That question belongs in the Human Scale policy test.

# 6. Subsidiarity and Catholic Social Thought

The principle of subsidiarity is often summarized as handling matters at the lowest or most local competent level while allowing larger institutions to support or act when smaller units cannot adequately perform the function.

A classic statement appears in *Quadragesimo Anno*.

Primary text:
- Vatican, *Quadragesimo Anno*: https://www.vatican.va/content/pius-xi/en/encyclicals/documents/hf_p-xi_enc_19310515_quadragesimo-anno.html

## Family resemblance

This is one of the clearest ancestors of Human Scale's principle:

> **power should live at the smallest scale capable of solving the problem well.**

Human Scale's later corrections — rights floor, institutional capacity, cross-boundary coordination, and vertical appeal — make it even closer to a mature subsidiarity model than to simplistic localism.

## Difference

Human Scale is religiously plural and does not derive its political anthropology from Catholic doctrine.

It also applies the scale test to software, energy, AI, data architecture, food, transport, and other technical systems beyond the traditional state/community framing.

## What to borrow

Human Scale should acknowledge subsidiarity explicitly rather than present the idea as novel.

# 7. E.F. Schumacher / “Small Is Beautiful” traditions

Schumacher's work questioned scale, centralized production, technological gigantism, and economic measurement detached from human and ecological purposes.

## Family resemblance

The resemblance is obvious:

- human-scale production;
- technology judged by human purposes;
- skepticism of growth/efficiency as complete measures;
- local competence;
- ecological limits;
- meaningful work.

## Difference

Human Scale has already been forced by its audits to abandon **small = good**.

It strongly defends large systems where scale improves:

- electrical reliability;
- specialized medicine;
- science;
- rights enforcement;
- disaster response;
- manufacturing;
- transport networks;
- digital infrastructure.

Human Scale's distinctive rule is therefore:

**scale should have to justify itself in both directions.**

Small systems can fail from weak capacity just as large systems can fail from distance and concentration.

---

# 8. Degrowth

Contemporary degrowth scholarship and activism generally challenges the pursuit of aggregate economic growth as the organizing goal of high-consumption economies and emphasizes ecological sustainability, sufficiency, redistribution, care, and well-being.

## Family resemblance

Human Scale shares skepticism toward:

- GDP as a sufficient measure of progress;
- consumption as a complete theory of welfare;
- ecological damage treated as an externality;
- labor and time organized only around expanding output.

It shares interest in:

- care;
- human time;
- sufficiency;
- reduced material waste;
- ecological limits;
- nonmarket sources of flourishing.

## Difference

Human Scale is not committed to reducing GDP, energy use, technological capability, or production in aggregate as an objective.

It is comfortable with **abundance** where abundance can be made clean, safe, resilient, and widely beneficial:

- energy;
- medicine;
- housing;
- computation;
- knowledge;
- transport access;
- assistive technology;
- food security.

Human Scale's question is not “growth or degrowth?”

It is:

**what should become more abundant, what should become less material-intensive, and which metric tells us whether lives and ecosystems actually improved?**

---

# 9. Ecomodernism

Ecomodernist arguments generally emphasize using technological capability, dense/efficient systems, clean energy, modernization, and intensified production to reduce environmental pressures while improving human welfare.

A movement statement is *An Ecomodernist Manifesto*.

Entry:
- https://www.ecomodernism.org/

## Family resemblance

Human Scale shares:

- refusal to romanticize poverty or preindustrial life;
- support for advanced energy;
- technological optimism under conditions;
- belief that modern science and infrastructure can reduce ecological harm;
- willingness to use nuclear power, dense systems, high-productivity agriculture, and other large technologies where evidence favors them.

## Difference

Human Scale gives more weight to:

- attention;
- local agency;
- repairability;
- social connection;
- human time;
- lived place;
- embodied participation in ecological systems;
- risks of dependency and institutional opacity.

Ecomodernist critics should ask Human Scale whether local participation is being valued more than measurable ecological outcomes.

Human Scale critics should ask ecomodernism whether technical decoupling is enough if everyday life becomes psychologically, socially, or politically alienating.

That disagreement is productive.

# 10. Strong Towns and incremental urbanism

Strong Towns emphasizes financially resilient local development, incremental change, productive places, local feedback, and skepticism toward large top-down infrastructure commitments whose long-term liabilities exceed their value.

Official principles:
- Strong Towns, Core Principles: https://www.strongtowns.org/journal/2022/12/19/the-strong-towns-approach

## Family resemblance

Human Scale shares:

- small experiments;
- local observation;
- “receipts” and measurable outcomes;
- streets and places designed around people rather than vehicle throughput alone;
- fiscal/infrastructure maintenance reality;
- skepticism toward giant irreversible plans;
- iterative adaptation.

## Difference

Human Scale is a broader philosophy of civilization rather than primarily a local-development framework.

It also explicitly pressure-tests accessibility, health, AI, energy, family, rights, spirituality, and labor.

## What to borrow

Human Scale should learn from Strong Towns' emphasis on **small bets, feedback, and financial reality** rather than invent its own language for every local implementation principle.

# 11. Solarpunk

Solarpunk is a decentralized cultural and aesthetic movement imagining ecological, technologically capable, community-oriented futures often involving renewable energy, gardens, craft, repair, mutual aid, and anti-dystopian optimism.

## Family resemblance

Visually and emotionally, Human Scale often lands near solarpunk:

- gardens + computers;
- clean energy;
- repair;
- local production;
- community;
- ecological integration;
- technology in service of life;
- optimistic future-building rather than pure critique.

## Difference

Human Scale is trying to become less dependent on aesthetic intuition.

Its Architecture audit explicitly says a humane place need not **look** organic, low-tech, village-like, or green.

A concrete high-rise, nuclear plant, industrial facility, automated warehouse, highway, cloud service, or dense city can be Human Scale if it serves the relevant functions better.

Solarpunk is useful as imagination.

Human Scale's Evidence Atlas exists to prevent imagination from becoming evidence.

---

# 12. Effective Altruism

Effective Altruism asks how evidence and reasoning can be used to do more good with limited resources and often emphasizes explicit comparison of interventions and opportunity cost.

## Family resemblance

Human Scale shares:

- evidence grading;
- willingness to compare interventions;
- concern with opportunity cost;
- desire to revise beliefs;
- interest in measurable outcomes;
- global as well as local consequences.

## Difference

Human Scale is more skeptical of reducing value to one maximizable metric and places more weight on:

- rights;
- participation;
- plural values;
- local knowledge;
- relationships;
- agency;
- institutional legitimacy;
- meaning;
- place;
- democratic disagreement.

Human Scale should nevertheless borrow EA's uncomfortable question:

**What else could we have done with these resources, and would it have produced more of the outcomes we claim to care about?**

---

# 13. Doughnut / Well-being Economics

Frameworks such as Doughnut Economics and well-being budgeting reject GDP as the sole measure of success and attempt to combine social foundations with ecological constraints.

## Family resemblance

Human Scale strongly shares the desire for dashboards of actual human and ecological outcomes rather than output alone.

## Difference

Human Scale is less a macroeconomic accounting framework and more a theory of lived environments, agency, scale, technology, and institutional design.

Its distinctive contribution could be to connect macro indicators to **human-time and capability receipts** at the level where people experience systems.

---

# 14. Where Human Scale may actually be distinctive

No single ingredient appears unique.

The potentially distinctive combination is:

1. **Capability + embodiment:** begin from what diverse people can actually do in bodies and environments.
2. **Scale as empirical variable:** small and large systems both have to justify themselves.
3. **Technological abundance without technological totalism:** use powerful modern systems while preserving agency, exit, repair, privacy, skill, and human time where those matter.
4. **Pluralism with a rights floor:** community, family, spirituality, and localism stop where coercion and rights violations begin.
5. **Evidence / values separation:** empirical findings are not allowed to smuggle in political conclusions.
6. **Versioned doctrine:** public revisions and audits are part of the philosophy itself.
7. **Receipts:** translate abstractions into measurable human time, access, burden, capability, resilience, and local implementation.
8. **Falsification as culture:** ask critics to produce the next doctrine diff.
9. **Personal + institutional bridge:** Field Guide and Program address individual adaptation and collective redesign without pretending one substitutes for the other.
10. **The village + the computer:** refuse the forced choice between advanced capability and a more embodied, relational, ecological life.

Whether that combination deserves a new label should be earned through use, not declared.

# 15. The strongest criticism from each neighbor

Human Scale should keep these objections visible:

- **Capability approach:** Are you measuring actual capability, or aesthetic/environmental proxies?
- **Liberalism:** When does flourishing design become paternalism?
- **Conservatism:** What evolved function will your clean reform accidentally destroy?
- **Socialism:** How meaningful is exit when ownership and bargaining power are highly unequal?
- **Libertarianism:** Is coercion genuinely necessary for this outcome?
- **Subsidiarity:** Have you really assigned the function to the lowest competent level — with support rather than abandonment?
- **Schumacher:** Has scale become so large that people lose comprehension, participation, or meaningful work?
- **Degrowth:** Are claims of clean abundance hiding rebound, extraction, or ecological limits?
- **Ecomodernism:** Are you romanticizing local participation when centralized/high-productivity systems achieve better ecological outcomes?
- **Strong Towns:** Can you test this cheaply before committing to a giant irreversible plan?
- **Solarpunk:** Can people imagine and desire your future, or is it only a policy spreadsheet?
- **Effective Altruism:** What is the opportunity cost, and are you choosing a lower-impact intervention because it feels aligned?
- **Well-being economics:** Can your values become measurable enough to influence real budgets and institutions?

A mature Human Scale project should be able to answer these critics without redefining itself to make every disagreement disappear.

---

# 16. Recommended project changes

1. **Explicitly acknowledge intellectual ancestors and neighbors.**
2. **Use the capability approach more deliberately when talking about practical freedom.**
3. **Name subsidiarity as an important precedent for the scale principle.**
4. **Distinguish Human Scale from “small is beautiful” by making the two-way scale test explicit.**
5. **Distinguish Human Scale from degrowth by defining selective abundance and selective dematerialization.**
6. **Use ecomodernism as a recurring ecological challenge against romantic localism.**
7. **Borrow Strong Towns-style incremental experimentation for local implementation.**
8. **Borrow EA's opportunity-cost discipline without reducing all values to one metric.**
9. **Treat solarpunk as imagination, not evidence.**
10. **Do not market Human Scale as unprecedented. Market the versioned synthesis, evidence discipline, and implementation method.**

---

# Result of Cross-Cutting Audit 09

**Human Scale is not intellectually isolated, and it should stop behaving as though every ingredient is new. Its strongest claim to distinctiveness is a synthesis: capability and embodiment, pluralism and rights, selective abundance, empirical scale, technological agency, public falsification, and practical receipts connecting the philosophy to ordinary life.**

The project earns its name only if that synthesis proves useful.
