# Cross-Cutting Audit 07 — Ownership & Automation Distribution

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> Productivity is a technical outcome. Who receives its gains is an institutional outcome.

Human Scale repeatedly argues that automation should be allowed to return some human time and that ownership can matter for practical freedom.

Those are important political intuitions, but they become sloppy if the doctrine implies that technology itself determines wages, profits, prices, employment, leisure, or ownership.

It does not.

The same productivity improvement can produce very different outcomes depending on competition, bargaining power, labor markets, taxation, ownership, regulation, consumer demand, investment, social insurance, intellectual property, capital markets, and the design of the firm.

The cross-cutting question is therefore:

**When AI and automation create more output with less human labor per task, through which channels do the gains reach ordinary people — and who bears the transition costs?**

---

# 1. Exposure, productivity, displacement, and distribution are different questions

A technology can affect a job without eliminating it.

A job can become more productive without wages rising proportionally.

A firm's profits can rise while prices fall.

Consumers can gain even when workers in one occupation lose.

A worker can keep a job while losing bargaining power, or change tasks while earning more.

Human Scale should separate at least five questions:

1. **Capability:** What tasks can the technology perform?
2. **Adoption:** Where is the technology actually deployed?
3. **Productivity:** Does output per unit of labor/capital/time improve in real settings?
4. **Labor transition:** Which tasks, occupations, hours, skills, and jobs change?
5. **Distribution:** Who receives wages, profits, lower prices, ownership returns, tax revenue, public services, or time?

Collapsing these into “AI will take jobs” or “AI will create abundance” hides the actual institutional problem.

ILO's 2025 work on generative-AI exposure similarly emphasizes job transformation rather than treating occupational exposure as automatic job disappearance.

Source:
- ILO, Generative AI and Jobs: A Refined Global Index of Occupational Exposure: https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure

---

# 2. Productivity gains have multiple legitimate claimants

A productivity gain can appear as:

- higher wages;
- higher profits;
- lower prices;
- better quality;
- new products;
- more output;
- shorter hours;
- more hiring elsewhere;
- public tax revenue;
- ownership returns;
- investment in future capacity;
- unemployment or reduced hours for displaced workers;
- some combination of all of these.

Human Scale should not pretend every gain ought to become leisure.

Investment can produce future capability. Lower prices can help consumers. Profit can reward risk and fund innovation. Wages can support workers. Taxes can fund public goods. Shorter hours can return time.

The doctrine's value judgment is narrower:

**human time and broadly shared material gains should be legitimate outcomes of rising productivity, not accidental leftovers after every other claim has been satisfied.**

That is a political commitment, not a theorem of economics.

---

# 3. Ownership matters because income streams come from different places

People can receive economic gains through:

- wages;
- self-employment/business income;
- savings interest;
- pensions;
- housing or land ownership;
- shares and retirement accounts;
- employee ownership;
- cooperative ownership;
- public/social wealth funds;
- intellectual property;
- community ownership;
- transfers and social insurance.

If automation makes productive capital more important relative to routine labor in a particular domain, ownership of that capital can influence who receives upside.

But “broaden ownership” is not one policy.

Different mechanisms create different risks involving:

- concentration;
- liquidity;
- diversification;
- governance;
- worker exposure to one employer;
- public accountability;
- incentives;
- entrepreneurship;
- administrative complexity;
- political capture.

Human Scale should treat ownership forms as mechanisms to test rather than moral categories.

---

# 4. Employee ownership can align gains — and concentrate risk

Employee stock, profit sharing, cooperatives, and employee-ownership structures can give workers a claim on enterprise upside and sometimes a greater governance role.

They can also concentrate a worker's labor income and financial wealth in the same organization.

A firm failure can then threaten both job and savings.

Human Scale should therefore prefer designs that consider:

- diversification;
- vesting;
- liquidity;
- voting/governance rights;
- transparency;
- whether ownership is truly additional compensation rather than substituted for adequate pay;
- what happens when the worker leaves;
- whether lower-wage workers participate meaningfully.

Employee ownership should be evaluated as one tool for distribution and voice, not a universal firm model.

---

# 5. Competition is a distribution mechanism too

When productivity gains occur in competitive markets, some gains may reach consumers through lower prices, better quality, or new services.

When markets are highly concentrated or switching costs are high, firms may retain more of the gains.

Human Scale's technology and economics chapters already emphasize credible exit and competition.

The cross-cutting correction is:

**distribution policy is not only taxes and transfers. Market structure, interoperability, entry barriers, procurement, labor mobility, and switching costs can influence who captures technological gains.**

This does not mean more competition solves every distribution problem.

Some sectors have network effects, natural-monopoly features, high fixed costs, strong scale economies, or safety requirements.

Again: function before ideology.

---

# 6. Bargaining power affects transition outcomes

Workers negotiate from different positions depending on:

- unemployment and labor demand;
- specialized skills;
- credential requirements;
- immigration status;
- savings and material security;
- geographic mobility;
- employer concentration;
- unions or collective institutions;
- noncompete and contractual restrictions;
- access to healthcare and benefits outside the job;
- knowledge of how AI changes their own work.

ILO case studies on AI and algorithmic management show that social dialogue is one institutional mechanism through which workers and employers can negotiate implementation.

Source:
- ILO, Global case studies of social dialogue on AI and algorithmic management: https://www.ilo.org/publications/global-case-studies-social-dialogue-ai-and-algorithmic-management

Human Scale should remain plural about the mechanism — direct employee voice, unions, works councils, ownership, labor standards, portable benefits, or other structures — while asking whether workers have meaningful information and credible influence over changes that reshape their work.

---

# 7. Material security changes the meaning of technological freedom

A worker with savings, portable healthcare, available jobs, affordable housing, and usable retraining can respond differently to automation than a worker one missed paycheck away from eviction.

Human Scale should therefore treat transition policy as partly a question of **option value**.

Possible mechanisms include:

- unemployment insurance;
- wage insurance;
- portable benefits;
- training or education;
- relocation support;
- public healthcare or other benefit portability;
- cash transfers;
- tax credits;
- severance;
- worker ownership;
- labor-market matching;
- entrepreneurship support;
- public employment;
- reduced work time;
- retirement transitions.

No single mechanism is scientifically mandated.

They should be compared by:

- actual take-up;
- speed;
- income replacement;
- employment outcomes;
- administrative burden;
- incentive effects;
- equity;
- cost;
- whether people can realistically use them during disruption.

---

# 8. Training is useful but can become a ritual answer

“Retrain workers” is not enough.

Training helps when:

- a real labor demand exists;
- the skill is learnable in the available time;
- credentials are recognized;
- the person can afford to train;
- childcare, disability, transport, language, and scheduling barriers are addressed;
- wages justify the transition;
- jobs are geographically or remotely accessible.

Training fails when institutions teach skills without realistic demand or when displaced workers absorb most transition cost while firms capture most gains.

Human Scale should measure training by downstream capability and employment, not enrollment or certificates.

---

# 9. Shorter work is one distribution channel

If a task takes half as long, the saved time can become:

- more tasks;
- fewer workers;
- shorter schedules;
- faster service;
- higher quality;
- more training;
- lower prices;
- more profit.

Shorter work is therefore not an automatic consequence of automation.

It is an institutional choice that may work in some settings and fail in others.

Human Scale should continue to test shorter days, weeks, job sharing, phased retirement, and other designs by:

- output;
- service quality;
- workload intensity;
- wages;
- hiring;
- health;
- worker preference;
- customer impact;
- small-business viability.

The value claim remains:

**time should be allowed to compete with output as one possible dividend of progress.**

---

# 10. Public ownership is not one thing

Governments can own or invest in:

- infrastructure;
- utilities;
- land;
- pension funds;
- sovereign/social wealth funds;
- research institutions;
- data or digital infrastructure;
- enterprises.

Public ownership can socialize returns or protect public goods.

It can also create political interference, weak competition, corruption, opaque governance, poor capital allocation, or barriers to private experimentation.

Human Scale should apply the same policy test:

- What problem does ownership solve?
- Why ownership rather than regulation, procurement, subsidy, taxation, competition, or public standards?
- Who governs the asset?
- Who receives returns?
- What transparency exists?
- Can poor performance be corrected?
- Is private entry still possible where useful?

The doctrine should not move from “corporate concentration is bad” to “state ownership is automatically human-scale.”

---

# 11. Data and models may become productive assets

AI depends on combinations of:

- compute;
- energy;
- chips;
- models;
- data;
- software;
- talent;
- distribution;
- user networks;
- intellectual property;
- capital.

Debates over ownership and distribution may increasingly involve access to these assets, not only factory machinery or land.

Possible Human Scale questions:

- Can users export their data and work?
- Can small firms access competitive AI capability?
- Are public-interest datasets available under appropriate privacy rules?
- Do dominant platforms create switching barriers?
- Are public research investments generating broadly accessible knowledge?
- Does regulation entrench incumbents unintentionally?
- Can open models increase competition without creating unacceptable security risks?

The architecture of AI markets can become distribution policy.

---

# 12. Automation can expand unpaid capability too

Not every gain appears in GDP or employment.

AI can help people:

- learn;
- translate;
- navigate bureaucracy;
- repair things;
- create art;
- write software;
- manage disability;
- plan care;
- understand documents;
- start small businesses;
- organize communities.

These capabilities can matter even without a wage increase.

Human Scale should therefore avoid evaluating AI distribution only through jobs and income.

The relevant outcome is broader:

**who gains practical capability, time, ownership, consumption, security, and voice?**

---

# 13. Recommended doctrine-wide changes

1. **Separate productivity from distribution.** Technology changes feasible production; institutions shape who receives the gains.
2. **Separate AI exposure, adoption, productivity, labor transition, and distribution.**
3. **Treat wages, lower prices, profit, ownership, public revenue, and time as distinct distribution channels.**
4. **Keep broader ownership as a hypothesis to test through multiple mechanisms.**
5. **Evaluate employee ownership with diversification and worker-risk concerns.**
6. **Treat competition, interoperability, entry, and switching costs as distribution mechanisms too.**
7. **Make worker voice about information and meaningful influence, not one required institutional form.**
8. **Treat material security as transition infrastructure.**
9. **Evaluate retraining by real downstream outcomes.**
10. **Keep shorter work as one possible productivity dividend, not a universal endpoint.**
11. **Evaluate public ownership functionally rather than ideologically.**
12. **Measure capability gains outside formal employment and income.**

---

# 14. What would change our minds?

Human Scale should weaken ownership-focused policies where broad ownership creates little meaningful income or agency, imposes high risk, or is inferior to wages, competition, public services, or other mechanisms.

It should strengthen employee or cooperative ownership where rigorous evidence shows durable gains in wealth, voice, productivity, resilience, or worker outcomes without unacceptable concentration risk.

It should favor lower prices over direct redistribution where competition reliably passes productivity gains to consumers and distributional outcomes are acceptable.

It should strengthen social-insurance or transition systems where displacement is concentrated and markets do not provide realistic adjustment paths.

It should reduce shorter-work emphasis where reduced hours intensify work, lower material security, reduce service quality, or are consistently rejected by workers with meaningful choice.

And it should revise the entire automation program if real-world evidence shows that the most important gains arrive through channels Human Scale has underweighted.

---

# Result of Cross-Cutting Audit 07

**AI and automation do not contain a built-in social contract. They expand what can be produced. Human beings still decide — through firms, markets, ownership, bargaining, public institutions, and politics — whether the result becomes wider capability, higher wages, lower prices, profit, public wealth, more time, concentrated power, or some combination.**

The distribution of abundance is part of the design.
