Human Scale Evidence Atlas

What do we actually know, what do we merely suspect, what do we value, and what are we choosing politically?

A philosophy becomes more trustworthy when it knows which parts are evidence, which parts are hypotheses, and which parts are choices.

Why this exists

The Human Scale Doctrine makes claims about bodies, relationships, work, technology, place, ecology, institutions, and civilization. Those claims should not all be treated as though they have the same kind of support.

This atlas does not try to prove Human Scale as one package. It breaks the worldview into claims that can be challenged separately.

The purpose is not to decorate the doctrine with citations. The purpose is to make it harder for the doctrine to lie to itself.

The labels

Strong evidence

Consistent empirical support

Substantial evidence supports the claim, while mechanisms, dose, subgroups, and policy implications may still be uncertain.

Moderate

Direction is credible

Evidence points somewhere useful, but causality, magnitude, generalizability, or implementation remains meaningfully uncertain.

Hypothesis

Worth testing, not declaring

A plausible principle that should remain vulnerable to evidence and domain-specific failure.

Value / proposal

A choice, not a discovery

Evidence can inform it, but science alone cannot tell us what society ought to value or which institution must implement that value.

Initial claim register

Strong evidence

1. Regular physical activity matters for health.

The doctrine's emphasis on movement has a strong empirical foundation. This supports designing daily life so movement is easier; it does not establish one universal exercise system or imply that exercise solves every health problem.

WHO Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour (2020) ↗
Ekelund et al., The Lancet (2016) ↗
Strong association

2. Social relationships and connection matter for health.

A large literature links stronger social relationships with better health and survival. Causal pathways remain complex, so Human Scale should not turn this into a claim that every person needs the same amount of contact or that tightly knit communities are automatically good.

Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton, PLOS Medicine (2010) ↗
Strong evidence

3. Chronically very long working hours can create measurable health risk.

WHO/ILO analyses link high working-hour exposure, especially 55+ hours per week, with increased ischemic heart disease and stroke risk. This supports treating time as occupational health; it does not prove one ideal workweek for every occupation or person.

Pega et al., Environment International (2021) ↗
Moderate evidence

4. Nature contact is associated with health and well-being.

Green and natural environments are repeatedly associated with well-being and health. Much of the population evidence remains observational. The often-cited 120-minute finding is an association, not proof of a universal biological threshold.

White et al., Scientific Reports (2019) ↗
Moderate evidence

5. The built environment can influence movement and health.

Walkability, connectivity, proximity, transit, and land-use patterns are associated with physical activity and several health outcomes. Longitudinal research strengthens the case that place matters, while residential self-selection and socioeconomic differences still complicate causal claims.

Creatore et al., JAMA (2016) ↗
Moderate / emerging

6. Unpredictable work schedules can damage well-being.

Research among hourly and service-sector workers links schedule instability with sleep disruption, psychological distress, and worse well-being. The literature is newer and more context-sensitive than the evidence on very long working hours.

Schneider & Harknett, Social Forces (2019) ↗

Claims we should not call settled science

Working hypothesis

Human-scale systems preserve agency and resilience.

Sometimes. Local systems can also be under-resourced, exclusionary, corrupt, fragile, or incapable of solving cross-boundary problems. The real rule stays: prefer smaller scale when it works.

Value + hypothesis

Open, repairable, interoperable technology protects agency.

Exit, ownership, portability, and repair matter to the doctrine. But security, coordination, support, incentives, and technical complexity create real tradeoffs that must be tested by domain.

Value + proposal

Productivity gains should return some human time.

Data can measure productivity, health, income, and time. It cannot decide how gains should be divided among wages, prices, profits, ownership, public goods, and leisure. Human Scale makes a value choice here.

Value + proposal

Everyone should have meaningful access to the living world.

Nature research can inform this commitment, but equal access to gardens, trees, habitat, parks, food forests, and stewardship is ultimately a moral and political decision.

Political design

Power should operate at the smallest effective scale.

Subsidiarity is a political design principle, not a law of nature. The scale should follow the problem while a rights floor remains protected.

Design value

Advanced technology should become quieter.

This is partly a preference. It becomes empirical when we ask whether a tool actually saves time, reduces interruptions, preserves skill, protects exit, and improves outcomes.

What would change our minds?

Local systems repeatedly perform worse without meaningful gains in agency, resilience, or accountability.
Stronger causal studies fail to reproduce expected benefits from specific nature interventions.
Shorter-work experiments consistently worsen well-being or material security once income and workload are controlled.
Open or interoperable systems create harms that cannot be mitigated without destroying the intended benefit.
Dense, advanced urban environments meet needs for nature, movement, community, childhood, quiet, and agency as well as or better than smaller alternatives.
A favored policy produces large unintended burdens for the people it was supposed to help.
A doctrine that cannot lose an argument is not learning.

The research backlog

The next audits should cover sleep and circadian rhythm; childhood independence and outdoor play; green-space quality; gardening; third places; commuting; schedule control; shorter-workweek trials; disaster resilience; local and employee ownership; housing supply and neighborhood form; shade and urban heat; cooking and dietary patterns; right-to-repair; digital attention; AI and skill retention; energy reliability; administrative burden; and decentralization versus state capacity.

Research rules

State claims narrowly enough to be wrong.
Prefer stronger designs. Systematic reviews, large longitudinal work, randomized trials where feasible, quasi-experiments, official datasets, and primary sources.
Separate association from causation.
Report uncertainty and contrary evidence.
Do not turn one population into a universal human rule.
Separate evidence → values → political mechanism.
Ask what result would make us change the doctrine.

The purpose

This first bibliography is intentionally small and the grades are provisional. The atlas should expand chapter by chapter and become stricter as the doctrine becomes more ambitious.

Human Scale should be allowed to have convictions. It should not be allowed to hide uncertainty.
