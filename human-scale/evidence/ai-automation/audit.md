# Evidence Audit 17 — AI & Automation Under Pressure

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> AI should expand capability without quietly converting convenience into dependency, delegation into lost accountability, or occupational exposure into a prediction of inevitable job loss.

Chapter 17 is deliberately neither anti-AI nor acceleration-at-any-cost. Its central value — intelligence in service of human agency — survives.

The audit adds a stricter empirical discipline:

- AI risk is context specific;
- occupational exposure is not the same as automation or unemployment;
- augmentation and automation often coexist within the same job;
- “human in the loop” is meaningful only when the human has information, time, authority, and competence;
- open, local, small, private, frontier, proprietary, and centralized AI each create different tradeoffs rather than a moral ranking;
- claims about deskilling and dependency need domain-specific evidence.

---

# 1. What survives strongly

## AI risk should be managed by context, impact, and lifecycle

**Grade: Strong governance framework**

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework treats AI as a socio-technical risk-management problem. Its trustworthiness characteristics include validity and reliability, safety, security and resilience, accountability and transparency, explainability and interpretability, privacy enhancement, and fairness with harmful bias managed.

NIST explicitly emphasizes context and tradeoffs rather than one universal risk threshold.

Sources:
- NIST AI RMF: https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework
- NIST AI RMF FAQs: https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework/ai-risk-management-framework-faqs
- NIST Generative AI Profile: https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence

This strongly supports the chapter's rule that a birthday-card chatbot and an automated high-stakes eligibility or clinical system should not share one governance model.

## Human rights, agency, transparency, safety, and accountability are legitimate AI design goals

**Grade: Strong international governance consensus / value framework**

The OECD AI Principles, updated in 2024, explicitly include human-centred values, human agency and oversight, transparency, robustness, security, safety, accountability, privacy, fairness, and rights.

Source:
- OECD AI Principles: https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/ai-principles.html

Human Scale's agency language therefore sits inside a broader international governance tradition rather than being an eccentric anti-automation preference.

## Generative AI is likely to transform many jobs, but exposure is not job loss

**Grade: Strong current labor-market exposure analysis; long-term outcomes uncertain**

ILO's 2025 global index estimates that one in four workers is in an occupation with some exposure to generative AI, while a much smaller share is in the highest exposure category. The ILO emphasizes transformation rather than wholesale replacement as the more likely near-term effect.

Source:
- ILO, Generative AI and Jobs: A Refined Global Index of Occupational Exposure: https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-refined-global-index-occupational-exposure

Human Scale should therefore stop using “automation risk” as shorthand for unemployment prediction.

A task can be exposed while the job changes, grows, fragments, becomes more productive, or shifts toward other tasks.

## Worker participation can shape AI implementation

**Grade: Case-study evidence / institutional mechanism**

ILO case studies document social dialogue around AI and algorithmic management across multiple countries and levels of organization.

This does not prove one union, works-council, or governance model is optimal. It does support the chapter's claim that people affected by workplace AI can possess relevant local knowledge and legitimate interests in implementation.

Source:
- ILO, Global case studies of social dialogue on AI and algorithmic management: https://www.ilo.org/publications/global-case-studies-social-dialogue-ai-and-algorithmic-management

# 2. What becomes more careful

## “Augmentation is better than automation”

**Grade: Reject as universal rule**

Full automation can be excellent for dangerous, repetitive, high-volume, or machine-speed tasks.

Human augmentation can be better where judgment, accountability, relationship, improvisation, skill maintenance, or rights matter.

Many systems will mix both.

The Human Scale question becomes:

**Which human capabilities still need to exist when the system fails, is unavailable, becomes unaffordable, or encounters an unfamiliar case?**

## “Human in the loop” protects agency

**Grade: Design claim requiring operational definition**

A reviewer is not meaningful oversight if they lack:

- the underlying evidence;
- time to review;
- authority to override;
- competence to detect failure;
- an alternative process;
- protection from retaliation for disagreement;
- a way to document and escalate recurring errors.

Human Scale should speak about **effective review and override**, not a decorative human loop.

## “AI causes deskilling”

**Grade: Plausible, domain specific, not settled generally**

Tools can reduce practice in some skills while creating new skills and making other expertise more accessible.

Calculators changed arithmetic practice. GPS changed navigation. Search changed recall strategies. Coding assistants may change programming. Clinical decision support may change diagnostic behavior.

The correct research question is:

- what capability declines;
- whether that capability is still operationally necessary;
- whether new capability replaces it;
- whether users can recover the skill if automation fails;
- whether the tool improves real outcomes overall.

## “Local or open AI protects freedom”

**Grade: Mechanism with tradeoffs**

Local models can improve privacy, offline resilience, latency, and user control.

Cloud models can provide stronger capability, updates, security operations, collaboration, and lower device requirements.

Open models can increase inspection, adaptation, competition, and research while also reducing centralized control over misuse and shifting security/support burdens.

Proprietary systems can fund safety engineering and support while increasing lock-in and opacity.

Human Scale should evaluate actual exit, portability, privacy, capability, cost, security, governance, and resilience rather than the label.

## “AI should give people time back”

**Grade: Value judgment + political economy**

AI can reduce task time. Nothing in the technology decides whether the gain becomes:

- more output;
- lower prices;
- higher wages;
- profit;
- taxes;
- ownership returns;
- shorter hours;
- new services;
- unemployment;
- or some combination.

Human Scale chooses human time as one legitimate claimant on productivity gains. That is a political value, not a technical forecast.

# 3. Reliability must be compared against real alternatives

AI is not required to be perfect to be useful.

Humans, institutions, forms, search engines, call centers, classifiers, and professional judgments also fail.

The proper comparison is:

**AI-assisted system versus the realistic system it replaces or augments.**

Depending on risk, evaluation may need:

- accuracy and calibration;
- false-positive and false-negative costs;
- subgroup performance;
- robustness to changed inputs;
- security and adversarial testing;
- latency and availability;
- user comprehension;
- workflow effects;
- recourse and appeal;
- incident reporting;
- post-deployment monitoring;
- ability to shut down, roll back, or fall back to another process.

A system can be more accurate on average and still be unacceptable if its rare failures are catastrophic or impossible to challenge.

---

# 4. AI companions and simulated relationship

AI systems can provide conversation, practice, coaching, accessibility, language support, entertainment, and companionship.

Human Scale should avoid two unsupported extremes:

- “AI relationships are fake and therefore worthless”;
- “AI companionship can substitute for human relationship without meaningful cost.”

The relevant questions are empirical and ethical:

- does the system reduce or increase unwanted loneliness;
- does it support or displace human relationships;
- does it encourage dependency;
- how does it handle crisis and vulnerability;
- is the user clearly informed that the system is synthetic;
- does the business model benefit from escalating emotional dependency;
- what data is collected from intimate conversation;
- can the user leave with their data and without punitive manipulation?

This should become its own future evidence track.

---

# 5. Recommended Chapter 17 changes

1. **Keep risk-specific governance strongly.** AI is not one category of use.
2. **Replace augmentation preference with capability analysis.** Automate freely where lost human capability is not needed; preserve it where failure, rights, judgment, or resilience require it.
3. **Define meaningful human review operationally.** Information + time + competence + authority + alternative + appeal.
4. **Separate exposure, task automation, job transformation, and job loss.** They are not synonyms.
5. **Treat deskilling as a domain-specific hypothesis.** Measure retained and newly created capability.
6. **Demote local/open/proprietary/centralized from moral labels to architecture choices.**
7. **Compare AI to realistic alternatives, not perfection.**
8. **Keep distribution of productivity gains explicitly political.**
9. **Make rollback, fallback, monitoring, and incident learning part of agency.**
10. **Create a separate research track for AI companionship and relational dependence.**

---

# 6. What would change our minds?

Human Scale should favor full automation more strongly where real-world evaluations show it outperforms human or augmented alternatives on outcomes, safety, equity, reliability, cost, and user preference while preserving adequate fallback.

It should reduce concern about deskilling where users retain necessary competence or where the displaced skill no longer provides meaningful resilience or value.

It should reduce preference for open/local systems where they repeatedly produce worse security, reliability, accessibility, support, or cost without meaningful gains in agency.

It should strengthen labor protections or transition systems where actual data show concentrated displacement, wage loss, loss of bargaining power, or unequal distribution of AI gains.

It should revise AI governance when monitoring, incident data, and better evaluations show risks or benefits different from those anticipated.

---

# Result of Audit 17

**Human Scale AI is not defined by keeping humans in every task. It is defined by preserving the human capabilities, rights, accountability, practical exit, and distributional choices that still matter after the task changes.**

The best automation may sometimes remove the human from the loop entirely.

The important question is whether the human remains in control of the life and institutions around the loop.
