# Evidence Audit 08: Governance

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> Power close enough to understand is valuable. Power capable enough to solve the problem is valuable too.

This audit puts Chapter 8 — *Governance: Power Close Enough to Understand* — under pressure.

The chapter blends claims about:

- subsidiarity;
- local knowledge;
- participation;
- institutional capacity;
- bureaucracy;
- administrative burden;
- transparency;
- digital government;
- corruption;
- public-sector technology;
- emergency power;
- public trust;
- rights;
- scale.

Many are real design tensions rather than one-directional laws.

---

# Summary Judgment

**Keep subsidiarity as a design heuristic, not an empirical law. Strengthen the doctrine around a two-part test: capability + accountability.**

The strongest foundations are:

1. Government capacity matters. Rules and rights that cannot be implemented are weak institutions.
2. Administrative burden can impose real time, learning, psychological, and compliance costs on people using public systems.
3. Local decision-makers can possess useful contextual knowledge that distant institutions lack.
4. Local institutions can also be captured by concentrated interests, lack expertise, reproduce local prejudice, or be unable to handle cross-boundary problems.
5. Larger institutions can pool expertise, fiscal capacity, infrastructure, risk, data, and rights enforcement.
6. Larger institutions can also become remote, slow, opaque, standardized beyond usefulness, and difficult for ordinary people to challenge.
7. Transparency helps only when information is accessible, interpretable, timely, and connected to accountability.
8. Digital government can reduce friction while creating new barriers for people without devices, literacy, language access, disability support, stable identity documents, or connectivity.
9. Corruption and arbitrary power damage institutional legitimacy and performance.
10. Emergency powers sometimes need speed and central coordination; they also need scope, review, expiration, and post-event accountability.

The weaker or normative claims are:

- local government is inherently more democratic;
- participation automatically improves decisions;
- more transparency is always better;
- digitization automatically simplifies government;
- one-stop portals necessarily reduce burden;
- elected control is always enough accountability;
- centralization is inherently less humane;
- decentralization inherently increases resilience;
- citizens should participate constantly in governance;
- public trust is always desirable regardless of institutional performance.

# Claim Register

## Claim 1 — State capacity matters

**Grade: Strong institutional principle**

A government can formally promise rights, services, safety, infrastructure, and regulation while lacking the ability to deliver them.

Capacity includes combinations of:

- competent staff;
- fiscal resources;
- data;
- legal authority;
- procurement;
- operational systems;
- professional norms;
- enforcement;
- maintenance;
- technology;
- institutional memory.

Human Scale therefore rejects a version of small government that measures success only by organizational size.

A small institution that cannot perform its legitimate function can create more friction and arbitrary power than a larger competent one.

Official reference:
- World Bank Worldwide Governance Indicators: https://www.worldbank.org/en/publication/worldwide-governance-indicators

---

## Claim 2 — Administrative burden is a real human cost

**Grade: Strong research concept; magnitude is process-specific**

People interacting with government may face:

- learning costs: discovering a program, rule, office, deadline, or eligibility requirement;
- compliance costs: forms, documentation, appointments, travel, repeated verification;
- psychological costs: stigma, uncertainty, fear of mistakes, adversarial procedures.

Not every burden is unnecessary.

Identity verification, fraud controls, due process, safety review, environmental assessment, and recordkeeping can serve legitimate purposes.

**Doctrine implication:** Measure the burden and the function separately.

Ask:

> Which step protects something important, and which step exists because the system is poorly integrated or unable to trust its own information?

---

## Claim 3 — Local knowledge can improve decisions

**Grade: Plausible and often strong in context; not universal**

Local residents, workers, operators, clinicians, teachers, engineers, and frontline staff may know details that central decision-makers cannot easily observe.

This can matter for:

- land use;
- street design;
- service delivery;
- school operations;
- public safety;
- ecosystem management;
- disaster response;
- workplace rules.

But local knowledge can be:

- incomplete;
- biased;
- unrepresentative;
- dominated by people with time and status;
- resistant to newcomers or broader evidence.

**Doctrine implication:** Build channels for local information without treating local preference as automatically correct.

---

## Claim 4 — Participation can improve legitimacy and information, and can be captured

**Grade: Context-dependent**

Public meetings, advisory groups, participatory budgeting, consultations, juries, elections, surveys, and citizen panels can:

- reveal local knowledge;
- expose tradeoffs;
- increase legitimacy;
- identify implementation problems;
- help institutions discover values they cannot infer from technical analysis.

They can also overrepresent:

- homeowners;
- organized groups;
- retirees;
- professionals;
- people with flexible schedules;
- people fluent in institutional language.

Low participation does not necessarily mean public consent.

High participation does not necessarily mean representative participation.

**Doctrine implication:** Evaluate who participates, who cannot, and what authority the process actually has.

---

## Claim 5 — Subsidiarity is a heuristic, not a law

**Grade: Political design principle**

The Human Scale version of subsidiarity is:

> place a decision at the smallest scale that can solve the problem competently while protecting rights and accounting for cross-boundary effects.

This formulation already contains reasons to scale up:

- pollution crosses borders;
- epidemics cross borders;
- transmission grids cross borders;
- national defense crosses borders;
- civil rights may need protection from local majorities;
- research and infrastructure benefit from pooled resources;
- insurance works through risk pooling;
- financial systems are interconnected.

**Doctrine implication:** “Local where possible” should never mean “local regardless of problem structure.”

---

## Claim 6 — Large institutions can create valuable professional capacity

**Grade: Strong structural mechanism**

Scale can support:

- specialized expertise;
- laboratories;
- legal teams;
- cybersecurity;
- procurement leverage;
- large infrastructure;
- data systems;
- continuity;
- standards;
- surge capacity;
- risk pooling.

Scale can also create layers of management, slow feedback, diffuse responsibility, and standardized rules that fit no one well.

**Doctrine implication:** Ask what capability is being purchased by scale and whether the accountability system scales with it.

---

## Claim 7 — Transparency is not the same as accountability

**Grade: Strong corrective principle**

Publishing thousands of pages, datasets, meeting packets, legal notices, or dashboards can technically make information public while leaving ordinary people unable to answer:

- What happened?
- Who decided?
- Why?
- Who benefits?
- What did it cost?
- What can I do next?

Good transparency needs:

- discoverability;
- understandable formats;
- timely disclosure;
- source data where appropriate;
- clear responsibility;
- routes to challenge or act.

**Doctrine implication:** Measure whether transparency changes legibility and accountability rather than page count.

---

## Claim 8 — Digital government can reduce burden and create a digital gate

**Grade: Strong structural tradeoff**

Digital services can eliminate:

- travel;
- paper forms;
- repeated data entry;
- office-hour constraints;
- manual processing;
- status uncertainty.

They can create new problems through:

- inaccessible interfaces;
- account lockout;
- identity verification failures;
- poor mobile support;
- language barriers;
- unstable internet;
- lack of human help;
- inaccessible PDFs;
- systems that cannot share information.

**Doctrine implication:** Digital-first should not become digital-only for essential services without workable alternatives.

Useful public-sector reference:
- OECD public governance: https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/public-governance.html

---

## Claim 9 — Government metrics can improve performance and distort it

**Grade: Strong organizational principle**

What institutions measure can influence behavior.

Metrics can reveal:

- wait times;
- error rates;
- cost;
- safety;
- outcomes;
- backlog;
- service quality.

Metrics can also be gamed or substitute for the underlying mission.

Examples:

- closing tickets without solving problems;
- reducing wait time by reducing access;
- increasing test scores by narrowing curriculum;
- maximizing arrests rather than safety;
- processing applications quickly but incorrectly.

**Doctrine implication:** Use multiple measures and preserve room for qualitative judgment and audits.

---

## Claim 10 — Corruption and arbitrary enforcement undermine capability and legitimacy

**Grade: Strong institutional principle**

Corruption can redirect public resources, distort procurement, create unequal enforcement, discourage investment, and make formal rules less credible.

Anti-corruption systems can include:

- transparent procurement;
- conflict-of-interest rules;
- audits;
- asset disclosure where appropriate;
- independent investigation;
- whistleblower protection;
- competitive processes;
- records;
- judicial review.

These systems can themselves become burdensome or politicized.

**Doctrine implication:** Anti-corruption controls should be designed around specific risks rather than universal distrust of every public servant.

---

## Claim 11 — Public trust should be calibrated, not maximized blindly

**Grade: Corrective principle**

High trust in a corrupt institution is not good.

Low trust in a competent institution can also damage cooperation and legitimacy.

Human Scale therefore values **trustworthiness** before trust.

Trustworthy institutions tend to require combinations of:

- competence;
- honesty;
- consistency;
- fairness;
- explanation;
- accountability;
- correction.

The goal is not public relations that manufactures confidence.

It is performance worthy of confidence.

---

## Claim 12 — Emergency powers can be necessary and dangerous

**Grade: Structural governance tension**

Disasters, epidemics, war, fires, infrastructure failures, and financial crises may require rapid coordination and temporary concentration of authority.

Slow ordinary procedure may be inadequate.

Emergency power can also persist, expand, or be abused.

**Doctrine implication:** Emergency authority should include as much as feasible:

- defined triggers;
- scope;
- time limits;
- reporting;
- independent review;
- legislative/judicial checks appropriate to the system;
- after-action evaluation;
- termination mechanisms.

Resilience includes the ability to return to ordinary governance.

---

## Claim 13 — AI can reduce bureaucracy and obscure responsibility

**Grade: Emerging design issue**

AI may help:

- answer questions;
- translate;
- summarize rules;
- prefill forms;
- find errors;
- triage cases;
- detect anomalies;
- make public records searchable.

It can also:

- hallucinate rules;
- create inconsistent decisions;
- hide the responsible official;
- amplify bias;
- make appeal difficult;
- shift people into automated loops with no human resolution.

**Doctrine implication:** Public AI should make government more legible, not create a new layer of unchallengeable pseudo-authority.

For consequential decisions, meaningful human authority, auditability, notice, and appeal matter.

Official technical reference:
- NIST AI Risk Management Framework: https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework

# Recommended Chapter Changes

## Change 1 — Core governance rule

Refine subsidiarity into:

> **Put power at the smallest scale that can solve the problem competently, protect rights, internalize important spillovers, and remain accountable.**

## Change 2 — Capability + accountability

Make this the two-axis governance test:

- **Capability:** Can the institution actually do the job?
- **Accountability:** Can affected people understand, challenge, replace, review, or constrain the institution appropriately?

A system weak on either axis should be redesigned.

## Change 3 — Participation

Use:

> Participation is a source of information and legitimacy, not a guarantee of representativeness or technical correctness.

Design participation for:

- multiple times;
- remote/in-person options;
- language access;
- disability access;
- childcare where relevant;
- plain-language materials;
- representative sampling where useful.

## Change 4 — Administrative burden

Add a **burden budget** to policy design.

For each new rule or service, estimate:

- citizen time;
- forms;
- documents;
- travel;
- logins;
- waiting;
- appeals;
- recurring reporting.

Then ask which steps are essential.

## Change 5 — Digital public infrastructure

Prefer reusable components where appropriate:

- identity;
- payments;
- notifications;
- document exchange;
- status tracking;
- accessibility;
- open standards;
- APIs;

without forcing one monolithic application to own every public interaction.

## Change 6 — Transparency

Move from data dumping to **answerable questions**:

- who decided;
- under what authority;
- what evidence;
- how much it cost;
- what happened;
- how to appeal or participate.

# What Chapter 8 Gets Right

The chapter is strongest when it says:

- power should be understandable;
- local knowledge matters;
- rights can require larger institutions;
- government needs actual capacity;
- bureaucracy should be simplified for citizens;
- digital tools should make institutions legible;
- emergency powers need limits;
- public participation should produce real influence;
- corruption and opaque responsibility matter.

It is weakest wherever “smallest scale” can be mistaken for “best scale regardless of capacity or capture.”

---

# What Would Change Our Minds?

Human Scale should revise favored governance principles if evidence shows that:

- local participation systematically worsens outcomes because capture dominates local knowledge;
- centralized professional systems reliably outperform local discretion without meaningful accountability costs;
- burden-reduction efforts materially increase fraud, safety failures, inequity, or erroneous payments;
- digital-first systems consistently exclude vulnerable populations despite accessibility efforts;
- public transparency produces privacy/security harm or information overload without accountability benefit;
- participatory processes consume large amounts of citizen time while having little influence;
- emergency-power safeguards prevent necessary response more than they prevent abuse;
- AI assistance measurably improves accuracy, consistency, accessibility, and appeal rather than obscuring responsibility.

Human Scale should be willing to strengthen centralized institutions where centralization clearly buys capability and rights protection.

---

# Result of Audit 08

**Chapter 8 survives, but subsidiarity becomes capability + accountability rather than a presumption that nearer power is better power.**

The stronger principle is:

> Put public power at the smallest scale that can solve the problem competently, protect rights, internalize important spillovers, and remain accountable. Build larger institutions when scale buys real capability, and build stronger review when scale makes power harder to see.

Human Scale does not want weak government.

It wants government strong enough to perform legitimate tasks and constrained enough to remain answerable to human beings.
