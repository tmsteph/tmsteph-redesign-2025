Claim-by-claim pressure test

Food Under Pressure

Keep nourishment, safety, resilience, culture, and stewardship. Stop treating local, natural, or homemade as automatic proof of quality.

Locality is a variable, not a virtue.

Summary judgment

Chapter 9 survives, but its evaluation criteria become more concrete. A humane food system should feed people safely, reliably, affordably, and well while preserving long-term ecological capacity and meaningful agency.

The correction: proximity to production can support resilience, culture, and participation, but it is not a shortcut around nutrition, safety, tradeoffs, or measurement.

What survives strongly

Strong public-health consensus

Diet quality matters.

WHO describes healthy diets as adequate, balanced, moderate, diverse, and safe. Many dietary patterns can satisfy those principles; Human Scale does not need a universal diet ideology.

WHO Healthy diet (2026) ↗

Policy evidence

The food environment changes what is easy to eat.

Price, availability, marketing, schools, workplaces, retail, delivery, kitchens, and public procurement shape choices. This supports changing defaults without turning every meal into regulation.

WHO urban food environments (2026) ↗ · WHO fiscal guidance ↗

Foundational

Food safety outranks romantic directness.

Microbial and chemical hazards can appear anywhere in a supply chain. Trustworthy standards, testing, sanitation, and expertise remain necessary even in local systems.

WHO food safety guidance ↗

Systems evidence

Trade can increase resilience.

FAO identifies diverse international sourcing as one strategy for buffering food systems against shocks. The doctrine's strongest formula is local capability plus wider exchange.

FAO on trade and resilience ↗

Measurement evidence

Food waste is large enough to measure seriously.

UNEP documents substantial household, food-service, and retail waste and provides a framework for measurement and reduction.

What becomes conditional

Reject blanket claim

Local food is not automatically better.

Distance alone does not decide nutrition, water use, emissions, labor conditions, price, biodiversity impact, or resilience. Evaluate the full system.

Capability, not obligation

Cooking and gardening can be valuable without becoming duties.

Skills and access can increase flexibility and learning. Disability, time, kitchen access, water, soil safety, household size, and convenience options change the tradeoff.

Model locally

Regional processing may add resilience.

Cold storage, mills, kitchens, distribution, and other middle infrastructure can remove bottlenecks, but duplicated capacity can also raise costs. Find the actual single points of failure.

Layered transparency

Understandable food still needs expert institutions.

Labels and traceability can help consumers, but modern food chemistry, safety, genetics, and logistics cannot be personally audited by every eater.

Judge farming by functions

Human Scale should avoid treating labels such as local, organic, regenerative, traditional, or industrial as conclusions. Ask what the practice does to yield, nutrition, soil, water, habitat, emissions, pests, resilience, labor, animal welfare, affordability, and long-term productive capacity.

Different crops, soils, climates, technologies, and communities can produce different correct answers.

Recommended Chapter 9 changes

Safety + affordability: move them to the top of the food-system test.
Locality: demote it from virtue to measurable mechanism.
Trade: keep it explicitly as a source of diversity and resilience.
Cooking + growing: preserve access and competence without universal obligation.
Agriculture: replace identity labels with outcome metrics.
Transparency: combine understandable information with competent institutions.
Waste: measure and reduce it without turning food into moral anxiety.

What would change our minds?

Reduce emphasis on regional capacity if local models repeatedly cost more, fail more often, or create greater ecological damage without meaningful resilience benefits. Increase it where real bottlenecks repeatedly create shortages or where strategic redundancy measurably improves recovery.

Strengthen specific food-environment policies when high-quality evaluations show durable improvements in nutrition, health, equity, or affordability without large unintended burdens.

Result of Audit 09

A humane food system is safe, nourishing, affordable, ecologically durable, resilient, and understandable enough for meaningful agency.

Locality, cooking, gardening, trade, processing, and technology are tools to combine — not identities to defend.
