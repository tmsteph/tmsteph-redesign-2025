# Evidence Audit 09 — Food Under Pressure

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> A food system should be judged first by whether it feeds people safely and well. Locality, naturalness, tradition, and visibility are secondary mechanisms, not automatic virtues.

Chapter 9 argues for nourishing food, cooking competence, ecological stewardship, regional capacity, wider trade, worker dignity, animal welfare, and less waste.

Most of that direction survives. Several claims need sharper boundaries.

The strongest correction is this:

**Human Scale should stop treating proximity to production as a proxy for a good food system.**

A distant food can be nutritious, safe, affordable, low-impact, and resilient. A local food can be expensive, unsafe, ecologically inappropriate, exploitative, or resource-intensive. Local and regional capacity can matter for resilience, culture, and participation, but those benefits must be measured.

---

# 1. What survives strongly

## Diet quality matters for health

**Grade: Strong evidence / public-health consensus**

WHO guidance treats healthy diets as diverse, adequate, balanced, moderate, and safe. Healthy diets can take many cultural forms; there is no single mandatory Human Scale diet.

WHO's 2026 guidance emphasizes minimally processed and unprocessed foods, fruits, vegetables, pulses, whole grains, varied protein sources, moderation of free sugars, sodium, unhealthy fats, and the need to adapt the exact diet to age, activity, culture, availability, and health needs.

Human Scale should therefore keep a broad food-quality principle while rejecting universal ancestral, vegan, carnivore, low-fat, low-carb, or purity rules.

Source:
- WHO, Healthy diet (2026): https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet

## Food environments shape choices

**Grade: Strong policy evidence, variable effect sizes**

Food choice is not only willpower. Price, availability, marketing, school food, workplace food, neighborhood retail, delivery systems, time, transportation, kitchens, and product formulation all change what is easy to eat.

WHO now explicitly treats food environments as a public-health target and publishes guidance on school food, fiscal measures, marketing, labeling, procurement, and urban food environments.

This supports the chapter's claim that systems can make healthier defaults easier without dictating every meal.

Sources:
- WHO, Healthy diet (2026): https://www.who.int/en/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet
- WHO, Supporting healthy diets and safe foods in urban food environments (2026): https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/B09723
- WHO, Fiscal policies to promote healthy diets (2024): https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240091016/

## Food safety is foundational

**Grade: Strong evidence / institutional consensus**

A food system is not humane if it is culturally meaningful but unsafe. Microbial, chemical, and physical hazards can arise at production, processing, transport, retail, food service, and home preparation.

Human Scale should explicitly rank food safety above romantic ideas of directness or minimal processing when those goals conflict.

Source:
- WHO, Guidance on safe and healthy food: https://www.who.int/tools/compendium-on-health-and-environment/safe-and-healthy-food

## Food waste is large enough to justify systematic measurement

**Grade: Strong measurement evidence**

UNEP's Food Waste Index documents large amounts of waste across households, food service, and retail. The evidence supports measuring waste and testing prevention, redistribution, storage, portioning, and circular uses.

It does not support treating every discarded gram as moral failure. Some waste is unavoidable, safety margins matter, and measurement should separate edible from inedible material where useful.

Source:
- UNEP, Food Waste Index Report 2024: https://www.unep.org/resources/publication/food-waste-index-report-2024

## Trade can increase resilience

**Grade: Strong systems argument / context dependent**

FAO explicitly identifies diversified international sourcing as one way to buffer national agrifood systems against shocks and stresses. Trade can spread risk across climates and harvests, increase dietary diversity, and prevent local failure from becoming local scarcity.

This strongly supports the existing Human Scale formula:

**local capability plus wider exchange.**

Source:
- FAO, International trade and the resilience of national agrifood systems (2021): https://www.fao.org/agrifood-economics/publications/detail/en/c/1457060/

# 2. What becomes more conditional

## “Local food is better”

**Grade: Reject as blanket claim**

Distance alone does not determine nutrition, emissions, water use, biodiversity impact, labor conditions, resilience, or cost.

A Human Scale evaluation should ask instead:

- what is being produced;
- where the climate fits the crop;
- how much water, fertilizer, land, energy, and refrigeration it requires;
- how it is transported;
- how vulnerable the supply chain is;
- what local capacity is strategically useful;
- whether direct relationships create benefits people actually use.

Locality is one variable.

## “Growing food reconnects people to food”

**Grade: Value + plausible educational mechanism**

Gardening can teach biological processes, seasonality, skill, and stewardship. It can also be inaccessible, time-consuming, contaminated, water-intensive, frustrating, or irrelevant to a person's food security.

Human Scale should promote optional access to growing and agricultural learning rather than implying everyone needs to garden.

## “Cooking competence protects freedom”

**Grade: Plausible mechanism, under-specified**

Cooking can reduce dependence on prepared food, improve flexibility, save money in some circumstances, and transmit culture. But disability, time scarcity, energy costs, household size, kitchen access, caregiving, and available prepared foods change the tradeoff.

The better claim is:

**people should have access to food skills and facilities, while convenience remains legitimate.**

## “Regional processing capacity creates resilience”

**Grade: Working hypothesis that should be modeled locally**

Cold storage, mills, kitchens, slaughter capacity, distribution hubs, and processing can reduce single-point dependence in some regions.

But duplicating infrastructure can also raise costs, reduce utilization, create regulatory burden, and lose economies of scale.

The correct question is not “how local can we make it?” but:

**which bottlenecks become dangerous when they are too concentrated?**

## “Food should remain understandable”

**Grade: Value judgment with practical limits**

Transparency, labeling, and traceability can support agency. Modern food safety, chemistry, genetics, logistics, and processing are too complex for every consumer to personally verify.

Human Scale therefore needs trustworthy institutions, standards, testing, and expert systems in addition to understandable labels and direct relationships.

---

# 3. Agriculture and ecology

Agriculture is inseparable from soil, water, climate, nutrients, habitat, pests, genetics, labor, markets, and land competition.

The chapter should avoid one-word solution categories such as “regenerative,” “organic,” “industrial,” or “local” as though the label decides outcomes.

Human Scale should judge practices by measurable functions:

- yield and food quality;
- soil erosion and soil function;
- water use and water pollution;
- nutrient losses;
- pesticide risk;
- greenhouse-gas emissions;
- biodiversity and habitat;
- animal welfare where relevant;
- worker safety;
- affordability;
- resilience to drought, flood, disease, pests, and trade disruption;
- long-term productive capacity.

Different crops, climates, soils, and technologies will produce different answers.

---

# 4. Workers and animals

## Food-system labor

**Grade: Value judgment informed by occupational evidence**

Food affordability should not depend on hidden coercion or preventable injury. Farm, processing, logistics, retail, restaurant, and domestic food work all carry different risks.

Human Scale should support credible safety standards, lawful treatment, sanitation, heat protection, and worker voice while acknowledging that compliance design can affect small producers differently from large firms.

## Animal welfare

**Grade: Moral commitment + empirical measurement problem**

Animal welfare is not settled by whether an animal system is small or large, local or distant. Welfare must be assessed through species-appropriate conditions, health, behavior, transport, handling, slaughter, stocking, injury, disease, and environmental conditions.

Human Scale can take welfare seriously without requiring one universal ethical conclusion about eating animals.

---

# 5. Recommended Chapter 9 changes

1. **Move safety and affordability higher.** A food system must first feed people safely, reliably, and affordably.
2. **Demote locality from virtue to variable.** Keep regional capacity where it produces resilience, culture, participation, or useful redundancy.
3. **Keep trade explicitly.** Diverse external sourcing can increase resilience.
4. **Treat gardening and cooking as capabilities, not obligations.** Access and competence matter; universal participation does not.
5. **Replace farming labels with outcome measures.** Soil, water, habitat, yield, emissions, labor, welfare, and affordability matter more than identity categories.
6. **Treat transparency as layered.** Consumers need understandable information, while institutions still need expert testing and enforcement.
7. **Keep waste measurable and non-moralizing.** Prevention first, then safe redistribution or cycling where practical.

---

# 6. What would change our minds?

Human Scale should reduce emphasis on local/regional capacity if robust local models repeatedly show higher cost, lower reliability, greater environmental damage, or no meaningful resilience benefit compared with diversified wider supply.

It should increase emphasis on regional capacity when particular processing or distribution bottlenecks repeatedly create severe shortages or when local redundancy measurably improves recovery from shocks.

It should weaken cooking and gardening recommendations if better evidence shows these activities provide little capability, health, educational, or resilience benefit under realistic conditions.

It should strengthen specific food-environment policies when high-quality evaluations show durable improvements in diet, health, equity, or affordability without large unintended burdens.

---

# Result of Audit 09

**A humane food system is safe, nourishing, affordable, ecologically durable, resilient, and understandable enough for meaningful agency. Locality, cooking, gardening, trade, processing, and technology are tools to combine—not identities to defend.**

Human Scale keeps the relationship between body, land, culture, labor, and food.

It gives up the idea that closeness automatically makes that relationship good.
