# Evidence Audit 09: Food

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> Food is biology, culture, agriculture, logistics, labor, ecology, pleasure, risk, and care. No single food label captures all of those at once.

This audit puts Chapter 9 — *Food: Nourishment, Culture & Living Systems* — under pressure.

The chapter blends claims about:

- diet quality;
- cooking;
- whole ingredients;
- gardens;
- local food;
- regional capacity;
- trade;
- soil;
- regenerative agriculture;
- organic agriculture;
- livestock;
- food workers;
- food waste;
- resilience;
- culture;
- school meals;
- affordability.

Some claims are well supported.

Others are values or implementation hypotheses that need to remain conditional.

---

# Summary Judgment

**Keep nourishment, affordability, food competence, food safety, ecological stewardship, and resilience at the center. Stop treating local, organic, regenerative, home-cooked, plant-based, pasture-based, or minimally processed as single-word guarantees of health or sustainability.**

The strongest foundations are:

1. Diet quality matters for health.
2. Food safety matters independently of whether food is local, natural, organic, or homemade.
3. Agriculture depends on functioning soil, water, climate, nutrients, pollination, labor, energy, and infrastructure.
4. Different foods and production systems have very different environmental footprints.
5. Food loss and waste consume land, water, energy, money, and labor without nourishing people.
6. Affordability and access strongly constrain what people can realistically eat.
7. Trade can increase variety and buffer local crop failures while also creating exposure to distant disruptions and concentrated supply chains.
8. Local and regional capacity can increase some forms of resilience, but locality alone does not prove lower emissions, better nutrition, or greater food security.
9. Cooking competence can expand agency and options; it is not a moral test or a guarantee of a healthy diet.
10. Agricultural practices should be judged by measurable outcomes rather than movement labels.

The weaker or normative claims are:

- local food is inherently more sustainable;
- organic food is inherently healthier or more ecological across all outcomes;
- regenerative agriculture has one standardized definition or universally superior practice set;
- home cooking automatically produces a healthy diet;
- every household should grow food;
- food forests are generally appropriate urban infrastructure;
- livestock is either inherently good or inherently incompatible with sustainable food systems;
- one universal human diet can be inferred from evolutionary history;
- food miles alone adequately measure environmental impact.

# Claim Register

## Claim 1 — Diet quality matters

**Grade: Strong public-health consensus**

The World Health Organization describes healthy diets as diverse, balanced, and adequate, with emphasis on foods such as fruits, vegetables, pulses, nuts, and whole grains while limiting free sugars, sodium, industrial trans fats, and excessive saturated fat.

The exact healthy pattern can vary by:

- culture;
- age;
- activity;
- health conditions;
- pregnancy;
- allergies;
- food availability;
- economics.

Human Scale should therefore resist one universal menu.

Official reference:
https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet

---

## Claim 2 — Food safety is part of healthy food

**Grade: Strong**

Food can be nutritionally desirable and still be unsafe because of:

- pathogens;
- toxins;
- contamination;
- improper storage;
- unsafe water;
- poor temperature control;
- cross-contamination;
- unsafe preservation.

“Natural,” “local,” “raw,” “homemade,” or “organic” do not remove microbiological or chemical risk.

Human Scale should keep competence and food safety together.

Official reference:
https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/food-safety

---

## Claim 3 — Cooking expands agency but does not guarantee diet quality

**Grade: Value + plausible behavioral mechanism**

Cooking can give people control over:

- ingredients;
- portioning;
- cost;
- leftovers;
- cultural food;
- family participation;
- adaptation to dietary needs.

A home-cooked meal can still be nutritionally poor.

A commercially prepared meal can be nutritionally excellent.

Some people lack:

- time;
- kitchens;
- physical ability;
- equipment;
- refrigeration;
- money;
- energy;
- interest.

**Doctrine implication:** Treat cooking as useful competence and cultural participation, not moral superiority.

---

## Claim 4 — “Processed” is too broad by itself

**Grade: Strong conceptual correction**

Processing includes:

- freezing;
- pasteurization;
- fermentation;
- milling;
- canning;
- drying;
- fortification;
- cooking;
- industrial formulation.

Many forms of processing improve:

- safety;
- shelf life;
- convenience;
- digestibility;
- nutrient availability;
- seasonal resilience.

Other formulations can make it easier to consume high levels of sodium, free sugars, refined starches, or saturated fat.

Human Scale should discuss actual composition, purpose, and dietary pattern rather than treating “processed” as one biological category.

Research on ultra-processed-food classifications is important and growing, but observational associations should not be converted into a claim that every industrially formulated food has the same effect.

---

## Claim 5 — Food production often matters more than food miles for environmental impact

**Grade: Strong lifecycle insight; product-specific**

Lifecycle research shows that environmental impacts differ greatly among foods and production systems, and production often dominates transport for many products.

Air freight can be important for some foods.

Cold chains, storage, packaging, land-use change, fertilizer, feed, methane, energy, and yield can matter more than physical distance alone.

**Doctrine implication:** Local food can have social and resilience value without claiming that fewer miles automatically means lower environmental impact.

Primary research:
Poore & Nemecek, *Science* (2018), DOI: https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaq0216

---

## Claim 6 — Local and regional food capacity can add resilience, and trade can add resilience

**Grade: Structural tradeoff**

Regional capacity can reduce dependence on some long supply chains and preserve:

- farming knowledge;
- processing;
- storage;
- distribution;
- local relationships;
- emergency options.

Trade can reduce dependence on one local climate, crop, harvest, or disaster.

A drought-resistant food system may need imports.

A globally optimized food system can still become fragile when production or processing is highly concentrated.

**Doctrine implication:** Prefer **diversified supply** rather than romantic self-sufficiency.

---

## Claim 7 — Soil function matters, but “soil health” needs measurable definitions

**Grade: Strong ecological/agronomic foundation + variable metrics**

Soils influence:

- water infiltration and storage;
- nutrient cycling;
- rooting;
- erosion;
- carbon;
- microbial and biological processes;
- crop productivity.

Different soils and production systems have different constraints.

Useful measures can include combinations of:

- organic matter/carbon;
- aggregate stability;
- erosion;
- nutrient status;
- compaction;
- infiltration;
- biological indicators;
- yield;
- input requirements.

**Doctrine implication:** Keep soil stewardship central while demanding outcomes rather than relying on branding.

---

## Claim 8 — “Regenerative agriculture” is an umbrella, not one standardized treatment

**Grade: Working framework / heterogeneous practices**

Regenerative programs may include combinations of:

- cover crops;
- reduced tillage;
- crop rotation;
- integrated livestock;
- perennial crops;
- compost;
- agroforestry;
- reduced bare soil;
- biodiversity practices.

Outcomes depend on:

- climate;
- crop;
- soil;
- baseline;
- fertilizer strategy;
- water;
- grazing pressure;
- measurement horizon.

Some practices can improve some outcomes while worsening others.

**Doctrine implication:** Evaluate soil, water, biodiversity, emissions, yield, labor, farmer income, and resilience directly.

---

## Claim 9 — Organic agriculture creates real benefits and tradeoffs

**Grade: Context-dependent**

Organic standards can reduce or prohibit particular synthetic inputs and encourage certain management practices.

Possible benefits and tradeoffs vary across:

- pesticide exposure;
- biodiversity;
- soil management;
- yield;
- land requirement;
- nutrient losses;
- price;
- labor;
- animal systems.

Human Scale should not reduce sustainability to the presence or absence of an organic label.

Labels can remain useful information.

They are not complete system assessments.

---

## Claim 10 — Livestock impacts vary, with important category differences

**Grade: Strong general principle / highly system-specific**

Animal agriculture produces:

- food;
- livelihoods;
- manure;
- traction in some systems;
- use of grasslands or byproducts humans cannot eat.

It can also create substantial:

- methane;
- land demand;
- feed demand;
- water use;
- nutrient pollution;
- animal-welfare concerns.

Ruminant systems and monogastric systems differ.

Pasture, feedlot, mixed-crop livestock, dairy, eggs, poultry, aquaculture, and fisheries differ.

**Doctrine implication:** Avoid both “animals are inherently sustainable” and “all animal agriculture is one environmental category.”

Animal welfare remains a value even where environmental outcomes are acceptable.

---

## Claim 11 — Gardens can create benefits and should not be universalized

**Grade: Moderate / context-dependent**

Gardens can support:

- access to fresh foods;
- physical activity;
- learning;
- social contact;
- cultural crops;
- ecological knowledge;
- household enjoyment.

They can also require:

- water;
- time;
- land;
- maintenance;
- safe soil;
- pest management;
- physical ability.

A person is not less human-scale because they do not want to garden.

The Human Scale value is **access and opportunity**, not compulsory participation.

---

## Claim 12 — Urban agriculture requires contamination awareness

**Grade: Strong safety correction**

Urban soils can contain contaminants depending on prior land use, building materials, traffic, industry, fill, and other history.

Raised beds, clean soil, barriers, testing, crop choices, hygiene, and site assessment can reduce risk depending on the context.

**Doctrine implication:** Every public garden or food-forest proposal needs a site-safety plan before romantic participation language.

---

## Claim 13 — Food waste is an efficiency, climate, and food-system problem

**Grade: Strong**

Food loss and waste embed wasted:

- land;
- water;
- labor;
- energy;
- fertilizer;
- transport;
- refrigeration;
- money.

Waste occurs at farms, processing, retail, food service, and households for different reasons.

Solutions therefore differ:

- storage;
- cold chains;
- forecasting;
- packaging;
- date labeling;
- donation;
- portioning;
- preservation;
- consumer habits;
- composting after edible-use options.

Official reference:
https://www.fao.org/platform-food-loss-waste/en/

---

## Claim 14 — Food affordability and convenience are part of nutrition design

**Grade: Strong practical constraint**

A diet that is theoretically healthy but too expensive, time-intensive, geographically inaccessible, culturally alien, or physically difficult to prepare will not function as a population strategy.

Human Scale should include:

- price;
- time;
- equipment;
- transport;
- storage;
- disability;
- childcare;
- work schedules;
- cultural fit

inside the food environment.

Convenience is not automatically the enemy.

**Good convenience can be an accessibility technology.**

---

## Claim 15 — School and institutional meals are infrastructure

**Grade: Policy/value + measurable outcomes**

Schools, hospitals, workplaces, prisons, care facilities, and other institutions feed large numbers of people.

Their procurement and menus can affect:

- nutrition;
- cost;
- food waste;
- labor;
- culture;
- local suppliers;
- safety;
- resilience.

Human Scale should evaluate institutional food by outcomes rather than use procurement as symbolic politics.

# Recommended Chapter Changes

## Change 1 — Core thesis

Use:

> A good food system should make nourishing, safe, culturally workable food realistically available while preserving the ecological and economic foundations that produce it.

## Change 2 — Competence

Keep cooking, gardening, preservation, and food knowledge as **capabilities people should have opportunities to learn**, not duties required to count as healthy or virtuous.

## Change 3 — Locality

Replace “more local is better” with:

> retain useful regional production, processing, storage, knowledge, and markets while using wider trade to diversify climate, crop, and supply-chain risk.

## Change 4 — Agriculture

Use outcome metrics across labels:

- nutrition;
- yield;
- farmer income;
- labor;
- soil;
- water;
- emissions;
- biodiversity;
- animal welfare;
- input dependence;
- resilience;
- affordability.

## Change 5 — Convenience

Recognize that safe, nutritious, convenient food can expand agency for:

- caregivers;
- disabled people;
- older adults;
- shift workers;
- people without full kitchens;
- people with limited time.

Human Scale should design **better convenience**, not simply demand more household labor.

## Change 6 — Public growing projects

Every edible-landscape project should include:

- soil/site assessment;
- water plan;
- crop suitability;
- maintenance;
- harvest rules;
- accessibility;
- food safety;
- liability/governance;
- ecological fit;
- actual user demand.

---

# What Chapter 9 Gets Right

The chapter is strongest when it says:

- food is a relationship between body, ecology, labor, culture, and infrastructure;
- one universal diet should not become doctrine;
- cooking and food knowledge can create agency;
- soil and water matter;
- local capacity and global trade can coexist;
- agriculture should regenerate rather than consume its own foundation;
- food workers and farmers matter;
- gardens and food forests should be tested rather than worshiped;
- resilience requires capacity, not aesthetics.

It is weakest wherever natural, local, organic, homemade, or regenerative becomes shorthand for good.

---

# What Would Change Our Minds?

Human Scale should revise favored food policies if stronger evidence shows that:

- local sourcing consistently raises environmental impacts or food prices without meaningful resilience/community benefits;
- garden programs see low sustained participation or create safety and maintenance burdens exceeding benefits;
- regenerative practices reduce yields enough to increase total land pressure without compensating ecological gains;
- organic systems outperform or underperform alternatives consistently on the outcomes that matter in specific crops/regions;
- convenient prepared foods produce equal or better nutrition and access than household-cooking interventions in relevant populations;
- trade restrictions reduce resilience by concentrating production geographically;
- institutional procurement rules raise cost or reduce nutrition without achieving measurable ecological or local-economic benefits.

Human Scale should follow the outcome, not the label.

---

# Result of Audit 09

**Chapter 9 survives, but the food philosophy becomes less aesthetic and more outcome-driven.**

The stronger principle is:

> A Human Scale food system should make safe, nourishing, culturally workable food realistically available; preserve soil, water, biodiversity, farm viability, and useful regional capacity; use trade where it increases resilience or affordability; reduce waste; and give people opportunities for food competence without making household labor a moral requirement.

Local can be good.

Convenient can be good.

Industrial can be good.

Traditional can be good.

The question is what the food actually does — to bodies, workers, households, ecosystems, and the resilience of the system that produced it.
