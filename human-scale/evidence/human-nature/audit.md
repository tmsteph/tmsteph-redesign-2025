# Evidence Audit 01: Human Nature

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> The mismatch lens is useful only if it does not become a machine for turning every preference into biology.

This audit puts Chapter 1 — *Human Nature: The Environment We Were Built For* — under pressure.

The chapter contains several different kinds of statements:

- straightforward biological claims
- broad public-health claims
- evolutionary interpretations
- social-science associations
- design hypotheses
- philosophical values
- political proposals

Those categories should not blur together.

The purpose of this audit is not to defend the chapter. It is to identify which statements deserve confidence, which need narrower language, which are useful hypotheses, and which are simply values the doctrine chooses.

---

# Summary Judgment

**Keep the core mismatch lens, but narrow its language.**

Chapter 1 remains useful if mismatch means:

> a testable possibility that a modern environment interacts badly with recurring human biological, psychological, or social characteristics.

It becomes weak if mismatch means:

> modern life differs from an imagined ancestral norm, therefore modern life is unhealthy.

The audit supports several revisions:

1. Replace vague ancestral claims with specific modern exposures and measurable outcomes.
2. Replace “small communities” as a presumed biological requirement with the better-supported language of **supportive relationships, belonging, and reciprocal social connection**.
3. Replace vague “seasonal rhythms” with **sleep, circadian timing, daylight/darkness, and schedule regularity** when those are the actual claims.
4. Treat “learning through doing” as a **context-dependent educational hypothesis**, not a universal human need.
5. Treat nature contact as **promising/moderate evidence plus a value commitment**, not proof that everyone requires the same dose or type of nature.
6. Separate basic material/ecological requirements from aesthetically desirable contact with the living world.
7. Treat meaning, story, ritual, purpose, status, and low stimulation as distinct claims rather than one scientifically proven package.

---

# Claim Register

## Claim 1 — Human beings require physical movement for health

**Grade: Strong evidence**

This is one of the safest foundations in the doctrine. Regular physical activity has broad physical and mental-health benefits. The World Health Organization publishes evidence-based physical-activity recommendations across the lifespan.

What the evidence supports:

- movement matters for health
- sedentary environments can create health risks
- environments can influence opportunities for movement

What it does **not** prove:

- every person should perform the same exercise
- ancestral movement patterns are automatically optimal
- walking neighborhoods alone solve population health
- deliberate exercise is somehow less authentic than “natural” movement

**Doctrine implication:** Keep movement as a major Human Scale concern, but judge specific interventions empirically.

Primary / official source:
- WHO, *Guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour* (2020): https://www.who.int/publications/b/55518

---

## Claim 2 — Sleep is a biological requirement and institutions can make it harder

**Grade: Strong evidence for sleep requirement; specific schedule interventions are context-dependent**

The doctrine is strongest when it says bodies require sleep and that environmental schedules, light, noise, work, school, caregiving, and technology can affect sleep opportunity.

The phrase “daily rhythms” is useful when it refers to measurable sleep/circadian processes.

The phrase “seasonal rhythms” is too vague to carry the same evidentiary weight.

A current example comes from school start-time research: a 2026 randomized trial found that a one-hour delay in an early-adolescent school schedule produced a 26-minute between-group difference in sleep after six months, lower sleepiness, and improved inhibitory control. But effects of start-time changes vary by context and can attenuate.

**Doctrine implication:** Prefer “sleep, daylight/darkness, and circadian timing” over generalized “seasonal rhythm” language.

Sources:
- Reynaud et al., randomized school-start trial (2026): https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-50892-6
- Dunster et al./Fairfax quasi-experimental start-time study: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29157637/
- South Korea longitudinal quasi-experiment showing attenuation: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38849057/

---

## Claim 3 — Social connection and belonging matter

**Grade: Strong association / strong public-health consensus; mechanisms and optimal forms vary**

The doctrine should keep social connection near its center.

But “face-to-face relationships” is too narrow if stated as the only legitimate form of relationship. Human relationships can be maintained through many media, and digital communication can be valuable, especially where geography, disability, minority status, or circumstance limits local connection.

The better empirical claim is:

> supportive, wanted, sufficiently strong social relationships and a sense of belonging are important to health and well-being.

The Human Scale value judgment can then add:

> communities should create more opportunities for repeated, reciprocal, local relationships because those relationships provide forms of practical support and shared life that remote connection cannot always replace.

**Doctrine implication:** Replace “face-to-face relationships” as a universal need with “supportive relationships, belonging, care, and reciprocal social connection,” while continuing to investigate what local physical proximity uniquely contributes.

Official source:
- CDC, *Social Connection*: https://www.cdc.gov/social-connectedness/about/

---

## Claim 4 — Humans are adapted to one specific ancestral social scale

**Grade: Too broad / revise**

Chapter 1 has sometimes gestured toward “small communities.” Human evolutionary history certainly involved social groups and repeated relationships, but moving from that fact to a single ideal modern group size is not justified by the evidence assembled here.

Small communities can create:

- belonging
- reciprocity
- legibility
- mutual aid

They can also create:

- conformity
- exclusion
- gossip
- local monopoly
- domination
- lack of privacy
- inability to solve large-scale problems

**Doctrine implication:** Do not claim that “small community” is itself a human biological requirement. Keep the political principle already developed elsewhere: **scale is a hypothesis to test, not a moral shortcut.**

---

## Claim 5 — Play is developmentally important

**Grade: Strong clinical/developmental support for children; do not universalize every form of play**

The American Academy of Pediatrics describes developmentally appropriate play as supporting social-emotional, cognitive, language, self-regulatory, and executive-function development.

This strongly supports the childhood chapter's concern about eliminating play from childhood.

It does not establish:

- that all unstructured play is beneficial
- that adult-directed learning is inferior
- that all ages require the same play environments
- that risk should be maximized

**Doctrine implication:** Keep play as a major childhood need/opportunity. For adults, speak more carefully about recreation, exploration, creativity, social play, and leisure rather than assuming the childhood literature transfers unchanged.

Official clinical source:
- American Academy of Pediatrics, *The Power of Play* (2018; reaffirmed 2025): https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2018-2058

---

## Claim 6 — “Learning through doing” is a universal human need

**Grade: Overstated as written; strong evidence exists in narrower educational contexts**

There is substantial evidence that active-learning approaches can outperform traditional lecturing in some domains. A major meta-analysis in undergraduate STEM found higher assessment performance and lower failure rates under active learning.

But this does not prove that every subject, age, task, or learner should always learn by doing.

Learning can require:

- explanation
- listening
- reading
- memorization
- deliberate practice
- observation
- discussion
- imitation
- retrieval
- active problem solving
- projects
- apprenticeship

**Doctrine implication:** Replace “learning through doing” in lists of universal needs with something like **“opportunities to learn, practice, explore, and develop competence.”** Treat pedagogy as context-dependent.

Source:
- Freeman et al., PNAS (2014): https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1319030111

## Claim 7 — Nature contact benefits health and well-being

**Grade: Moderate evidence; causal magnitude and best forms remain uncertain**

The Evidence Atlas already grades this more cautiously than the original doctrine sometimes implies.

The useful empirical claim is:

> contact with green/natural environments is associated with multiple health and well-being outcomes, and some interventions show benefits, but the literature is heterogeneous and many population studies are observational.

The doctrine then makes a separate value commitment:

> ordinary people should have meaningful access to living systems and opportunities for stewardship, not only scenic exposure.

That second statement is political and ethical. It should not be disguised as the inevitable conclusion of nature-exposure studies.

**Doctrine implication:** Keep nature access central, but distinguish **exposure**, **participation**, **stewardship**, and **ecological health** as different claims.

---

## Claim 8 — Contact with the physical world is a universal psychological requirement

**Grade: Working hypothesis / value judgment**

Humans obviously require a functioning material environment: air, water, food, temperature regulation, shelter, and ecological systems are physical necessities.

But a broad psychological claim that everyone requires a specific amount of soil, handcraft, manual work, or tactile experience is not yet established here.

Human Scale strongly values embodied competence and direct participation in material life. That value can stand without pretending it is a settled medical fact.

**Doctrine implication:** Separate **material biological requirements** from the doctrine's preference for embodied competence, craft, repair, gardening, and direct participation.

---

## Claim 9 — Low stimulation / quiet is a universal human need

**Grade: Plausible but underspecified**

Different people vary substantially in desired stimulation, temperament, neurotype, occupation, culture, and sensory needs.

There are strong reasons to study sleep disruption, chronic noise, attention capture, stress, and compulsive digital design. But “periods of low stimulation” is currently too broad to treat as a universal biological prescription.

**Doctrine implication:** Reframe toward **the ability to access rest, sleep, focused attention, and environments not continuously demanding response**. That is more precise and more compatible with individual differences.

---

## Claim 10 — Autonomy and meaningful choice matter

**Grade: Strong theoretical and empirical tradition, but political translation is not automatic**

A large psychological literature treats autonomy or perceived choice/control as important to motivation and well-being. The current audit has not yet completed a full autonomy literature review.

Human Scale should therefore keep autonomy as a core design value while avoiding claims that one political arrangement automatically maximizes it.

A small local institution can be coercive. A large institution can protect freedom. A market can expand exit or create dependency. A public program can expand capability or create bureaucracy.

**Doctrine implication:** Keep agency/autonomy as a core evaluative dimension; test institutional mechanisms separately.

---

## Claim 11 — Meaning, story, ritual, spirituality, and purpose are one scientifically established need

**Grade: Split the bundle**

These are related but not identical.

Evidence can study purpose, religious participation, existential meaning, narrative identity, ritual, meditation, community, and well-being separately.

Human Scale also makes a philosophical judgment that a good society should leave room for people to ask what life is for.

That judgment does not require proving one metaphysical system.

**Doctrine implication:** Public writing should say that humans are meaning-making creatures and that societies should preserve room for philosophical, religious, artistic, contemplative, and existential life. Specific health claims should be evaluated separately.

---

## Claim 12 — Modern environments can outpace biological adaptation

**Grade: Strong as a general possibility; weak as an automatic diagnosis**

Biological evolution operates across generations while technological and institutional conditions can change within years or even months. That makes mismatch possible.

But the fact that something is new does not show that it is harmful.

Humans also have unusually strong behavioral and cultural flexibility. Eyeglasses, writing, vaccines, refrigeration, public sanitation, anesthesia, computers, and many other evolutionarily novel technologies can produce enormous benefits.

**Doctrine implication:** Novelty is a reason to investigate, not a reason to condemn.

A good mismatch argument must identify:

1. the relevant human trait or constraint
2. the modern exposure
3. a measurable outcome
4. plausible mechanisms
5. alternatives and confounders
6. evidence that changing the exposure changes the outcome

# Language Changes Recommended

## Change 1 — Opening description

Current doctrine language has included:

> nature, small communities, physical movement, direct relationships, seasonal rhythms, and meaningful dependence on the living world

Recommended:

> nature, physical movement, sleep and light-dark rhythms, supportive social relationships, and dependence on living ecological systems

Why:
- removes an unsupported ideal group size
- replaces vague seasonal rhythm with more measurable biology
- avoids implying only face-to-face contact counts as relationship

## Change 2 — Recurring-needs list

Instead of treating the following as equivalent scientific facts:

- movement
- sleep and daily rhythms
- face-to-face relationships
- belonging and mutual dependence
- play and exploration
- learning through doing
- autonomy and meaningful choice
- status and recognition
- contribution to a group
- contact with the physical world
- periods of low stimulation
- attachment, care, touch, and affection
- meaning, story, ritual, or purpose
- a functioning ecological environment

Group them by confidence and type.

### Strong / broad foundations
- movement
- sleep
- supportive social connection
- attachment and care during development
- a functioning physical/ecological environment

### Strong in specific contexts, but not one universal prescription
- play in childhood
- learning and practice
- autonomy / agency
- daylight and circadian timing

### Working hypotheses / individual variation important
- particular amounts of nature contact
- local physical proximity versus remote relationships
- low-stimulation periods
- particular forms of embodied craft or manual competence

### Philosophical / value domains
- meaning
- story
- ritual
- spirituality
- purpose
- contribution
- beauty

This does not make the latter unimportant. It makes the claim type honest.

# What Chapter 1 Gets Right

The audit does **not** overturn the chapter's central direction.

It strengthens several parts:

- personal responsibility is incomplete without environmental analysis
- movement and sleep are legitimate design concerns
- social connection and belonging deserve institutional attention
- childhood needs more than academic output
- technology can change the choice environment
- built environments can make healthy behavior easier or harder
- the naturalistic fallacy warning is essential
- experiments are better than ideological certainty
- scale must justify itself

The chapter is strongest when it asks:

> What does this environment reliably make easier or harder for actual human beings?

It is weakest when it says:

> humans evolved around X, therefore society should restore X.

---

# What Would Change Our Minds Further?

We should revise Human Scale more aggressively if stronger evidence shows that:

- many supposed mismatch effects disappear after socioeconomic and selection factors are controlled
- remote relationships provide the same practical and psychological benefits as local relationships across most relevant outcomes
- nature access adds little once movement, income, air quality, and social context are controlled
- deliberately designed high-stimulation digital environments produce durable gains without meaningful attention, sleep, or agency tradeoffs
- highly centralized systems routinely outperform distributed alternatives while remaining equally legible, resilient, and accountable
- specific Human Scale interventions increase burden, exclusion, cost, or risk without producing the intended human benefits

The doctrine should also change when evidence shows a modern environment is **better** than the ancestral analogue.

Mismatch research should find matches too.

---

# Research Backlog for Human Nature

Next evidence modules should examine:

- sleep duration, regularity, circadian timing, and artificial light
- autonomy, control, and well-being
- commuting and time use
- chronic noise and sensory load
- attention capture and persuasive product design
- face-to-face versus digitally mediated relationships
- loneliness versus objective isolation
- touch and affection across age groups and cultures
- purpose and meaning
- craft/manual competence and well-being
- boredom and unstructured time
- adult play and recreation
- gardening and active stewardship versus passive green-space exposure
- evolutionary mismatch as a formal framework across medicine and psychology
- neurodiversity and variation in sensory/social needs

# Result of Audit 01

**Chapter 1 survives, but it becomes narrower and stronger.**

The core claim is no longer:

> humans evolved in one kind of environment, so we should rebuild it.

The stronger claim is:

> modern systems can change human environments faster than we understand their effects. We should identify recurring human constraints, measure how environments interact with them, redesign harmful defaults where evidence supports it, and keep our values explicit when evidence cannot choose for us.

That version of Human Scale is less romantic.

It is also harder to knock down.
