# Evidence Audit 15 — Justice & Rights Under Pressure

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> Rights are not a scientific discovery. They are a normative commitment about how power may treat a person — and they matter precisely when local consensus, efficiency, tradition, or majority preference points the other way.

Chapter 15 exists because Human Scale's preference for local, understandable, relational institutions creates a predictable danger: proximity can make power easier to understand while also making domination more personal.

The audit strongly keeps the chapter's central correction:

**localism needs a rights floor, and a rights floor needs capable institutions and usable procedures.**

The most important epistemic clarification is that this chapter is primarily normative and institutional rather than empirical. Evidence can tell us whether procedures work, whether discrimination occurs, whether appeals are accessible, whether coercion is reduced, or whether institutions are trusted. Evidence alone cannot derive the proposition that every person possesses equal dignity or should have freedom of conscience.

---

# 1. What survives strongly as a normative and legal framework

## Equal dignity and rights constrain local power

**Grade: Normative commitment grounded in international human-rights frameworks**

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights was adopted in 1948 as a foundational statement of rights to be protected across legal and cultural contexts. Human Scale can use this tradition as a rights floor without pretending the UDHR resolves every conflict or mechanically dictates every policy.

Source:
- OHCHR, Universal Declaration of Human Rights: https://europe.ohchr.org/universal-declaration-human-rights

## Rule of law requires more than locally understandable government

**Grade: Strong institutional framework**

The United Nations defines rule of law through accountability to publicly promulgated, equally enforced, independently adjudicated laws consistent with human-rights norms. It includes equality before the law, accountability, fairness, legal certainty, avoidance of arbitrariness, participation, and procedural transparency.

This directly strengthens Human Scale's governance doctrine:

**legibility is not enough if the rules are arbitrary, discriminatory, or impossible to challenge.**

Sources:
- UN, What is the Rule of Law?: https://www.un.org/ruleoflaw/what-is-the-rule-of-law/
- UN, Rule of Law and Human Rights: https://www.un.org/ruleoflaw/rule-of-law-and-human-rights/

## Access to justice is part of making rights usable

**Grade: Strong institutional principle**

The UN links access to justice with fair, transparent, effective, non-discriminatory, and accountable services and with the ability to challenge discrimination and hold decision-makers accountable.

A formal right without an understandable, affordable, accessible procedure can remain largely symbolic.

Source:
- UN, Rule of Law and Justice: https://www.un.org/ruleoflaw/thematic-areas/justice-2/

## Freedom of thought, conscience, religion, expression, and association are protections for pluralism

**Grade: Normative/legal framework**

The ICCPR protects freedom of thought, conscience, and religion, as well as expression and other civil and political rights. The Human Rights Committee's General Comment No. 22 explicitly interprets freedom of religion or belief broadly to include theistic, non-theistic, and atheistic beliefs, as well as the right not to profess a religion.

This strongly supports the chapter's pluralism principle: Human Scale cannot use community, local culture, or spirituality to demand ideological conformity.

Sources:
- OHCHR, International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights: https://2covenants.ohchr.org/About-ICCPR.html
- Human Rights Committee, General Comment No. 22: https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=NDHbUvPo0H0e6ReM%2BeoASfvbY3aH6JttLpsRvkmL87wD80W4xnAiRpmKA6ZLwTp9xzCLNUYwTBP35uH1Fw6yqQ%3D%3D

# 2. What becomes more careful

## “Rights are universal and obvious”

**Grade: Reject the word obvious**

Human-rights frameworks represent powerful moral and legal commitments, but societies continue to disagree about foundations, scope, conflicts, remedies, positive versus negative duties, proportionality, and institutional design.

Human Scale should state its rights commitments clearly rather than smuggle them in as scientific facts.

## “Local government is more democratic”

**Grade: Context-dependent hypothesis**

Local institutions can be easier to observe and influence. They can also be captured by dominant families, property owners, employers, religious groups, political machines, or highly motivated minorities.

The relevant variables are participation, competition, rights protection, transparency, capacity, representation, corruption, appeal, and exit — not geographic scale alone.

## “Due process is bureaucracy”

**Grade: Important correction**

Some procedure is friction because somebody must be allowed to know the accusation, see evidence, respond, receive reasons, obtain impartial review, and appeal serious errors.

Human Scale should distinguish:

- **protective procedure** — steps that constrain arbitrary power;
- **administrative waste** — duplicate, opaque, irrelevant, or poorly designed burdens that do not materially improve fairness or accuracy.

The project should reduce the second without accidentally stripping the first.

## “Equal treatment means identical treatment”

**Grade: Reject as blanket principle**

Equal dignity can require different accommodations or procedures when relevant circumstances differ. Accessibility, childhood, disability, language, capacity, medical needs, and other conditions can make formally identical treatment substantively exclusionary.

Human Scale should ask whether distinctions are relevant, proportionate, transparent, and rights-compatible rather than whether everyone receives literally the same process.

## “Rights solve hard conflicts”

**Grade: Reject**

Rights can conflict or require balancing: expression and harassment; religion and equal treatment; privacy and public safety; transparency and confidentiality; parental authority and child protection; protest and access; property and environmental regulation.

The rights floor narrows what power may do. It does not eliminate the need for institutions capable of legitimate adjudication.

---

# 3. Rights need institutional capacity

Human Scale often criticizes bureaucracy and centralization. Justice provides a reason not to overcorrect.

A rights floor can require institutions capable of:

- independent adjudication;
- investigation;
- legal aid or accessible representation where stakes justify it;
- enforcement of judgments;
- protection from retaliation;
- interpretation and disability access;
- public records and reason-giving;
- oversight of police, prisons, employers, public agencies, and other powerful actors;
- appeals beyond the immediate local institution;
- emergency protection when family or community is unsafe.

A person cannot exercise a right against a powerful local actor if the only available institution is controlled by that actor.

This is one of the clearest cases where **larger scale can protect Human Scale values**.

---

# 4. Practical freedom matters, but it is not unlimited entitlement

The chapter is right that rights on paper can be unusable in practice.

A person may formally be free to leave while lacking documents, transportation, accessible housing, money, communication, childcare, legal protection, or a safe place to go.

Human Scale should therefore distinguish:

- legal prohibition on coercion;
- procedural ability to challenge coercion;
- material conditions that affect whether exit is realistic.

How far society should go in guaranteeing the material conditions of practical exit is a political question involving cost, responsibility, incentives, and competing rights.

The doctrine can still state the value:

**freedom becomes weaker when every realistic alternative is blocked.**

---

# 5. Rights also constrain Human Scale itself

A movement built around community, localism, family, spirituality, health, children, media, or ecological goals can become coercive if it treats its preferred model as permission to control dissenters.

Human Scale should therefore bind itself to rules such as:

- no compulsory ideology;
- no religious test for belonging;
- no punishment for peaceful criticism;
- no fake grassroots support;
- no doxxing or retaliation against critics;
- no treating children, disabled people, minorities, migrants, dissenters, or unpopular groups as abstractions to be sacrificed for social cohesion;
- no local experiment without a path to raise safety and rights concerns;
- no empirical claim insulated from challenge because it supports the moral program.

The rights floor is not only for government.

It is a constraint on the movement too.

---

# 6. Recommended Chapter 15 changes

1. **Label the rights floor explicitly as a normative commitment.** Evidence informs implementation; it does not derive equal dignity.
2. **Anchor rule of law in non-arbitrariness, equality, independent adjudication, participation, and procedural transparency.**
3. **Separate protective procedure from bureaucratic waste.** Faster is not automatically fairer.
4. **Treat local democracy as a hypothesis rather than a fact.** Measure capture, representation, transparency, rights, and appeal.
5. **Keep practical exit.** Distinguish legal, procedural, and material dimensions of freedom.
6. **Keep pluralism strongly.** Freedom of thought includes religious, nonreligious, and dissenting convictions.
7. **Recognize legitimate rights conflicts.** Build adjudication rather than pretending slogans resolve them.
8. **Require outside appeal where local power is implicated.** Subsidiarity needs a vertical escape route.
9. **Make accessibility part of equal justice.** A procedure that cannot be used is not meaningfully equal.
10. **Bind Human Scale itself to the rights floor.** The doctrine should not demand freedoms it refuses to critics.

---

# 7. What would change our minds?

Because the basic rights floor is a normative commitment, empirical evidence cannot simply falsify the value of equal dignity or freedom of conscience.

Evidence can and should change the mechanisms used to protect those values.

Human Scale should revise a procedural safeguard when high-quality evidence shows it creates large burdens without meaningful improvement in fairness, accuracy, accountability, or protection.

It should strengthen centralized or regional rights enforcement where local institutions repeatedly fail minorities, vulnerable people, dissenters, or unpopular defendants.

It should favor local adjudication where it reliably protects rights, reduces delay and cost, improves accessibility, and maintains real independence and appeal.

It should reject any “rights” mechanism that becomes an opaque tool for arbitrary punishment, selective enforcement, political retaliation, or permanent emergency power.

---

# Result of Audit 15

**Human Scale needs a floor beneath subsidiarity: equal dignity, pluralism, non-arbitrary power, usable due process, and institutions strong enough to protect a person when their family, community, employer, local majority, or government is wrong.**

Rights are not the opposite of community.

They are part of what makes belonging voluntary enough to be humane.
