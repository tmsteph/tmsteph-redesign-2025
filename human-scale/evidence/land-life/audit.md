# Evidence Audit 02: Land & Life

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> The living world matters without requiring us to pretend every garden, tree, farm, or “natural” practice is automatically good.

This audit puts Chapter 2 — *Land & Life: Reconnecting Humans to the Living World* — under pressure.

Chapter 2 makes several distinct kinds of claims:

- ecological dependence
- health effects of green space
- benefits of gardening
- benefits of trees and vegetation
- soil function
- local food and resilience
- community effects
- stewardship and competence
- child development
- land access
- public-land design
- agricultural policy

These should not all receive the same evidentiary grade.

---

# Summary Judgment

**Keep the chapter’s political and ecological direction. Narrow its psychological claims and add stronger safety and tradeoff language.**

The strongest foundations are:

1. Human societies materially depend on functioning ecological systems.
2. Soil performs essential water, nutrient, habitat, filtration, and production functions.
3. Trees and vegetation can provide measurable urban cooling and stormwater benefits.
4. Safe access to parks and green space can support physical activity.
5. Community gardening has experimental evidence for some behavioral and well-being outcomes.
6. Urban growing can create contamination risks that must be assessed rather than romanticized.

The weaker claims are:

- gardening necessarily creates ecological understanding
- caring for living things is universally psychologically grounding
- food forests are inherently superior public landscapes
- local food is automatically more sustainable or resilient
- direct participation in living systems is a proven universal biological requirement
- public land should be productive in any particular proportion

Those are hypotheses, values, or design proposals.

---

# Claim Register

## Claim 1 — Human civilization depends on functioning ecological systems

**Grade: Strong / foundational**

This is not metaphorical.

Food, fresh water, climate regulation, pollination, soil formation, nutrient cycling, materials, disease dynamics, habitat, and many other processes connect human societies to ecosystems.

The Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services documents the dependence of human quality of life and economies on nature’s contributions to people and the consequences of biodiversity loss.

Human Scale should keep the statement:

> civilization exists inside the living world, not outside it.

What does not automatically follow is one particular land-use policy.

Source:
- IPBES Global Assessment (2019): https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.3831673
- IPBES Nexus Assessment (2024): https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.13850054

---

## Claim 2 — Soil is living infrastructure with multiple functions

**Grade: Strong / official agronomic foundation**

USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service defines soil health as the continued capacity of soil to function as a living ecosystem sustaining plants, animals, and humans.

NRCS identifies functions including:

- regulating water
- sustaining plant and animal life
- filtering and buffering pollutants
- nutrient cycling
- physical stability and support

This supports treating soil condition as more than a private farm concern.

It does **not** prove that one named agricultural philosophy or practice package is universally optimal.

Management must fit soil, crop, climate, economics, labor, pests, water, and other conditions.

Source:
- USDA NRCS Soil Health: https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/conservation-basics/soil/soil-health

---

## Claim 3 — Trees and vegetation can function as urban infrastructure

**Grade: Strong for specific physical functions**

EPA documents mechanisms by which trees and vegetation reduce heat through shade and evapotranspiration, and identifies stormwater, air-quality, energy, and other co-benefits.

This supports Chapter 2’s claim that a tree can be more than decoration.

The exact performance depends on:

- species
- canopy
- climate
- water availability
- placement
- maintenance
- allergies
- infrastructure conflicts
- wildfire risk
- local ecology

“Plant more trees” is therefore not a context-free policy.

Source:
- EPA, *Benefits of Trees and Vegetation*: https://www.epa.gov/heatislands/benefits-trees-and-vegetation
- EPA, *Urban Street Trees and Green Infrastructure*: https://www.epa.gov/water-research/urban-street-trees-and-green-infrastructure

---

## Claim 4 — Access to parks and green space can support physical activity

**Grade: Moderate-to-strong for access/activity association and intervention logic**

CDC states that people with safer, closer access to parks, trails, and recreation areas tend to be more physically active, and that park/trail infrastructure can be part of community physical-activity strategies.

Important limitations:

- proximity is not sufficient if a park feels unsafe
- quality and accessibility matter
- parks can be unequally distributed
- people self-select neighborhoods
- “green space” is a broad category

The stronger Human Scale claim is:

> safe, accessible green and recreation infrastructure can expand opportunities for movement and outdoor time.

That is more defensible than claiming that any green space automatically improves health.

Source:
- CDC, *Parks, Trails, and Health* (2025): https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-places/php/key-topics/parks.html

---

## Claim 5 — Community gardening can change some health-related behaviors

**Grade: Moderate evidence with a randomized trial**

A randomized controlled trial in Denver-area adults assigned participants to community gardening or a waitlist control.

The intervention produced increases in physical activity and fiber intake and reductions in perceived stress/anxiety outcomes, while not every anthropometric or diet outcome changed.

This is important because it moves gardening evidence beyond cross-sectional self-selection alone.

It does not establish:

- gardening as treatment for a disease
- identical effects across climates or populations
- that every garden program works
- that gardening is necessary for flourishing
- that benefits come uniquely from contact with soil rather than movement, social interaction, food access, purpose, outdoor time, or the combined intervention

**Doctrine implication:** Chapter 2 can say gardening is a promising multi-function intervention, not that gardening necessarily “changes the relationship with food” in the same way for everyone.

Primary study:
- Litt et al., *Lancet Planetary Health* (2023): https://doi.org/10.1016/S2542-5196(22)00303-5

---

## Claim 6 — Urban agriculture can provide real benefits

**Grade: Supported as a multifunctional practice; outcome magnitude varies**

USDA supports urban agriculture as a legitimate component of local and regional food systems and identifies food access, jobs, green space, training, and community benefits among its potential functions.

These are not guaranteed outcomes.

Urban farming can also face:

- high land cost
- limited water
- contaminated soil
- labor requirements
- vandalism or theft
- uncertain tenure
- pest pressure
- zoning restrictions
- small production volume
- conflict with housing and other uses

**Doctrine implication:** Keep urban agriculture as an option inside diverse landscapes, not a universal substitute for conventional farming or food trade.

Official source:
- USDA NRCS Urban Agriculture: https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/getting-assistance/other-topics/urban-agriculture

---

## Claim 7 — Urban soil can create health risks

**Grade: Strong enough to require explicit safety language**

This was underemphasized in the original chapter.

Urban soil may contain lead, arsenic, petroleum residues, industrial contaminants, legacy pesticide residues, or other hazards depending on site history.

EPA explicitly recommends site assessment and, where appropriate, soil testing before converting potentially contaminated land to food production.

Research in active urban gardens has documented lead and arsenic contamination and shows that some remediation approaches have limited effectiveness.

**Doctrine implication:** Every public recommendation for urban gardening should include site history, soil safety, clean-soil or raised-bed options where needed, water quality, and appropriate technical support.

Official / primary sources:
- EPA Urban Agriculture and Brownfields: https://www.epa.gov/brownfields/urban-agriculture
- EPA Lead in Soil: https://www.epa.gov/lead/lead-soil
- Paltseva et al., *Science of the Total Environment* (2020): https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scitotenv.2020.137965

---

## Claim 8 — “Participation changes the relationship”

**Grade: Working hypothesis / qualitative claim**

This line is philosophically attractive but difficult to state as a universal empirical claim.

Growing food may increase:

- knowledge
- competence
- observation
- attachment to place
- environmental attitudes
- social connection

It can also create:

- frustration
- burden
- cost
- failure
- conflict
- disinterest

The gardening RCT supports some behavioral and well-being outcomes, but it does not prove a specific transformation in ecological identity.

**Doctrine implication:** Rephrase as:

> participation **can** make ecological processes more visible and can build practical knowledge or stewardship for some people.

That is a hypothesis worth testing.

---

## Claim 9 — Caring for plants or animals is psychologically grounding

**Grade: Plausible, variable, and underspecified**

Caring for living things can create routine, responsibility, attachment, sensory contact, purpose, and companionship.

It can also create grief, cost, labor, allergies, injury, stress, or welfare problems.

The Human Scale value is legitimate:

> people should have opportunities to care for life if they want them.

It should not become:

> every person needs a plant or animal for mental health.

Animal policy also has to prioritize the welfare of the animal, not only the human experience.

---

## Claim 10 — Food forests are desirable public infrastructure

**Grade: Political/design proposal with weak universal evidence**

Food forests and edible perennial landscapes can be useful experiments.

They may provide:

- food
- shade
- habitat
- education
- community participation
- perennial cover

They also create questions about:

- water demand
- harvest responsibility
- fallen fruit
- pests
- allergens
- invasive species
- sanitation
- maintenance
- food safety
- accessibility
- vandalism
- liability
- climate suitability

**Doctrine implication:** Keep food forests in the experimental toolkit. Remove any suggestion that public landscaping should generally become food production.

The better principle is:

> public landscapes should be allowed to perform multiple ecological and human functions when those functions fit the site.

---

## Claim 11 — Local food is inherently more sustainable

**Grade: Reject as a blanket claim**

Distance is only one part of food-system impact.

Production method, energy, fertilizer, storage, processing, waste, transport mode, yield, seasonality, packaging, refrigeration, and land-use change can all matter.

Chapter 2 already says total local self-sufficiency is not the goal.

That caution should become stronger.

**Doctrine implication:** Value local and regional capacity for competence, access, redundancy, culture, fresh foods, and some resilience benefits where demonstrated — not as an automatic carbon or ecological advantage.

---

## Claim 12 — Regenerative agriculture is one scientifically settled package

**Grade: Reject label-first reasoning**

“Regenerative” can refer to different bundles of practices.

USDA NRCS provides more precise soil-health principles such as maintaining living roots, minimizing disturbance where appropriate, maintaining soil cover, and increasing diversity.

Specific practices have tradeoffs and context dependencies.

**Doctrine implication:** Keep the chapter’s existing instinct:

> care more about measurable soil, water, biodiversity, productivity, farmer, and economic outcomes than loyalty to an agricultural label.

Strengthen that sentence.

---

## Claim 13 — Everyone should have meaningful access to the living world

**Grade: Value judgment / political proposal**

The Evidence Atlas should stop trying to “prove” this value.

Evidence can tell us about:

- physical activity
- heat
- air and water
- green-space use
- stress
- gardening
- childhood development
- accessibility
- biodiversity

It cannot determine that every person has a right to nearby living land.

Human Scale chooses that as a value because it considers ecological participation, outdoor access, and the visibility of life-support systems part of a good civilization.

The honest form is stronger:

> **Human Scale values meaningful access to the living world and proposes that land-use systems make such access broadly available.**

Then the political mechanisms must compete on cost, equity, maintenance, ecology, and use.

# Recommended Chapter Changes

## Change 1 — Opening thesis

Original:

> Humans do not only need to look at nature. We need ways to participate in the living world.

Recommended:

> Humans depend on the living world whether or not modern systems make that dependence visible. Human Scale values opportunities to experience, understand, and participate in living systems directly.

Why:
- removes a universal psychological necessity claim
- preserves the philosophical direction

## Change 2 — Participation

Replace:

> participation changes the relationship

With:

> participation can make ecological processes visible and can build practical knowledge, stewardship, and attachment to place.

## Change 3 — Gardening

Replace categorical claims that growing food changes everyone’s relationship with food with:

> growing food can make seasonality, water, soil, labor, risk, and ecological dependence more visible — and randomized evidence suggests community gardening can improve some health-related behaviors and well-being outcomes.

## Change 4 — Urban land safety

Add explicit language:

> Before using urban land for food production, site history, soil contamination, water, accessibility, maintenance, and long-term tenure should be assessed. In contaminated sites, clean soil, raised beds, containers, hydroponics, remediation, or non-food uses may be safer.

## Change 5 — Food forests

Move from recommendation toward experimentation.

Use:

> food forests, orchards, and edible landscapes are options to test where climate, maintenance, water, ecology, food safety, and community capacity support them.

## Change 6 — Agriculture

Strengthen outcome-first language:

> judge agricultural practices by soil function, water, biodiversity, emissions, yield, nutrition, worker and animal welfare, farmer economics, and resilience — not by labels alone.

# What Chapter 2 Gets Right

The evidence audit does not weaken several of the chapter’s most important political insights:

- access is not identical to ownership
- renters and people without yards must be included
- trees and shade can be infrastructure
- ecological systems belong inside land-use planning
- public space can serve diverse functions
- agriculture and landscape design involve tradeoffs
- participation and stewardship are legitimate values even when their psychological effects are not universal laws
- modern technology can support ecological knowledge and reduce drudgery without replacing direct experience

Chapter 2 is strongest when it asks:

> **Where does life go in the systems we build?**

It is weakest when it implies:

> contact with soil or living things creates the same psychological benefit for everyone.

---

# What Would Change Our Minds?

Human Scale should weaken specific Land & Life proposals if evidence shows that:

- community gardens create burdens or exclusion without meaningful health, food, social, or educational benefits
- green infrastructure performs poorly relative to other heat, stormwater, or public-space interventions in a specific context
- food forests create persistent maintenance, safety, water, pest, or ecological problems that outweigh benefits
- local food policies raise cost or reduce nutrition without meaningful resilience, access, cultural, or ecological gains
- urban agricultural land displaces higher-value housing or public uses without comparable public benefit
- participation programs are used mainly by already advantaged residents despite efforts at access
- soil-health interventions reduce yields or farmer viability without compensating long-term benefits
- ecological restoration conflicts with accessibility, housing, safety, or other legitimate human needs in ways that cannot be resolved

A Human Scale landscape should not maximize greenery or food production.

It should fit ecological and human purposes to place.

---

# Result of Audit 02

**Chapter 2 survives as a statement of ecological dependence and a political commitment to living-world access. Its psychology becomes more modest and its implementation becomes more technical.**

The stronger Land & Life principle is:

> Humans materially depend on living systems. Safe access to green space, trees, soil, gardens, habitat, and opportunities for stewardship can provide multiple human and ecological benefits. The exact form should be tested against local ecology, safety, maintenance, water, access, cost, and competing land needs.

That is less romantic than “return to nature.”

It is more useful for actually building places.
