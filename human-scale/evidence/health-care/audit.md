# Evidence Audit 12 — Health & Care Under Pressure

**Human Scale Evidence Atlas — August 2026**

> Health care should be person-centred, evidence-based, safe, accessible, coordinated, and financially survivable. “Natural,” “local,” “preventive,” “high-tech,” and “more care” are not automatic proxies for quality.

Chapter 12 deliberately begins from modern medicine rather than from nostalgia. That choice survives the audit.

The strongest Human Scale contribution is not a new clinical theory. It is a health-system design question:

**Can a person move through prevention, primary care, specialty care, emergency care, rehabilitation, mental health, disability support, long-term care, and palliative care without becoming the sole integration layer for the system?**

---

# 1. What survives strongly

## Primary health care is a strong organizing framework

**Grade: Strong official framework / broad evidence base**

WHO defines primary health care as a whole-of-society approach spanning health promotion, prevention, treatment, rehabilitation, and palliative care, with services as close as feasible to everyday life.

WHO also describes five primary-care functions: first-contact accessibility, continuity, coordination, comprehensiveness, and people-centredness.

This strongly supports the chapter's emphasis on longitudinal relationships and coordination.

Sources:
- WHO Primary health care: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/primary-health-care
- WHO Primary care: https://www.who.int/teams/integrated-health-services/clinical-services-and-systems/primary-care

## Access without financial catastrophe is a legitimate health-system goal

**Grade: Strong international policy consensus / value goal**

WHO's definition of universal health coverage combines access to needed quality services with protection from financial hardship.

Evidence can measure access, unmet need, service quality, out-of-pocket burden, and financial catastrophe.

It cannot by itself choose one financing model.

Human Scale should therefore support the goal while leaving taxes, social insurance, regulated private coverage, public provision, employer systems, and mixed systems open to comparative evidence and political debate.

Source:
- WHO Universal health coverage: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/universal-health-coverage-%28uhc%29

## Quality and safety are measurable system properties

**Grade: Strong institutional and clinical evidence**

WHO defines quality around effective, safe, people-centred care and treats patient safety as a system responsibility rather than only an individual clinician responsibility.

Human Scale should resist the temptation to equate warm interpersonal care with safe care. Relationship quality and technical quality both matter.

Sources:
- WHO Quality health services: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/quality-health-services
- WHO Safer primary care: https://www.who.int/teams/integrated-health-services/patient-safety/research/safer-primary-care

## Rehabilitation is not an optional afterthought

**Grade: Strong official framework**

WHO defines rehabilitation around optimizing functioning and reducing disability in interaction with the person's environment. It belongs alongside prevention, treatment, and palliative care within universal health coverage.

This aligns especially well with Human Scale's focus on capability rather than only disease labels.

Source:
- WHO Rehabilitation: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/rehabilitation

## Palliative care is part of humane medicine

**Grade: Strong clinical and ethical framework**

Palliative care aims to relieve physical, psychosocial, and spiritual suffering and support patients and families facing life-threatening illness.

Human Scale should preserve the principle that medicine is not only cure. Relief, function, goals, dignity, and care remain important when cure is impossible.

Source:
- WHO Palliative care: https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/palliative-care

# 2. What becomes more careful

## “Prevention is better than treatment”

**Grade: Reject as universal slogan**

Some preventive interventions are extraordinarily effective. Others are low-value, burdensome, costly, or create false positives and overdiagnosis.

Human Scale should ask:

- what risk is being prevented;
- how large the benefit is;
- who is likely to benefit;
- what harms, false positives, side effects, cost, and burden occur;
- whether treatment after disease appears is better in that context.

Prevention and treatment are complements.

## “Care close to home is better”

**Grade: Mechanism, not endpoint**

Primary care, rehabilitation, home care, telehealth, and community services can reduce travel and improve continuity.

Rare disease, trauma, complex surgery, intensive care, specialized imaging, and other services often benefit from regional concentration and expertise.

The correct rule is:

**provide care as close as feasible without sacrificing technical quality, safety, expertise, or efficient scale.**

## “Continuity is always best”

**Grade: Strong value with access tradeoffs**

Longitudinal relationships can improve coordination and context. But requiring one clinician can create delays or bottlenecks.

Human Scale should value continuity of **information, responsibility, and care plan**, not only continuity with one individual professional.

## “More information creates more agency”

**Grade: Conditional**

Patients need understandable information and access to records. Too much unfiltered data, conflicting portals, raw results, alerts, forms, and jargon can create cognitive burden rather than agency.

The goal is usable information with escalation to expertise.

## “Self-care reduces dependence”

**Grade: Useful capability, dangerous if moralized**

Self-management, first aid, medication knowledge, home monitoring, and prevention can expand capability.

They must not become an excuse to shift professional responsibility, cost, or clinical uncertainty onto sick and disabled people.

# 3. Specialization and integration

Specialization is not a violation of Human Scale. It is one of modern medicine's great strengths.

The problem is fragmentation.

A person with multiple conditions may interact with primary care, specialists, labs, imaging, pharmacies, hospitals, rehabilitation, insurers, caregivers, devices, and portals.

Human Scale should test health systems by whether:

- one care plan remains intelligible;
- medication lists reconcile;
- information travels reliably;
- responsibility is clear;
- referrals close the loop;
- patients know who to contact;
- results are explained;
- disagreement has an appeal or second-opinion path;
- transitions between hospital, home, rehabilitation, and long-term care are coordinated.

The system can be technologically complex while remaining humanly legible.

# 4. AI and health

WHO's AI-for-health guidance recognizes meaningful opportunities in diagnosis, treatment, research, drug development, surveillance, and health-system management while centering ethics, human rights, safety, equity, accountability, and governance.

Source:
- WHO Ethics and governance of artificial intelligence for health: https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240029200

Human Scale should therefore reject both “AI should not touch medicine” and “a model is good enough if its benchmark is high.”

A health AI system should be evaluated by:

- clinical outcome and calibration;
- population validity;
- workflow effects;
- failure detection;
- meaningful human review;
- appeal and override;
- privacy and security;
- bias and access;
- whether clinicians lose important skills;
- whether the system saves or consumes human time;
- liability and responsibility when it is wrong.

---

# 5. Recommended Chapter 12 changes

1. **Keep modern medicine explicitly.** Human Scale is not an alternative-medicine doctrine.
2. **Make primary health care the organizing framework, not primary care as a gatekeeping ideology.**
3. **Define access + financial protection as goals while keeping financing mechanisms open.**
4. **Treat safety and technical effectiveness as coequal with people-centredness.**
5. **Keep specialization; attack fragmentation.**
6. **Elevate rehabilitation and function.** Health is not only cure or survival.
7. **Keep palliative care and goals-of-care planning near the center.**
8. **Replace “prevention first” with evidence-based prevention.**
9. **Treat information overload and administrative burden as health-system harms when they impair care.**
10. **Govern health AI by risk, evidence, accountability, and real human control.**

---

# 6. What would change our minds?

Human Scale should weaken continuity or community-based-care claims when concentrated or rapid-access models consistently produce better outcomes without unacceptable loss of coordination or burden.

It should reject administrative simplification that removes safety checks, useful documentation, fraud controls, or necessary clinical information.

It should expand AI delegation where rigorous real-world evaluations show better outcomes, safety, access, equity, and clinician/patient experience with robust failure handling.

It should restrict or remove AI where benchmark gains fail to translate into real clinical benefit, create automation bias, worsen inequity, deskill clinicians in necessary functions, or obscure accountability.

It should revise any health recommendation when high-quality clinical guidelines or evidence contradict it.

---

# Result of Audit 12

**A Human Scale health system should combine modern technical excellence with continuity, coordination, usable information, financial protection, rehabilitation, caregiving, and dignity.**

The goal is not care that feels small.

The goal is a system complex enough to save lives without making the person disappear inside it.
