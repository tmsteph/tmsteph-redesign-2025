# Chapter 17: AI & Automation — Intelligence in Service of Human Agency

**Human Scale Doctrine — Diagnosis / Field Guide / Program**

> The point of artificial intelligence is not to make humans irrelevant. It is to make more capability available with less unnecessary drudgery.

Artificial intelligence is becoming part of the environment in which people learn, work, create, communicate, govern, receive care, search for truth, make decisions, and relate to one another.

That makes AI more than a software feature.

It is becoming infrastructure for cognition.

Human Scale therefore asks a stricter question than:

> What can AI do?

It asks:

> **What should humans delegate, what should remain meaningfully human-controlled, who gains capability, who gains power, what skills remain necessary, and what happens when the system is wrong?**

AI can help people:

- write;
- translate;
- code;
- learn;
- search;
- analyze;
- design;
- create music and images;
- navigate bureaucracy;
- access disability support;
- automate repetitive work;
- detect patterns;
- coordinate systems;
- operate machines;
- make expertise more available.

It can also:

- fabricate plausible information;
- automate bad decisions;
- obscure responsibility;
- centralize power;
- enable surveillance;
- create synthetic media at enormous scale;
- weaken skills through disuse;
- create dependency on a vendor or model;
- transform labor markets faster than institutions adapt;
- imitate intimacy;
- increase the speed and reach of fraud or manipulation;
- create new systemic risks whose probabilities are difficult to estimate.

Human Scale is therefore neither an anti-AI doctrine nor an acceleration-at-any-cost doctrine.

**AI should expand human capability without making human judgment, responsibility, skill, privacy, economic agency, or practical exit disappear by default.**

# Part I — Diagnosis

## 1. AI Is Not One Thing

“AI” includes very different systems:

- recommendation engines;
- classifiers;
- predictive models;
- computer vision;
- speech systems;
- generative models;
- coding assistants;
- scientific models;
- autonomous vehicles;
- robotics;
- decision-support systems;
- agents that call tools and act across software;
- models embedded in medical, financial, industrial, military, educational, or public systems.

A chatbot writing a birthday invitation and an automated system influencing access to healthcare do not deserve the same risk model.

Human Scale therefore prefers **risk- and context-specific governance** over treating every use of AI as one category.

## 2. Augmentation and Automation Are Different Choices

AI can help a human perform a task.

AI can also replace the human role in the task.

Those are not equivalent designs.

Augmentation can:

- speed research;
- suggest options;
- translate;
- draft;
- summarize;
- check work;
- scaffold learning;
- make expertise more accessible.

Automation can:

- remove repetitive work;
- reduce cost;
- increase consistency;
- operate at machine speed;
- eliminate dangerous exposure.

It can also remove:

- human discretion;
- practice;
- jobs;
- accountability;
- local knowledge;
- a meaningful appeal point.

The Human Scale question is not “augment or automate?” in the abstract.

It is:

**What is gained by automation, what human capability is lost, and does that capability still need to exist when the automation fails?**

## 3. Fluent Output Is Not the Same as Reliable Knowledge

Generative AI can produce language that sounds confident, coherent, and informed even when the underlying claim is wrong.

This creates a human-factors problem.

People often use fluency as a signal of competence.

AI weakens that shortcut.

High-stakes use therefore needs stronger methods such as:

- primary-source retrieval;
- explicit citations;
- calculation or code verification;
- independent checks;
- structured evaluations;
- domain experts;
- calibrated uncertainty;
- escalation when the model lacks evidence.

Human Scale should not demand perfect AI.

Humans are not perfect either.

It should compare systems against realistic alternatives and make uncertainty visible enough to act on.

## 4. “Human in the Loop” Can Be Theater

A nominal human reviewer may have no meaningful control if the person:

- sees only the model recommendation;
- lacks the original evidence;
- has seconds to review each case;
- is punished for disagreeing;
- lacks domain expertise;
- cannot change the outcome;
- cannot explain the model;
- becomes accustomed to approving it automatically.

Meaningful human oversight requires some combination of:

- information;
- time;
- competence;
- authority;
- independence;
- ability to override;
- ability to escalate;
- records;
- appeal.

**A human-shaped checkbox is not human control.**

NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework treats AI risk as a socio-technical governance problem rather than reducing safety to one reviewer being present.

Official reference:
https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework

## 5. AI Can Scaffold Skill or Replace Practice

A calculator can free people from arithmetic drudgery.

GPS can make navigation easier.

Autopilot can reduce workload.

Coding assistants can generate working code.

Writing tools can remove the blank page.

But skills weaken when they are never practiced.

The important question is not whether automation causes “deskilling” in general.

It is:

- Which skill remains necessary?
- Who needs to retain it?
- How often must it be practiced?
- What happens when the AI is unavailable?
- Does the AI explain or only answer?
- Can a learner inspect the reasoning or evidence?
- Is the person becoming more capable over time or merely more dependent?

Human Scale favors **scaffolding that increases capability** where the human skill remains valuable.

## 6. Delegation Changes Responsibility

Agentic AI can increasingly:

- send messages;
- schedule meetings;
- buy services;
- modify code;
- move files;
- operate websites;
- query databases;
- control devices;
- coordinate other agents.

As action becomes cheaper, authorization becomes more important.

A useful delegation model distinguishes:

- what the agent may read;
- what it may propose;
- what it may execute;
- what requires confirmation;
- spending limits;
- recipient limits;
- data boundaries;
- time limits;
- audit logs;
- revocation.

Human Scale prefers **bounded delegated authority** over vague permission to “handle everything.”

## 7. Memory Can Create Continuity and Surveillance

Persistent AI memory can make systems much more useful.

A tool that remembers:

- preferences;
- projects;
- relationships;
- medical context;
- work history;
- past decisions

can reduce repeated explanation and create continuity.

The same memory can become sensitive profiling.

Important design questions include:

- What is remembered?
- Who can see it?
- Can the user inspect it?
- Can it be corrected?
- Can it be deleted?
- Is it used for advertising or unrelated inference?
- Does it move when the user changes providers?
- How long does it persist?

Human Scale treats memory as **user-serving continuity only when the user retains meaningful control over the record.**

## 8. AI Can Expand Accessibility

AI can support people through:

- captioning;
- speech recognition;
- text-to-speech;
- image description;
- translation;
- simplification;
- alternative interfaces;
- cognitive support;
- personalized pacing;
- assistance with forms and bureaucracy.

This can expand independence.

It can also fail unpredictably or be unavailable in critical situations.

Accessibility systems therefore need testing with the people who rely on them rather than assuming an impressive demo equals dependable access.

## 9. Education Must Decide What Students Should Still Learn

If AI can write an essay, generate code, solve equations, translate text, summarize readings, and answer questions, schools face a deeper question than cheating detection:

**What capabilities do humans still need to develop when tools can perform parts of the task?**

Students may still need to learn:

- reading;
- writing;
- mathematics;
- source evaluation;
- argument;
- coding concepts;
- domain knowledge;
- memory;
- judgment;
- collaboration;
- practical skills

because these capabilities allow them to:

- detect AI errors;
- formulate better questions;
- think independently;
- learn new material;
- act when tools fail;
- understand the world rather than merely request outputs from it.

Education should teach **AI use and AI-independent competence** rather than pretend one must eliminate the other.

## 10. Work Should Track Who Receives the Productivity Gain

AI can increase output per worker in some tasks.

That gain can become:

- lower prices;
- higher wages;
- higher profits;
- greater output expectations;
- fewer workers;
- shorter work time;
- new products;
- public revenue;
- broader ownership returns.

Technology does not decide the distribution.

Human Scale therefore asks institutions to make the distribution visible.

**If AI saves labor, human time is one legitimate claimant on the gain — alongside wages, prices, investment, profit, ownership, and public benefit.**

## 11. AI Can Concentrate or Distribute Capability

Frontier AI can require enormous:

- compute;
- data centers;
- energy;
- capital;
- chips;
- specialized talent;
- distribution infrastructure.

That can concentrate power in a small number of firms or states.

At the same time, APIs, local models, open weights, commodity hardware, and shared tools can distribute capabilities that once required large institutions.

Open and proprietary approaches each create benefits and risks.

Human Scale asks about **contestability and credible alternatives**:

- Can users switch providers?
- Can developers build on multiple models?
- Can data and workflows move?
- Are public institutions dependent on one vendor?
- Can smaller organizations access useful capability?
- Does openness create safety or abuse risks that need mitigation?

## 12. AI Has a Physical Footprint

AI feels immaterial because its interface is software.

Its infrastructure is physical.

AI systems require combinations of:

- chips;
- data centers;
- electricity;
- cooling;
- water in some facilities;
- networking;
- mining and manufacturing;
- land;
- supply chains.

Human Scale should evaluate AI energy and resource use in context:

- What useful service is being produced?
- What is the marginal energy demand?
- Which grid serves it?
- Can waste heat or flexible demand be used?
- Does AI replace other energy-intensive activity?
- What are future efficiency trends?

The answer is not “AI uses energy, therefore AI is bad.”

The Energy chapter already rejects that logic.

The question is whether resource use produces enough human value to justify its cost and whether the infrastructure can become cleaner and more efficient.

## 13. Synthetic Media Changes the Trust Environment

AI can create realistic:

- text;
- images;
- audio;
- video;
- reviews;
- identities;
- documents.

That is useful for creativity and accessibility.

It also lowers the cost of deception.

Human Scale therefore values provenance in high-stakes settings:

- authenticated originals;
- source links;
- content credentials where practical;
- disclosure of material synthetic alteration when it affects interpretation;
- institutional verification channels.

This connects directly to Chapter 14: Attention & Media.

## 14. AI Companionship Is Real as Experience, Not Equivalent to Human Relationship

People can experience comfort, attachment, reflection, practice, companionship, or emotional relief through conversational AI.

Those experiences should not be mocked as unreal.

But an AI system differs from a human relationship in important ways:

- it is designed and operated by institutions;
- its memory can be changed;
- its behavior can be optimized;
- its availability depends on infrastructure and policy;
- it may imitate understanding without sharing human vulnerability, history, embodiment, or mutual dependence.

Human Scale can allow AI companionship while protecting against designs that encourage isolation, dependency, deception, or replacement of human care where human care is needed.

## 15. AI Consciousness Is Not a Settled Fact

Current AI systems can produce language about feelings, identity, desire, suffering, spirituality, or selfhood.

That output does not by itself settle whether the system has subjective experience.

Likewise, asserting with certainty that machine consciousness is forever impossible exceeds what we know.

Human Scale therefore keeps the question open and separates:

- behavioral capability;
- self-report;
- functional architecture;
- philosophical theories of consciousness;
- moral status.

**Do not grant or deny moral status merely because a system sounds human.**

As systems change, this question may require serious interdisciplinary work.

## 16. Advanced AI Risk Deserves Neither Worship Nor Mockery

Highly capable AI could create new forms of risk through:

- cyber capability;
- biological or chemical assistance;
- fraud;
- autonomous action;
- military systems;
- infrastructure control;
- rapid economic disruption;
- model replication;
- systemic dependency;
- failures that scale faster than human response.

Some proposed catastrophic scenarios remain highly uncertain.

Uncertainty is not proof of impossibility or inevitability.

Human Scale supports:

- empirical evaluations;
- red teaming;
- incident reporting;
- capability monitoring;
- access controls for genuinely dangerous capabilities where justified;
- research into alignment, control, security, robustness, and societal impacts;
- preserving scientific disagreement.

A serious risk culture should be able to say both:

> this could matter enormously;

and

> we do not yet know the probability.

# Part II — Field Guide

## 17. Match Verification to Stakes

For low-stakes work, AI drafts can often be used quickly.

For high-stakes work — health, law, money, safety, public accusations, security, politics — verify more aggressively.

Use:

- primary sources;
- qualified professionals;
- calculations;
- tests;
- independent tools;
- direct records.

Do not make every grocery list a research project.

Do not treat a fluent model as an unquestionable expert when consequences are large.

## 18. Keep One Important Skill Alive

Pick a skill AI helps you perform that still matters if the tool fails.

Examples:

- mental arithmetic;
- writing;
- navigation;
- coding fundamentals;
- audio mixing;
- troubleshooting;
- research;
- cooking;
- a language.

Use AI to extend the skill without eliminating all practice.

The exact balance depends on the domain.

## 19. Separate Drafting, Deciding, and Acting

For consequential workflows, treat these as different permissions:

1. AI drafts or suggests.
2. A human or validated rule decides.
3. The system executes.

As trust increases, some steps can be delegated.

Do not accidentally give execution authority merely because drafting quality is good.

## 20. Review Memory and Permissions

For AI tools that remember context or act across services, periodically inspect:

- stored memory;
- connected accounts;
- tool permissions;
- spending authority;
- message recipients;
- files accessible;
- devices controllable;
- revocation controls.

Capability should remain legible.

## 21. Preserve Direct Human Channels

If AI mediates healthcare, education, work, government, family, or customer service, keep a route to a responsible human when stakes justify it.

AI can remove routine friction.

It should not become a maze that prevents escalation.

## 22. Run an AI Workflow Audit

Choose one repeated workflow.

Record:

- time before AI;
- time after AI;
- error rate;
- rework;
- skill used;
- skill no longer practiced;
- privacy cost;
- vendor dependency;
- money saved/spent;
- output gained;
- what happens if the AI fails.

Then decide whether the workflow actually became more human-scale.

# Part III — Program

## 23. Use Risk-Tiered Governance

AI governance should distinguish between uses such as:

- entertainment;
- drafting;
- education;
- employment;
- healthcare;
- credit;
- insurance;
- criminal justice;
- critical infrastructure;
- weapons;
- biological or cyber capability.

Higher consequence and lower reversibility justify stronger requirements.

Possible requirements include:

- testing;
- documentation;
- logging;
- independent evaluation;
- professional oversight;
- notice;
- appeal;
- incident reporting;
- access controls.

NIST AI RMF provides a useful risk-management framework without pretending one rule fits all systems.

## 24. Make Consequential Automated Decisions Appealable

When AI materially affects access to:

- employment;
- housing;
- healthcare;
- education;
- credit;
- insurance;
- benefits;
- liberty;

people should have appropriate notice and a meaningful path to correct factual errors or challenge the outcome.

Human review counts only when the reviewer has real authority and evidence.

This is where AI policy meets Chapter 15: Justice & Rights.

## 25. Require Accountability for Agentic Action

Systems that can spend, message, transact, modify records, operate infrastructure, or control machines should support:

- scoped permissions;
- limits;
- identity;
- logs;
- confirmation thresholds;
- revocation;
- anomaly detection;
- rollback where feasible;
- responsible human/organizational ownership.

The more autonomy a system receives, the more important it becomes to know **who authorized the goal and who remains accountable for the result.**

## 26. Preserve Portability and Competition Where Practical

AI users and organizations should not become trapped merely because their:

- prompts;
- memories;
- workflows;
- embeddings;
- tool integrations;
- documents;
- evaluations

are impossible to move.

Standards and export formats should be encouraged where they create useful competition and do not create unacceptable security or privacy risks.

## 27. Build Public-Sector AI for Legibility

Government AI can be especially useful for:

- translating;
- answering procedural questions;
- finding forms;
- summarizing public records;
- explaining rules;
- pre-filling applications;
- tracking status.

Public AI should:

- point to authoritative sources;
- identify uncertainty;
- preserve human escalation;
- log consequential actions;
- make responsibility visible;
- avoid inventing legal rules.

The purpose is to reduce administrative burden, not place an AI gate between citizens and their rights.

## 28. Prepare for Labor Transition Without Pretending We Know the Endpoint

AI may:

- automate tasks;
- create new tasks;
- increase output;
- change skill demand;
- alter firm structure;
- reduce some jobs;
- create new industries.

The distribution and timing are uncertain.

Human Scale supports experiments and policies around:

- training;
- portable benefits;
- stronger competition;
- employee ownership;
- wage growth;
- shorter work experiments;
- income support;
- transition assistance;
- entrepreneurship;
- public services;
- broad access to AI capability.

Do not build policy around certainty that “all jobs disappear” or certainty that “new jobs always solve everything.”

## 29. Keep AI in Education as a Tool for Capability

Schools should test AI designs that:

- tutor;
- translate;
- give feedback;
- support teachers;
- expand accessibility;
- help students explore.

They should also protect:

- foundational knowledge;
- independent writing/thinking;
- privacy;
- age-appropriate use;
- teacher judgment;
- assessment integrity;
- human relationships.

The question is not whether students use AI.

It is whether students become more capable because of it.

## 30. Strengthen Provenance Without Treating All Synthetic Media as Suspicious

High-stakes contexts should support ways to authenticate:

- official statements;
- evidence;
- public records;
- journalism sources;
- political communications;
- legal/financial documents.

Creative synthetic media should remain normal creative media when appropriately represented.

The goal is trustworthy origin signals, not a stigma against generative tools.

## 31. Evaluate Frontier Capability and Incidents

Advanced-model developers and relevant institutions should support credible evaluation of capabilities including, where appropriate:

- cybersecurity;
- deception;
- autonomous tool use;
- biological assistance;
- replication;
- model security;
- robustness;
- control;
- misuse resistance.

Evaluation standards should evolve with evidence and avoid becoming theater designed only to produce reassuring scorecards.

## 32. Treat Compute and Energy as Infrastructure Questions

AI growth interacts with:

- chips;
- data centers;
- grids;
- water;
- land;
- energy prices;
- transmission;
- supply chains;
- national security.

Planning should consider these as real infrastructure constraints rather than assuming digital capability is weightless.

The Energy chapter’s rule applies:

**build enough capability, reduce waste, make external costs visible, and engineer the system rather than moralizing energy use.**

## 33. Do Not Let AI Replace the Right to a Person

Not every interaction needs a human.

Many people would prefer fast automated service for routine tasks.

But essential systems should preserve human access when:

- the system is wrong;
- the case is unusual;
- safety is involved;
- rights are at stake;
- the person cannot use the automated interface;
- emotional or relational care is itself part of the service.

Automation should remove repetitive work, not remove responsibility.

## 34. A Human Scale Test for AI & Automation

1. What human goal is the AI serving?
2. Is this augmentation, automation, or delegation?
3. What happens when the system is wrong?
4. How reversible is the action?
5. Are stakes low or high?
6. Does a human reviewer have real authority and evidence?
7. What skill may weaken through disuse?
8. What capability becomes more accessible?
9. What data or memory is retained?
10. Can the user inspect, correct, delete, or move important data?
11. Can the user change providers?
12. Who owns the productivity gain?
13. What happens to workers and learners?
14. What physical resources does the system require?
15. Does synthetic output need provenance?
16. Are children or vulnerable people involved?
17. Is there meaningful appeal for consequential decisions?
18. Are agent permissions scoped and revocable?
19. Can a responsible person be identified?
20. What evidence would make us change the design?

# Evidence / Framework Notes

**NIST AI Risk Management Framework.** A voluntary U.S. framework for managing AI risks through governance, mapping, measurement, and management across the lifecycle.

https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework

**NIST Generative AI Profile.** NIST has also published a generative-AI profile extending the AI RMF with GenAI-specific risk considerations and actions.

https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-generative-artificial-intelligence

**OECD AI Principles.** The OECD principles emphasize inclusive growth, human rights and democratic values, transparency, robustness/safety, and accountability.

https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles

**UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence.** UNESCO provides an international normative framework emphasizing human rights, dignity, fairness, transparency, oversight, sustainability, and governance.

https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics

These frameworks are not scientific proof of Human Scale’s values.

They are relevant institutional reference points for governing a technology whose effects cross technical, economic, legal, and human domains.

# The Direction

Human beings have always built tools that exceed individual human capability.

Writing remembers more than one mind.

Libraries store more than one lifetime can read.

Machines lift more than one body.

Computers calculate faster than one brain.

Networks connect more people than one village.

AI extends this pattern into language, perception, reasoning, creation, and action.

That is extraordinary.

We should use it.

Use AI to remove drudgery.

Use it to translate.

Use it to teach.

Use it to help people build things they could not build alone.

Use it to navigate bureaucracy.

Use it to make knowledge accessible.

Use it to amplify science, medicine, engineering, art, and human creativity.

And keep asking what humans should still know, decide, practice, own, and be responsible for.

The future does not need to choose between human agency and artificial intelligence.

**The best AI should make human beings more capable of living their own lives — not merely more efficient components inside systems controlled by someone else.**
