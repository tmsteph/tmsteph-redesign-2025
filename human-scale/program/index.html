Human Scale Program

What should communities, workplaces, cities, governments, institutions, and technology systems change together?

Build systems that make a human life easier to live.

What this is

The Doctrine asks what kind of civilization we should build. The Field Guide asks what a person can do now. The Program asks what we should change together.

This is not a party platform and not a finished ideology. It is a working set of proposals that should be debated, tested, measured, revised, and abandoned when they fail.

The final measure is not maximum growth, localism, technology, government, markets, or efficiency. It is whether the system serves human life well.

Five rules for political action

1. Start with the actual problem. Do not begin with the institution you already prefer.
2. Use the smallest effective scale. Keep decisions close when possible; scale up when the problem genuinely crosses boundaries.
3. Preserve a rights floor. Localism never excuses coercion, exclusion, corruption, or loss of basic liberties.
4. Prefer reversible experiments under uncertainty. Pilot, phase, measure, and revise where possible.
5. Name the kind of claim. Evidence, hypothesis, values, and political choices are not the same thing.
Starter Program

Twelve directions

1

Living-world access

Parks, gardens, food forests, orchards, shade, school gardens, habitat, healthy soil, and real opportunities to grow or care for life.

Test: Can ordinary people participate without wealth, a private yard, or a long trip?

2

Proximity & housing

More homes near daily needs, safer walking, useful transit, shade, mixed uses, and less human time lost to compulsory distance.

Test: Does the place give people time back?

3

Public space

Libraries, parks, plazas, playgrounds, community rooms, markets, gardens, trails, seating, restrooms, art, and music.

Test: Where can people be together without having to buy something?

4

Human time at work

More predictable schedules, caregiving support, meaningful flexibility, less administrative waste, worker voice, and experiments that return productivity gains as time.

Test: Does better productivity improve human life too?

5

Technological freedom

Repairability, interoperability, data portability, durable formats, device longevity, offline resilience, transparent subscriptions, and open public standards.

Test: Can the user repair it, export their work, and credibly leave?

6

AI for agency

Use AI to reduce bureaucracy, assist people, teach, translate, and automate dangerous or monotonous work while preserving review, appeals, privacy, and human responsibility.

Test: Does AI expand capability or mostly transfer power away?

7

Security + ownership

Test simpler supports, employee ownership, profit sharing, cooperatives, small business, land trusts, asset-building, productive capital, and real competition.

Test: Can people survive disruption and gradually build credible choices?

8

Energy abundance + resilience

Judge nuclear, renewables, storage, transmission, efficiency, local generation, and grids by reliability, cost, ecology, safety, resilience, and maintainability.

Test: Are capability and reliability increasing while risks are reduced rather than hidden?

9

Food competence + capacity

Cooking education, gardens, regional processing, markets, soil and water stewardship, seed diversity, school meals, research, trade, and less waste.

Test: Can people eat well without degrading the living systems food depends on?

10

Legible institutions

Plain language, clear timelines, fewer duplicate forms, human support, appeals, transparent fees, usable public records, and automation that removes bureaucracy.

Test: Can a normal person understand what is required and challenge an error?

11

Human childhood

Outdoor play, nature, movement, art, music, making, practical skills, libraries, unstructured social time, mentorship, digital literacy, and growing autonomy.

Test: Are children becoming capable participants in bodies, places, relationships, tools, knowledge, and life?

12

Concrete civic power

Make democracy tangible through crossings, parks, gardens, schools, libraries, transit, housing, budgets, tree canopy, local business, and resilience projects.

Test: Can people find the decision-maker, participate, and see what happened?

Government and restraint

Human Scale does not begin with “government or no government” or “public or private.”

Government is strongest where rights need protection, genuinely shared infrastructure needs coordination, public goods are persistently underprovided, severe external harms need limits, fair rules need enforcement, or basic social order and security require collective capacity.

Government should be restrained where coercion is unnecessary, voluntary institutions solve the problem well, uniform rules erase useful local variation, or administrative power becomes unreviewable and self-perpetuating.

What institution can solve this problem competently while preserving rights, accountability, pluralism, and practical freedom?

The Human Scale policy test

What human problem is this actually trying to solve?
What evidence supports the diagnosis, and what remains uncertain?
What is the smallest scale capable of solving it effectively?
Which rights must remain protected?
Who gains power, and who bears new costs?
What happens to human time, family, and community?
What happens to ownership and practical exit?
What happens to soil, water, energy, climate, habitat, and future generations?
Can ordinary people understand and comply?
Can small organizations participate?
Can the policy be piloted or phased?
How will results be measured?
What evidence would make us revise or repeal it?

Start smaller than a revolution

The Program does not require national power to begin. A Human Scale project can start with one garden, one block, one employer, one school, one library, one open-source tool, one cooperative, one safer crossing, one repair event, one public meeting, or one local pilot.

The smallest project can produce evidence for the larger argument.

What this is not

Human Scale is not anti-city, anti-market, anti-government, anti-car, anti-AI, or a command to return to the past. Small institutions are not automatically virtuous, and every human value cannot be maximized at once.

Systems must justify themselves by the lives they make possible.

The direction

A Human Scale society can keep advanced medicine, computers, universities, global science, trade, industry, dense cities, large infrastructure, powerful energy systems, and technologies not yet imagined.

It should also give more people time, land access, healthy food, useful skills, durable relationships, meaningful ownership, understandable institutions, public space, quieter technology, resilient communities, and real influence over the systems that structure their lives.

The political project is not to make civilization smaller. It is to make civilization answerable to the human beings living inside it.

Build capability without captivity. Build abundance without disconnection. Build institutions without making people disappear inside them.
