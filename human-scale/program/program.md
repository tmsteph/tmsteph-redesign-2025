# The Human Scale Program

**Version 0.1 — August 2026**

> Build systems that make a human life easier to live.

The Human Scale Doctrine asks what kind of civilization human beings need.

The Field Guide asks what people can do now.

The Program asks the collective question:

**What should communities, workplaces, cities, governments, institutions, and technology systems change together?**

This is not a party platform and it is not a finished ideology.

It is a working program: proposals that follow from the doctrine, can be argued about in public, tested where possible, measured, revised, and abandoned when they fail.

## The standard

A proposal belongs in the Human Scale Program when it plausibly helps people gain more of the following without creating greater harms elsewhere:

- health
- time
- agency
- belonging
- practical freedom
- access to the living world
- useful competence
- resilience
- beauty
- meaningful work
- material security
- understandable institutions
- a healthy ecological inheritance

Efficiency, growth, profit, local control, public control, technological capability, and convenience can all be useful.

None is the final measure.

**The final question is whether the system serves human life well.**

## Five rules for political action

### 1. Start with the actual problem

Do not begin with the preferred institution. Begin with the condition people are experiencing. Then ask what is causing it, who controls the relevant environment, what scale can act, and what tradeoffs a proposed change creates.

### 2. Use the smallest effective scale

Neighborhoods should not wait for national government to plant a tree. Cities should not pretend they can solve atmospheric pollution alone.

A problem should be addressed at the smallest scale capable of solving it effectively while protecting rights and accounting for consequences that cross the boundary.

### 3. Preserve a rights floor

Localism does not justify discrimination, coercion, corruption, exclusion, or the removal of basic liberties.

Belonging should never require captivity.

### 4. Prefer reversible experiments under uncertainty

When evidence is incomplete, use pilots, prototypes, temporary rules, demonstration projects, phased implementation, and transparent evaluation where possible.

### 5. Name the kind of claim

Every proposal should distinguish evidence, hypothesis, value judgment, and political choice.

A scientific finding does not automatically dictate a policy. A political preference should not pretend to be science.

# The Starter Program

The first version of the Program contains twelve directions. They are deliberately broad enough to apply in different places and specific enough to suggest real projects.

## 1. Give everyone meaningful access to the living world

**Goal:** Nature should be part of ordinary life, not a luxury destination.

Public and community institutions should expand practical access to parks and natural areas, community gardens and allotments, food forests and public orchards, school gardens, urban farms where appropriate, trees and shade, habitat restoration, clean water and healthy soil, and opportunities to grow or care for living things.

Land access does not require every household to own acreage. Apartment residents, renters, children, older people, and people without cars should still be able to participate in living systems near where they live.

**Who can act:** neighborhoods, schools, land trusts, nonprofits, housing providers, cities, parks agencies, regional conservation bodies.

**Test:** Can an ordinary person regularly reach and participate in a living system without needing wealth, a private yard, or a long trip?

## 2. Build neighborhoods around proximity, not compulsory driving

**Goal:** Give people back time by reducing unnecessary distance.

Cities and regions should make it easier to create enough housing near jobs, schools, shops, transit, parks, childcare, food, and community life.

Useful tools can include accessory dwelling units, duplexes and small multifamily housing, courtyard housing, townhomes, mixed-use buildings, adaptive reuse, infill near services, safe sidewalks and crossings, shade and street trees, protected cycling where conditions justify it, frequent transit, and public seating.

Cars remain useful. The problem is designing ordinary life so that driving is mandatory for nearly every need.

**Who can act:** property owners, developers, cities, transit agencies, regional governments, states where local housing barriers create wider effects.

**Test:** Does the change reduce the amount of human time consumed by reaching ordinary life?

## 3. Treat public space as essential social infrastructure

**Goal:** Make it easier for strangers to become familiar and neighbors to become community.

Every community needs places where people can exist without needing to buy something.

Support and protect libraries, parks, plazas, playgrounds, recreation centers, community rooms, public markets, gardens, trails, shaded seating, public restrooms where feasible, and cultural or musical spaces.

Public space should be safe and usable while remaining genuinely public.

**Who can act:** neighborhoods, civic associations, libraries, schools, cities, parks departments, philanthropy, local businesses around shared spaces.

**Test:** Where can a person go nearby to be around other people without making a purchase?

## 4. Protect human time at work

**Goal:** Work should support a life rather than consume its entire structure.

Employers and public policy should experiment with ways to increase predictability, agency, and usable time without assuming one schedule fits every occupation.

Directions include predictable scheduling where practicable, clear boundaries around off-hours communication, paid leave and caregiving support, flexible scheduling where work allows it, reducing unnecessary meetings and administrative work, worker input into schedules and workflow, measuring productivity rather than visible busyness, and experiments with shorter workweeks or reduced hours when productivity permits.

Different industries have different realities. Emergency medicine, hospitality, construction, caregiving, manufacturing, software, education, and small business cannot be governed by identical assumptions.

**Who can act:** employers, workers, unions, professional associations, labor regulators, public employers.

**Test:** When productivity improves, does any of the gain return to people as time, flexibility, pay, lower prices, ownership, or better services?

## 5. Make technology easier to own, repair, understand, and leave

**Goal:** Technological freedom should include credible exit.

Support right-to-repair principles, interoperable standards, data portability, exportable user-created content, open file formats where practical, reasonable device longevity, security updates that preserve functionality where possible, offline or degraded-mode operation for critical tools, transparent subscription and cancellation practices, and public digital infrastructure built on durable standards.

Open source should be strongly considered for public infrastructure and systems where auditability, continuity, interoperability, or public reuse are important, while recognizing that proprietary systems can also provide value.

**Who can act:** technology companies, standards bodies, public procurement offices, regulators, open-source communities, consumers.

**Test:** Can the user repair it, understand its essential dependencies, export what belongs to them, and leave without losing their entire digital life?

## 6. Make AI increase human agency

**Goal:** Use AI to remove drudgery without making people administratively, economically, or intellectually disposable.

Priority uses include translating complex information into plain language, reducing repetitive paperwork, assisting people with disabilities, expanding access to tutoring and expertise, helping small organizations compete with large bureaucracies, automating dangerous or monotonous work, giving workers better tools, and making public services easier to navigate.

Guardrails should include human review for consequential decisions where appropriate, meaningful disclosure when automated systems materially affect people, appeal paths, privacy and security protections, attention to concentration of infrastructure and ownership, and preservation of human judgment in domains where responsibility matters.

The gains from automation are a political-economic question, not merely a technical one.

**Who can act:** companies, workers, schools, governments, standards bodies, civil society, researchers.

**Test:** Does the AI leave people with more capability and meaningful choice, or mainly transfer power away from them?

## 7. Broaden economic security and ownership

**Goal:** Practical freedom requires both a floor and pathways to build assets.

The Program does not prescribe one universal welfare or ownership model.

It supports testing combinations of simpler access to basic supports, fewer benefit cliffs and unnecessary administrative traps, employee ownership, profit sharing, cooperatives, retirement ownership, small-business formation, community land trusts, savings and asset-building mechanisms, access to tools and productive capital, and competition policy where monopoly or monopsony meaningfully reduces choice.

Security should make experimentation, caregiving, education, entrepreneurship, recovery, and leaving exploitation more possible — not trap people in permanent bureaucratic dependency.

**Who can act:** employers, cooperatives, community finance organizations, cities, states, national governments, regulators, financial institutions.

**Test:** Can ordinary people survive disruption and gradually gain ownership, or do they remain one failure away from losing all credible choices?

## 8. Build abundant, resilient, cleaner energy systems

**Goal:** Preserve the benefits of abundant energy while reducing pollution, fragility, and unnecessary dependence.

Human Scale is technology-neutral about the generation mix. Different regions may use different combinations of nuclear energy, solar, wind, hydroelectric power, geothermal, storage, transmission, demand management, efficiency, distributed generation, microgrids, and backup systems.

The goal is not universal off-grid living. Strong regional grids and local resilience can reinforce one another.

Energy policy should evaluate reliability, affordability, land use, emissions, waste, safety, supply chains, construction time, maintainability, and long-term resilience rather than turning one technology into an identity.

**Who can act:** households, utilities, cooperatives, regulators, cities, regions, states, national governments, grid operators.

**Test:** Is energy reliable and affordable while ecological and systemic risks are being reduced rather than hidden?

## 9. Rebuild food competence and regional food capacity

**Goal:** Keep modern abundance while reconnecting people with food, land, producers, and resilience.

Support school gardens and cooking education, community gardens and orchards, farmers markets, local and regional food hubs, regional cold storage and processing, public-interest agricultural research, soil and water stewardship, seed diversity, safe composting and nutrient cycling, food waste reduction, strong school meal programs, and access to simple nourishing ingredients.

The Program does not prescribe one universal diet or reject global trade. Regional capacity and wider trade can complement each other.

**Who can act:** families, schools, farmers, food businesses, universities, cities, regions, states, national agricultural programs.

**Test:** Can people obtain nourishing food while the productive ecological systems behind that food remain healthy enough to continue?

## 10. Make institutions legible

**Goal:** People should not need a professional interpreter for every interaction with government, healthcare, finance, education, utilities, or employment systems.

Institutions should reduce unnecessary administrative burden through plain-language notices, understandable eligibility rules, visible timelines, fewer duplicate forms, reusable verified information where privacy permits, accessible human support, clear appeals, transparent fees, simple public records, and automation that removes repetitive bureaucracy rather than merely removing people.

Complex systems still require expertise. Complexity inside an institution does not justify avoidable confusion at its public boundary.

**Who can act:** every large institution, especially governments, healthcare systems, schools, banks, insurers, utilities, and major employers.

**Test:** Can a normal person understand what is required, who is responsible, what happens next, and how to challenge an error?

## 11. Give children a more human environment

**Goal:** Childhood should include bodies, nature, play, relationships, competence, curiosity, and increasing autonomy — not only institutional supervision and screens.

Communities and schools should expand access to outdoor play, safe independent mobility appropriate to age and context, gardens and living systems, art and music, practical making and repair, physical activity, unstructured social time, libraries and public space, mentorship across generations, digital literacy that includes attention and privacy, and opportunities to contribute useful work to family and community.

Education should develop literacy, numeracy, scientific reasoning, historical understanding, civic competence, creativity, practical capability, and the ability to learn independently.

**Who can act:** families, schools, libraries, parks, youth organizations, cities, states, education systems.

**Test:** Is childhood producing capable young people with relationships to bodies, places, people, tools, knowledge, and the living world?

## 12. Restore civic power through concrete local participation

**Goal:** Democracy should be something people sometimes do, not only something they watch.

Support participation around understandable, tangible projects: a crossing, park, garden, school, library, housing plan, transit route, local business district, tree canopy, public budget, or neighborhood emergency plan.

Institutions should publish usable agendas, responsibilities, budgets, decisions, and results.

Civic systems should make it possible to participate without politics consuming a person's whole identity or life.

**Who can act:** residents, civic groups, cities, counties, school districts, public agencies, elected officials, local media.

**Test:** Can ordinary people discover who controls a local issue, understand the decision, participate, and see what happened afterward?

# What government should guarantee — and where it should be restrained

The doctrine does not yet claim a final list of universal guarantees.

The Program begins with a direction:

Government is most justified where it protects rights, coordinates genuinely shared infrastructure, provides or finances public goods that markets chronically underprovide, protects people from coercion or severe external harms, maintains fair rules, and provides a credible foundation of social order and material security.

Government should be restrained where coercion is unnecessary, where voluntary institutions can solve the problem well, where uniform rules erase useful local variation, or where administrative power becomes unreviewable and self-perpetuating.

The question is neither "government or no government" nor "public or private."

It is:

**What institution can solve this problem competently while preserving rights, accountability, pluralism, and practical freedom?**

# A Human Scale policy test

Before supporting a major proposal, ask:

1. What human problem is this actually trying to solve?
2. What evidence says the problem exists and what do we still not know?
3. What is the smallest scale capable of solving it effectively?
4. Which rights must remain protected regardless of local preference?
5. Who gains power if this policy succeeds?
6. Who loses power or bears new costs?
7. What happens to human time?
8. What happens to family and community life?
9. What happens to ownership and practical exit?
10. What happens to soil, water, energy, climate, habitat, and future generations?
11. Can ordinary people understand the rules?
12. Can small organizations comply and participate?
13. Is there a way to pilot or phase the policy?
14. How will results be measured?
15. What would cause us to change or repeal it?

# The first level of action

The Program does not require national political power to begin.

A Human Scale project can start with one garden, one block, one employer, one school, one library, one open-source tool, one cooperative, one safer crossing, one public meeting, one food forest, one repair event, one neighborhood resilience plan, or one local pilot.

The smallest project can produce evidence for the larger argument.

# What this Program is not

It is not a command to return to the past.

It is not anti-city, anti-market, anti-government, anti-car, or anti-AI.

It is not a claim that small institutions are automatically virtuous.

It is not a promise that every human value can be maximized at once.

Human Scale means **systems must justify themselves by the lives they make possible.**

# The direction

A Human Scale society would still have advanced medicine, computers, universities, global science, large infrastructure, trade, industry, dense cities, powerful energy systems, and technologies we have not yet imagined.

But more people would also have time, land access, healthy food, useful skills, durable relationships, meaningful ownership, understandable institutions, public space, quieter technology, resilient communities, and the ability to influence the systems that structure their lives.

The political project is not to make civilization smaller for its own sake.

It is to make civilization **answerable to the human beings living inside it.**

> Build capability without captivity.  
> Build abundance without disconnection.  
> Build institutions without making people disappear inside them.

**The purpose of the Program is to move from coping with human mismatch to redesigning the conditions that create it.**
