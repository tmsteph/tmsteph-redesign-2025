Governance

Power close enough to understand — subsidiarity, rights, capable institutions, civic participation, public technology, and accountability.

Power should be exercised at the smallest scale that can solve the problem well — and larger scales should remain accountable to the people whose lives they shape.

The starting point

Shared life requires courts, infrastructure, rules, public safety, water, standards, schools, emergency response, and ways to resolve conflict.

The goal is not “small government” or “large government” as an identity. It is government at the right scale, with enough capacity to succeed and enough accountability to remain human.
Part I — Diagnosis

Scale changes power

Local

Often easier to understand and influence, but still vulnerable to capture, exclusion, or pushing costs onto neighbors.

Large scale

Can protect rights, pool risk, and coordinate infrastructure, while also becoming distant and difficult to navigate.

Complexity

Expertise is necessary, but citizen-facing systems should remain legible enough to comply, appeal, and participate.

Capacity

Weak institutions fail people too. Accountability without capacity frustrates; capacity without accountability dominates.

Rights protect the person from the group

Community and democracy matter, but majorities and local institutions can be unjust. Humane governance needs durable protections for basic liberties, due process, privacy, equal treatment, belief, speech, and association.

Belonging must never require compulsory conformity.

Administrative burden is a political cost

Forms, repeated proofs, confusing eligibility, inaccessible offices, long waits, and opaque appeals can turn public systems into obstacles — especially for people with the least time or institutional knowledge.

Part II — Field Guide

Practice concrete citizenship

Find the real decision-maker

Learn whether the issue belongs to a city, county, school district, state, federal agency, utility, court, landlord, employer, or private platform.

Show up repeatedly

Know representatives, agendas, meetings, and local institutions. Repeated participation often matters more than one burst of outrage.

Work on real objects

A crossing, garden, library, permit, school rule, transit link, tree, park, or public space can unite people across ideology.

Keep relationships

Strong disagreement should not require permanent civic war. Democratic life depends on being able to meet again after losing a vote.

Part III — Program

Subsidiarity with evidence

Solve a problem at the smallest scale that can solve it effectively while protecting rights and accounting for consequences beyond that scale.

A neighborhood can manage some shared places. A city can design streets. Regions may need to coordinate transit or water. Nations may need to guarantee rights, currency, defense, or social insurance. Some ecological and cross-border problems require international coordination.

Build legible, capable institutions

Plain language

Rules, responsibilities, notices, timelines, budgets, contracts, and appeals should be understandable.

Less friction

Reduce duplicate forms and repetitive proof. Use automation to remove bureaucracy before removing human support.

Expertise + consent

Experts should expose evidence, uncertainty, assumptions, and tradeoffs without replacing democratic legitimacy.

Reversible experiments

Use pilots, sunsets, phased changes, and published results when uncertainty is high.

Emergency limits

Exceptional powers need clear triggers, scope, oversight, review, and expiration.

Anti-corruption

Open records, audits, conflict rules, procurement transparency, and independent oversight make capture harder.

Public technology should preserve agency

Digital government should favor interoperability, accessible records, security, privacy, and alternatives to one compulsory proprietary channel. AI can help translate, summarize, route, and reduce administrative work, but consequential public decisions should remain reviewable by humans.

A Human Scale governance test

What problem is being solved?
What is the smallest scale capable of solving it well?
What consequences cross that boundary?
Which rights must be protected regardless of local preference?
Can ordinary people understand who is responsible?
Can people participate or appeal without excessive time or expertise?
Does the institution have enough capacity to do its job?
Are expertise and evidence visible without becoming unaccountable rule?
Can the policy be tested, reviewed, and changed?
What prevents corruption, capture, or permanent emergency power?

The deeper idea

Power becomes dangerous when it is too distant to understand, too concentrated to contest, or too weak to perform the work assigned to it.

A humane political order should let neighborhoods solve neighborhood problems, cities solve city problems, and larger institutions solve the problems that genuinely require scale.

Power should live as close to the people as the problem allows — and remain accountable wherever it must grow larger.
