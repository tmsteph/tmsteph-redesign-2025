# Chapter 6: Energy — Power Without Dependence

**Revised after Evidence Audit 06 — August 2026**

> A Human Scale energy system should provide abundant useful energy while minimizing pollution and catastrophic risk, maintaining affordability, and surviving credible failures.

Modern energy is one of civilization's great liberating capabilities.

Reliable power supports:

- hospitals;
- refrigeration;
- water and sanitation;
- lighting;
- heating and cooling;
- communications;
- transportation;
- computation;
- research;
- education;
- manufacturing;
- food systems;
- emergency response.

Human Scale therefore rejects romantic energy poverty.

It also rejects the idea that energy abundance justifies ignoring:

- air pollution;
- greenhouse-gas emissions;
- habitat and land impacts;
- waste;
- safety;
- catastrophic risk;
- unaffordable systems;
- brittle dependencies.

The central correction from Evidence Audit 06 is simple:

**energy performance is system performance.**

A fuel, generator, battery, rooftop, reactor, grid, or microgrid cannot be evaluated in isolation from the network and failure modes around it.

# Part I — Diagnosis

## 1. Abundance Is a Value, Not Waste

Human Scale favors enough energy to support modern capability and future growth.

That does not mean maximizing consumption for its own sake.

A useful definition of abundance is:

> **enough reliable, affordable, lower-pollution energy to support health, comfort, mobility, industry, science, computation, food, water, and new human capability without treating waste as progress.**

Efficiency and abundance can reinforce one another.

A more efficient building needs less energy for the same comfort.

That saved capacity can reduce cost, reduce pollution, serve other loads, or make electrification easier.

## 2. Pollution Must Enter the Cost Model

A cheap energy source is not actually cheap if major costs are shifted into:

- disease;
- dirty air;
- climate risk;
- contaminated water;
- damaged ecosystems;
- cleanup;
- future generations.

Likewise, a low-emissions system that becomes prohibitively expensive or unreliable creates real human costs.

Human Scale therefore evaluates:

- monetary cost;
- reliability;
- health;
- emissions;
- safety;
- land;
- materials;
- institutional requirements;
- resilience;
- distribution of burdens.

## 3. Reliability Is a System Property

Reliability comes from portfolios and operations.

It depends on combinations of:

- generation;
- transmission;
- distribution;
- storage;
- reserves;
- fuel;
- flexible demand;
- forecasting;
- maintenance;
- operator practice;
- voltage/frequency control;
- weatherization;
- cybersecurity;
- physical security;
- interconnection.

A reliable system can contain variable generation.

A system full of dispatchable generators can still fail.

The relevant question is not:

> Is this technology reliable?

It is:

> **What role does this resource play in a reliable system, and what happens when it or something around it fails?**

## 4. Variable Renewables Create Integration Needs

Wind and solar vary with weather and time.

That is a physical characteristic, not a political insult.

High shares can require different combinations of:

- geographic diversity;
- transmission;
- batteries;
- longer-duration storage;
- hydro;
- nuclear;
- geothermal;
- other firm generation;
- demand response;
- sector coupling;
- forecasting;
- overbuilding and curtailment.

The best combination depends on place and system.

“Renewables are unreliable” is too crude.

“Storage solves everything” is too crude too.

## 5. Nuclear Is a Tool, Not an Identity

Nuclear can provide large quantities of firm low-carbon electricity.

That makes it a legitimate Human Scale option.

It also creates real requirements around:

- capital;
- construction delivery;
- regulation;
- operational safety;
- security;
- waste;
- decommissioning;
- cooling;
- public legitimacy.

Some jurisdictions may execute nuclear projects well.

Others may find different portfolios cheaper, faster, or easier to govern.

Human Scale owes no loyalty to nuclear power and no loyalty to excluding it.

## 6. Renewable Does Not Mean Impact-Free

Wind, solar, hydro, geothermal, biomass, batteries, and transmission require physical systems.

They can involve:

- land;
- habitat;
- rivers;
- mining;
- manufacturing;
- roads;
- waste;
- recycling;
- transmission corridors;
- community consent.

Low-carbon technology should not be judged as though its impacts are zero.

It should be compared against realistic alternatives over full lifecycles and whole systems.

## 7. Strong Grids Can Be Human Scale

A continental or regional grid is physically large.

It can still serve Human Scale values when scale produces real benefits:

- geographic diversity;
- resource sharing;
- lower reserve requirements;
- access to distant generation;
- lower costs;
- mutual assistance;
- electrification;
- multiple supply paths.

Large networks can also create:

- correlated failure;
- long permitting timelines;
- concentrated decision-making;
- wildfire or storm exposure;
- cybersecurity risks;
- difficult siting conflicts.

The doctrine's scale rule works here:

**network scale should justify itself through network benefit.**

## 8. Local Capability Is Not the Same as Isolation

Local energy can include:

- rooftop solar;
- batteries;
- generators;
- district energy;
- community solar;
- microgrids;
- thermal storage;
- demand management.

These can increase resilience in some failures.

But a grid-tied solar system that automatically shuts down during an outage may provide no backup.

A battery that is empty when an emergency begins may provide little benefit.

A generator without maintained fuel may fail.

Local resilience needs:

- critical-load definition;
- islanding where required;
- controls;
- maintenance;
- testing;
- fuel or stored energy;
- realistic outage scenarios.

The goal is not independence from everyone.

It is **diversified dependency with credible fallback.**

## 9. Efficiency Is Capacity

Energy efficiency can reduce the resources needed to provide:

- comfort;
- lighting;
- transport;
- industrial output;
- computing;
- refrigeration.

Human Scale values efficiency because needless waste consumes money, infrastructure, fuel, and ecological capacity.

But efficiency is not a substitute for building enough clean energy and networks.

Growing societies, electrification, AI/data centers, industry, climate adaptation, and new technologies can increase demand even as devices become more efficient.

## 10. Affordability Is Part of Energy Justice

Energy poverty can make:

- heat dangerous;
- cooling unaffordable;
- transportation inaccessible;
- food more expensive;
- industry uncompetitive;
- housing harder to maintain.

Energy policy therefore has distributional effects.

Human Scale asks:

- Who pays capital costs?
- Who receives subsidies?
- Who bears pollution?
- Who receives reliability?
- Who can install local systems?
- Who rents and cannot?
- Who lives near infrastructure?
- Who benefits from new investment?

Affordability should not be added after the engineering is finished.

# Part II — Field Guide

## 11. Know Your Critical Loads

In an outage, not every appliance is equally important.

Depending on the household, critical loads might include:

- medical equipment;
- refrigeration;
- phone charging;
- lighting;
- heating or cooling;
- internet/communication;
- mobility equipment;
- pumps;
- cooking.

Resilience planning starts by identifying the actual need.

## 12. Know What Your Backup Can Really Do

If you have solar, batteries, a generator, vehicle-to-load capability, or another backup system, understand:

- whether it works during a grid outage;
- how long it lasts;
- what loads it can support;
- how it is maintained;
- fuel requirements;
- safe operation;
- how often it is tested.

Do not count theoretical backup as real resilience.

## 13. Reduce Waste Before Buying Complexity

Air sealing, insulation, efficient appliances, heat-pump systems, controls, shade, and maintenance can sometimes reduce required energy more cheaply than adding generation or storage.

The optimal order is site-specific.

Human Scale prefers competence over gadget accumulation.

## 14. Learn Your Energy System

Know something about:

- local utility structure;
- generation mix;
- outage risks;
- peak demand;
- wildfire/storm risks;
- major transmission constraints;
- energy prices;
- available resilience programs;
- evacuation or emergency plans.

You do not need to become a grid engineer.

The purpose is to make critical dependency less mysterious.

# Part III — Program

## 15. Build Portfolios, Not Identities

Energy planning should compare realistic portfolios across:

- cost;
- reliability;
- emissions;
- health;
- land/material impact;
- build time;
- permitting;
- workforce;
- supply chains;
- fuel risk;
- resilience;
- public acceptance;
- institutional capacity.

Technology labels are inputs, not conclusions.

## 16. Expand Grids Where the Network Benefit Justifies It

Transmission and distribution investment can unlock:

- clean generation;
- geographic diversity;
- electrification;
- mutual assistance;
- lower congestion;
- new housing and industry.

Projects should still address:

- habitat;
- landowners;
- Indigenous rights where applicable;
- wildfire;
- visual impacts;
- community benefit;
- cost allocation;
- permitting legitimacy.

## 17. Build Local Resilience Around Critical Functions

Microgrids and distributed resources make most sense where the value of continuity is high or the larger network is weak.

Potential priorities include:

- hospitals;
- shelters;
- emergency services;
- water systems;
- communications;
- community resilience hubs;
- remote areas;
- medically vulnerable households.

Test systems under realistic outage conditions rather than relying on nameplate capacity.

## 18. Keep Nuclear in the Comparison Set

Jurisdictions with the institutional capacity, public legitimacy, financing, workforce, sites, and project discipline to build or retain nuclear power should be able to evaluate it alongside alternatives.

Jurisdictions should not be forced into nuclear because it is “serious” or forced away from it because it is “old.”

The same standard applies to renewables.

## 19. Protect Affordability During Transition

Decarbonization policies should track:

- household energy burden;
- industrial cost;
- reliability;
- tax/subsidy distribution;
- stranded assets;
- workforce effects;
- infrastructure investment;
- low-income impacts.

Transition speed matters.

So does whether the transition remains politically and materially durable.

## 20. Price External Costs Where Institutions Can Do So Legitimately

Pollution and climate costs should not remain invisible simply because they occur outside the utility bill.

Potential mechanisms vary by jurisdiction and can include:

- emissions standards;
- carbon pricing;
- clean-energy standards;
- pollution regulation;
- technology incentives;
- public investment;
- procurement.

Human Scale does not canonize one mechanism.

Compare effectiveness, burden, administrative complexity, innovation effects, leakage, and political durability.

## 21. A Human Scale Energy Test

1. What human service is the system trying to provide?
2. Is supply reliable under normal conditions?
3. What failures matter most?
4. What critical loads must survive?
5. What is the total system cost?
6. Who pays?
7. What pollution and climate impacts occur over the lifecycle?
8. What land, water, habitat, and material impacts occur?
9. What institutions and workforce are required?
10. How quickly can the system be built?
11. What maintenance does it require?
12. Does local capability add real resilience?
13. Does interconnection add real resilience?
14. Are energy burdens acceptable for low-income households?
15. Can the system expand for future demand?
16. What evidence would make us change the portfolio?

# The Direction

We should not build an energy civilization around guilt for using energy.

We should build one around competence.

Use less where waste adds no value.

Build more where energy expands health, comfort, mobility, science, production, resilience, and possibility.

Cut pollution where we can.

Track the pollution we cannot yet eliminate.

Keep grids where networks make us stronger.

Build local fallback where local capability genuinely matters.

Use nuclear where it performs.

Use renewables where they perform.

Build storage, transmission, demand flexibility, efficiency, geothermal, hydro, emerging technologies, or other resources where the system needs them.

And change the portfolio when reality proves a better one.

**The goal is not less power. It is enough clean, reliable, affordable power for human possibility — with the humility to engineer the system rather than worship the technology.**
