# Human Scale Field Guide

**Version 0.1 — August 2026**

> Start by making ordinary life more compatible with being human.

The Human Scale Doctrine asks what kind of civilization human beings need.

This Field Guide asks a more immediate question:

**What can I do inside the world that exists right now?**

This is not a purity test, a productivity system, an anti-modern lifestyle, or a demand that everyone move to the country.

It is a practical companion for recovering some of the conditions that human life repeatedly seems to need: body, time, relationships, living systems, useful work, agency, beauty, rest, competence, and meaning.

The aim is not to optimize every hour.

The aim is to make a good life easier to inhabit.

---

## 1. Start with the environment, not self-blame

When something repeatedly feels difficult, ask whether the surrounding environment is making the desired behavior hard.

Instead of only asking:

- Why do I keep looking at my phone?
- Why do I never exercise?
- Why do we eat badly when we are tired?
- Why do I never see my friends?
- Why do I feel disconnected from nature?

also ask:

- What is always within reach?
- What is physically nearby?
- What does the schedule permit?
- What requires driving, planning, money, or permission?
- What does the home make easy by default?
- Which systems are constantly asking for attention?

Willpower matters.

But design matters too.

**Change the default where you can.**

---

## 2. The daily anchors

You do not need a perfect routine.

Try to keep several basic forms of human life present in an ordinary day.

### Body

Move in ways that belong naturally inside the day.

Walk. Carry things. Stretch. Play. Work with your hands. Take stairs. Practice yoga. Exercise deliberately when useful.

Do not make movement exist only inside a special one-hour container called a workout.

### Outside

Spend some time under the real sky when circumstances allow.

Notice daylight, temperature, wind, clouds, trees, birds, water, soil, and the actual place where you live.

### Food

Eat at least some food that still resembles ingredients.

Cook something when you can.

Share food with another person when possible.

Do not turn nourishment into a moral performance.

### Living things

Touch the living world directly.

Water a plant. Grow herbs. Tend a garden. Care for an animal. Sit under a tree. Notice insects. Compost. Walk through a park. Learn the name of something growing nearby.

### People

Have at least one interaction that is not merely transactional.

Talk. Eat together. Play. Help. Ask for help. Listen. Make something together.

### Attention

Create at least one part of the day where no machine is continuously trying to redirect you.

The phone does not have to disappear.

It should sometimes become quiet.

### Inner life

Leave some room for silence, prayer, meditation, yoga, music, journaling, contemplation, philosophy, gratitude, or simple presence.

No single practice is mandatory.

The point is to remember that consciousness is more than incoming information.

### Sleep and ending

Let the day have an ending.

Work, news, messages, entertainment, and administration should not automatically expand until sleep is the only remaining boundary.

## 3. Make the home support the life you want

A home is not only a shelter. It is an environment that repeatedly shapes behavior.

Look for small changes that reduce friction.

Examples:

- make useful food easy to see and prepare
- keep a few reliable staple meals available
- keep walking shoes, bikes, instruments, books, tools, or art supplies accessible
- create a place where devices can become quiet
- give plants real space
- make one place comfortable for sitting together
- keep basic repair tools where they can actually be used
- reduce clutter that repeatedly consumes attention
- keep modest emergency supplies for outages or disruption
- make outdoor space useful when any is available
- create a small ritual place for prayer, meditation, reading, stretching, or quiet

Do not wait for the ideal house.

A windowsill, balcony, front step, courtyard, nearby park, library, community garden, or neighborhood can become part of the home environment too.

---

## 4. Recover a relationship with land and life

Private acreage is not required.

The practical goal is **meaningful participation in the living world.**

Start where you are:

- grow one edible or useful plant
- learn where the nearest community garden is
- use parks repeatedly instead of only as occasional destinations
- visit a farmers market, farm, orchard, nursery, or garden
- learn a few local plants, birds, trees, or seasonal patterns
- participate in a cleanup, planting day, garden, habitat project, or food forest
- let children participate in soil, water, plants, cooking, and animal care when appropriate
- support access to gardens and living land for people who do not own property

A plant you care for changes nature from scenery into relationship.

---

## 5. Make food a little less abstract

You do not need culinary perfection or one correct diet.

Build competence instead.

A useful household food system might include:

- a handful of flexible meals you can make without much thought
- basic pantry foods
- some fresh ingredients
- one or two meals worth making together
- simple knowledge of storage and leftovers
- a connection to at least one place food is grown or sold directly
- one thing you grow yourself if possible

When choosing food, ask more than whether it is convenient.

Ask whether it nourishes, whether you understand roughly what it is, whether it fits the household, and whether the system can be sustained without making every meal a project.

**A good food practice should make life more livable, not more anxious.**

---

## 6. Protect human time from work

Employment can be meaningful and necessary.

It should not automatically claim every expandable part of life.

Look for the hidden work around work:

- commuting
- preparation
- scheduling
- unpaid messages
- recovering from exhaustion
- arranging childcare
- administrative tasks
- constantly thinking about work after leaving it

Then ask what can be changed.

Possible moves include:

- protect a real end to the workday when you can
- batch administrative tasks instead of letting them fragment the whole day
- treat commuting time as part of the real cost of a job
- negotiate predictable schedules where possible
- automate bureaucracy before automating the meaningful parts of your work
- build skills, savings, relationships, or ownership that increase your ability to leave bad work
- keep at least one useful or creative activity in life that is not measured by an employer

**Your paid role is one part of your contribution, not the definition of your worth.**

---

## 7. Use technology; do not live inside it

Technology can remove enormous amounts of friction.

Use that power deliberately.

Try a technology audit:

### Keep

Tools that save real time, increase capability, connect you to people, support creativity, improve accessibility, help you learn, remove drudgery, or make important systems easier to navigate.

### Quiet

Tools that are useful but interrupt too often.

Turn off unnecessary notifications. Move distracting apps. Create device-free situations. Batch messages. Make quiet the default when immediate response is not actually required.

### Replace

Prefer tools that preserve agency when reasonable:

- repairable products
- exportable data
- interoperable formats
- open standards
- offline capability
- local copies of important information
- software and hardware you can leave without losing your life inside them

### Remove

Some tools create less value than the attention they consume.

You are allowed to stop using them.

### AI

Use AI to reduce bureaucracy, learn, create, plan, translate, explore, and expand capability.

Try not to outsource every judgment, relationship, creative impulse, or difficult thought merely because a machine can answer quickly.

**Use the machine. Receive the benefit. Return to life.**

---

## 8. Build community through repetition

Community usually grows slowly.

Do not begin by trying to create a movement.

Begin by becoming a familiar person somewhere.

- use the same park, library, cafe, market, garden, congregation, class, club, or public place repeatedly
- learn the names of neighbors
- invite someone to eat
- offer a tool, ride, meal, skill, introduction, or hour of help
- let other people help you too
- make music, garden, cook, repair, exercise, worship, volunteer, or build something with other people
- stay in some relationships long enough to survive disagreement
- protect privacy and leave relationships or groups that become coercive or unsafe

A healthy community is not constant togetherness.

It is a web of relationships strong enough that people are not completely alone when life becomes difficult.

---

## 9. Build practical economic freedom

Money is not the purpose of Human Scale.

But chronic financial fragility can make nearly every other part of life harder.

Where circumstances allow, move toward more margin:

- understand recurring expenses
- reduce fixed costs that create little real value
- keep some emergency reserve
- avoid unnecessary high-cost debt
- insure catastrophic risks where appropriate
- build useful skills
- own durable tools rather than repeatedly renting dependence when ownership makes sense
- build savings or productive ownership over time
- support small and local businesses when they genuinely provide good value
- create something useful that another person may willingly pay for

The goal is not wealth as status.

The goal is **enough security and ownership to have credible choices.**

---

## 10. Treat the neighborhood as part of your life

Walk your ordinary radius when practical.

Notice:

- where you can sit
- where there is shade
- where children can play
- where food is available
- where people gather without paying
- where crossings feel dangerous
- where trees or gardens could exist
- which trips require a car only because of design
- which public places feel loved and which feel abandoned

Then improve one small thing if you can.

A planter, bench, safer route, shared table, repaired gate, neighborhood cleanup, library visit, garden bed, shade tree, public comment, or recurring gathering can matter.

Human-scale civilization is partly built through a thousand small acts of making places worth inhabiting.

---

## 11. Leave room for spiritual and inner life

Human Scale does not require one theology.

It does insist that a human being is more than a consumer, worker, voter, data point, or biological machine.

Make room for practices that reconnect you with reality beyond constant economic and informational demand.

That may include:

- prayer
- meditation
- yoga
- scripture
- philosophy
- music
- art
- silence
- ritual
- service
- gratitude
- time in nature
- contemplation of death, love, God, consciousness, beauty, or meaning

The practice matters less than whether it genuinely returns you to attention, humility, relationship, and life.

## 12. A simple weekly rhythm

Human Scale should not require a complicated planner.

A week can be enough if it regularly contains:

### Daily

- some movement
- some outside time
- nourishing food
- direct human contact
- some contact with living systems
- a period of quieter attention
- an ending to the day

### Weekly

- a shared meal
- meaningful time in nature or a garden
- a longer period of movement or physical work
- maintenance or repair of something you depend on
- time with friends, family, neighbors, or community
- a period not organized around buying, working, or scrolling
- a short look at the week ahead

### Monthly

- review household money and recurring costs
- repair, donate, recycle, or remove something accumulating around you
- check basic household resilience
- examine which technologies are taking more than they give
- participate in one civic, neighborhood, school, garden, association, or community issue when something needs attention

This is a menu, not a scorecard.

## 13. The minimum viable Human Scale day

Some days are chaotic.

When everything else fails, make the standard smaller.

Try to do a few of these:

- step outside
- move your body
- eat one decent meal
- speak with someone you care about
- interact with a plant, animal, tree, garden, or other living thing
- spend a few quiet minutes without incoming information
- do one useful thing with your hands
- let the day end

You do not need to win the day.

You need to remain inside your life.

## 14. The seven-day Human Scale reset

Use this when life feels excessively abstract, digital, fragmented, or reactive.

### Day 1 — Observe

Do not fix everything.

Notice what repeatedly consumes time, attention, money, movement, or emotional energy.

Write down the three largest points of friction.

### Day 2 — Body

Spend more of the day physically inhabiting your life.

Walk, stretch, exercise, work with your hands, go outside, and notice where your normal environment encourages or discourages movement.

### Day 3 — Food & life

Cook something simple. Eat with someone if possible. Touch soil or care for something living. Identify one way food could become easier and less abstract in your household.

### Day 4 — Technology

Turn off notifications that do not deserve immediate access to you. Remove one low-value digital demand. Use technology intentionally for something genuinely useful.

### Day 5 — Place

Walk the neighborhood or ordinary daily route. Notice one place worth protecting and one small thing that would make the area more human.

### Day 6 — People

Call, visit, help, invite, join, or show up somewhere repeatedly. Strengthen one relationship rather than consuming more information about relationships.

### Day 7 — Design the next week

Choose only a few changes worth carrying forward.

A good starting set is:

1. **one daily anchor**
2. **one weekly relationship or nature practice**
3. **one environmental change that makes the desired behavior easier**

Keep those before adding more.

## 15. From personal friction to public action

Some problems cannot be solved by personal habits.

If thousands of people are individually compensating for the same bad environment, the environment may be the thing that needs to change.

Keep a **friction log**.

Examples:

- a dangerous crossing everyone avoids
- apartments with no practical access to growing space
- work schedules that make childcare or family meals nearly impossible
- public benefits that require repeated confusing paperwork
- a neighborhood with nowhere to gather without spending money
- devices that cannot be repaired
- a bus route that is too infrequent to trust
- a school with no shade or garden
- food that is available only through long trips
- public land that could support trees, habitat, gardens, or food forests

Then ask:

1. Who actually controls this?
2. At what scale can it be changed?
3. Who else experiences the same problem?
4. What is the smallest concrete improvement worth testing?
5. How would we know whether it worked?

Personal adaptation is useful.

**It should not become an excuse for permanent institutional failure.**

---

## 16. A Human Scale life test

Occasionally ask:

- Am I spending enough of my life inside my body rather than only inside information?
- Do I regularly encounter sunlight, weather, plants, animals, soil, water, or open sky?
- Do I have people I can rely on and people who can rely on me?
- Is work supporting my life or consuming nearly all of it?
- Does technology expand my agency more than it captures my attention?
- Can I cook, repair, navigate, create, or care for some of the things I depend on?
- Do I have enough financial margin to make real choices?
- Does my home make healthy and meaningful behavior easier?
- Does my neighborhood offer any real public life?
- Is there room for prayer, meditation, art, music, silence, philosophy, or wonder?
- Which recurring problem in my life is actually a systems problem?
- What could become simpler?

The point is not a perfect score.

The point is to notice direction.

---

## 17. Do not make Human Scale another optimization religion

There is a trap hidden inside every philosophy of better living.

It can become one more impossible standard.

A person begins worrying about perfect food, perfect sleep, perfect technology use, perfect community, perfect parenting, perfect exercise, perfect spirituality, perfect finances, and perfect ecological behavior.

That is not the goal.

Human Scale is supposed to reduce unnecessary pressure, not create a new form of it.

Life includes illness, conflict, screens, deadlines, convenience food, long drives, bad weeks, dependence, institutional complexity, and things we cannot control.

Use modern systems when they help.

Accept help.

Buy convenience when you need it.

Use the car.

Use the computer.

Use the hospital.

Use the grid.

Use AI.

Then keep asking whether these tools are helping you return to life or steadily replacing it.

**A good life is not another project-management system.**

---

## The direction

Start close.

Move your body.

Go outside.

Grow something.

Cook something.

Know somebody.

Make something.

Repair something.

Protect some time.

Quiet the machine sometimes.

Pay attention to what is alive.

Build enough material security to have choices.

Improve one place you actually inhabit.

Join with other people when the problem is larger than you.

And leave room for mystery.

**Human Scale begins wherever a human being stops treating life as an abstraction and starts participating in it again.**
