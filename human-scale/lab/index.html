Public workbench

Human Scale Lab

Evidence audits, personal observations, local civic projects, and design experiments that can strengthen, weaken, or change the doctrine.

The doctrine should produce experiments, not only opinions.

How the Lab works

1. Name the friction. What recurring human problem are we actually observing?
2. Change one thing. What is the smallest intervention worth testing?
3. Measure something real. Time, use, safety, cost, sleep, participation, burden, temperature, errors, access, or another outcome tied to the claim.
4. Record failure too. A failed experiment is useful if it changes what we believe or build.
5. Feed it back. Update the Field Guide, Program, Evidence Atlas, or doctrine when the result deserves it.
Not everything should be experimented on casually. Do not run unsafe medical, psychological, environmental, engineering, or human-subject experiments. Use professional and regulatory safeguards where they are required.

Active experiments

01 · Product

Calibrate the Human Scale Audit

Find confusing questions, missing domains, false precision, cultural bias, double-counting, and cases where the total score hides the useful story.

Failure condition: if the 0–100 score feels falsely authoritative, turn the Audit into a profile instead.

Try the Audit →
02 · Time

One Week of Human Time

Track where waking time actually goes before optimizing it: paid work, commuting, care, household labor, bureaucracy, relationships, making, recovery, entertainment, and unstructured time.

Question: which burdens are imposed rather than chosen?

03 · Technology

Quiet Technology

Keep a useful service but remove nonessential notifications, badges, feed shortcuts, and interruptions for one week.

Measure: useful tasks, missed information, checking, time, frustration, and benefit preserved.

04 · Community

Third-Place Inventory

Map where people can linger without spending much: libraries, parks, plazas, gardens, community rooms, beaches, recreation, and inexpensive gathering places.

Look for: shade, seating, bathrooms, accessibility, children, transit, cost, and repeated encounter.

05 · Land

Living-World Access Audit

Measure usable shade, outdoor space, gardens, habitat, renter access, soil safety, heat, walking routes, maintenance, and children’s access.

Distinction: usable access versus symbolic acreage.

06 · Institutions

Bureaucracy Receipt

Record forms, duplicate fields, calls, hold time, logins, documents, transfers, trips, delays, errors, appeals, and active human time for one real process.

Goal: distinguish safety/accountability from friction created by poor coordination.

07 · Local

Local Project, Real Receipt

Document one physical project—crossing, shade, garden, repair event, bench, tool library, meal, restoration, walking group—from problem through approvals, labor, maintenance, outcome, opposition, and failure.

Rule: one successful project is implementation evidence, not proof of a worldview.

What counts as a useful contribution?

Counterexample

A case where a Human Scale principle predicts the wrong thing.

Better evidence

A primary source, dataset, replication, failed replication, or methodological criticism.

Failed experiment

A garden, schedule, technology, localism, policy, or community intervention that created more burden than benefit.

Real receipt

Costs, time, approvals, participation, maintenance, before/after measures, and what actually happened.

Accessibility critique

A place where “healthy,” “local,” “walkable,” “independent,” or “simple” assumes an able-bodied or culturally narrow user.

Prototype

A calmer interface, local map, policy draft, civic tool, data visualization, open-source implementation, or physical design.

The point

Human Scale should become a network of claims, experiments, local projects, evidence audits, and design patterns—not only a book.

The project does not need applause as much as it needs information that changes what we build.
