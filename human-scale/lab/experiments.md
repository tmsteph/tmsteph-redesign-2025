# Human Scale Lab

**Version 0.1 — August 2026**

> The doctrine should produce experiments, not only opinions.

The Human Scale Lab is the project’s public workbench.

It connects four kinds of work:

1. **Evidence audits** — pressure-test claims and revise the doctrine when needed.
2. **Personal experiments** — low-risk ways to observe whether changing an environment changes daily life.
3. **Local experiments** — small civic or community projects that can generate evidence for larger ideas.
4. **Design experiments** — technology, policy, workplace, school, land, and institutional prototypes.

The Lab is not a collection of “life hacks.”

It exists to turn recurring friction into questions that can be observed, tested, compared, and shared.

---

# Experiment Rules

A Human Scale experiment should try to answer:

1. What problem are we observing?
2. What is the smallest change worth testing?
3. What outcome do we expect?
4. What could go wrong?
5. Who bears the work or risk?
6. What would count as improvement?
7. What would count as failure?
8. Can the change be reversed?
9. What did we learn that should change the doctrine, Field Guide, or Program?

Do not manipulate people without their knowledge.

Do not perform unsafe medical, psychological, environmental, or engineering experiments.

Do not use children or vulnerable people as experimental subjects without appropriate safeguards.

Where professional or regulatory expertise is required, use it.

# Active Experiment 01 — Calibrate the Human Scale Audit

**Question:** Does the current 15-question Audit surface meaningful friction without pretending to be a scientific diagnostic?

Public tool:
https://tmsteph.com/human-scale/audit/

Current hypotheses:

- people can distinguish between personal habits and environmental constraints after completing the Audit;
- domain-level results are more useful than the total score;
- some questions are probably double-counting related concepts;
- weighting every question equally is unlikely to be the final design;
- the Audit may need different versions for individuals, households, workplaces, neighborhoods, and cities.

What to collect:

- confusing questions
- missing domains
- questions that feel judgmental or culturally narrow
- domains that overlap too much
- examples where a low score is actually healthy or a high score hides a problem
- completion friction

What would change the tool:

If users consistently report that the total score feels falsely authoritative, de-emphasize or remove the total score and make the tool a profile instead.

# Active Experiment 02 — One Week of Human Time

**Question:** Where does a normal week actually go?

For seven days, roughly classify waking time into:

- paid work
- commuting
- household labor
- caregiving
- bureaucracy / errands
- recovery from work
- sleep preparation
- relationships
- movement
- food preparation / eating
- learning / making
- civic / community life
- entertainment
- unstructured time

Do not optimize during the week.

At the end ask:

- Which categories were chosen?
- Which were imposed?
- Which created energy?
- Which required recovery?
- What recurring burden could not be solved by “better time management”?
- What would one hour returned to the week actually be used for?

This experiment can inform the Work & Time audit.

# Active Experiment 03 — Quiet Technology

**Question:** Can a device or service preserve its useful function while demanding less attention?

Pick one technology you use frequently.

For one week, change only the attention layer:

- disable nonessential notifications;
- remove badges;
- remove one infinite-feed shortcut from the home screen;
- use direct search/bookmarks instead of recommendation feeds where practical;
- batch one category of messages;
- keep the underlying useful service available.

Observe:

- tasks completed
- missed important information
- compulsive checking
- time spent
- frustration
- benefit lost
- benefit preserved

The goal is not “less screen time” as an identity.

The question is whether the technology can become quieter without becoming less useful.

# Active Experiment 04 — Third-Place Inventory

**Question:** Where can people in a neighborhood be together without spending much money?

Choose a walkable or transit-connected area and inventory:

- libraries
- parks
- plazas
- playgrounds
- community centers
- faith spaces with public/community uses
- markets
- beaches / trails
- courtyards
- inexpensive cafes
- recreation facilities
- gardens
- informal gathering places

For each ask:

- Is it free or inexpensive?
- Are restrooms available?
- Is there shade / shelter?
- Are children welcome?
- Are older and disabled people able to use it?
- Can people linger?
- Are there hours when it is unavailable?
- Is transit or walking realistic?
- Does it support repeated encounters?

This can become a neighborhood social-infrastructure map.

# Active Experiment 05 — Living-World Access Audit

**Question:** Does a neighborhood provide usable access to outdoor and living systems rather than merely acreage on a map?

Observe:

- tree canopy / shade where climate requires it
- parks and natural areas
- safe walking routes
- gardens or growing opportunities
- soil safety where food is grown
- habitat / biodiversity
- children’s access
- renter access
- water availability
- maintenance
- public seating
- heat exposure

The key distinction is **usable access** versus symbolic green space.

# Active Experiment 06 — Bureaucracy Receipt

**Question:** How much time does a system require from the person it is supposedly serving?

Choose one real administrative process:

- healthcare
- benefits
- permitting
- school enrollment
- insurance
- banking
- employment onboarding
- public records
- licensing

Record:

- forms completed
- duplicate information
- calls
- transfers
- hold time
- logins
- documents requested
- days elapsed
- errors
- appeals
- in-person trips
- total active human time

Then ask which steps:

- protect safety or rights;
- provide necessary information;
- exist because systems cannot exchange data;
- exist because responsibility is unclear;
- could be automated;
- require a human appeal path.

This creates a concrete measure of institutional legibility.

# Active Experiment 07 — Local Project, Real Receipt

**Question:** Can one small physical improvement generate evidence for a larger Human Scale claim?

Possible examples:

- safer crossing request
- shade / tree project
- community garden improvement
- repair event
- tool library
- neighborhood meal
- school garden
- public bench or seating request
- free community workshop
- walking group
- habitat restoration
- shared compost

Document:

- the initial problem
- who had authority
- approvals required
- money and labor
- participation
- opposition
- maintenance
- measurable outcome
- what failed
- what should be tried next

A small project is not proof of a political theory.

It is evidence about implementation.

# Contributing Results

Useful contributions include:

- a counterexample to a doctrine claim;
- a better primary source;
- a failed Human Scale experiment;
- a successful local project with receipts;
- a correction to an evidence grade;
- a design prototype;
- a clearer explanation;
- a tradeoff the project is ignoring;
- an accessibility concern;
- a case where “human scale” becomes exclusionary, inefficient, unsafe, or romantic.

The project does not need applause as much as it needs **information that changes what we build.**
