The Economics of Freedom · Essay 01

Where Did the Productivity Go?

We have machines that can do the work of hundreds. So why does financial security still feel like a full-time job?

I'm a stagehand and a programmer. I've seen how technology changes work from both sides: a tool can make one person dramatically more capable, but that doesn't guarantee the person gets paid more or goes home earlier.

It's tempting to assume that if society produces more with less labor, everyone should automatically enjoy more leisure. But productivity is a measure of output relative to inputs—not a rule about who receives the benefits.

One machine, several possible futures

Suppose a workshop makes 100 chairs a week with ten workers. New machinery lets it make 200 with the same team. That gain could become higher wages, lower chair prices, shorter workweeks, more profit, or some combination. It could also support new investment. Technology creates possibilities; ownership, competition, institutions and bargaining power influence which possibility becomes reality.

Why doesn't everything get cheaper?

Some goods have become remarkably inexpensive relative to their capabilities—consider computing. Other essentials, especially housing in desirable locations, are constrained by land, infrastructure, permitting and financing. Healthcare and education involve complex labor, regulation and payment systems. Faster computers cannot manufacture more coastline.

Three different kinds of freedom

  1. Income security: Enough reliable cash flow to cover necessities and unexpected costs.
  2. Time freedom: The ability to reduce compulsory work without losing security.
  3. Creative freedom: Access to tools, knowledge and opportunity to build useful things.

These overlap, but they aren't interchangeable. A high salary with no time is different from low expenses with modest income. A free software tool can increase creative freedom without paying the rent.

Open source is a clue, not the whole answer

Software has an unusual economic property: after the initial work, another copy can be distributed at extremely low marginal cost. Open source lets that knowledge circulate. Physical abundance is harder. Chips require fabrication plants; solar panels need materials and installation; houses require land, labor and permits. But sharing designs, repair knowledge and automation can still reduce duplication and lower barriers to entry.

Who gets the upside?

There isn't one simple villain or one magic fix. Competitive markets can pass productivity gains to customers through lower prices. Workers can benefit through pay and reduced hours. Owners can reinvest profits and expand production. Public infrastructure, taxes and social insurance can spread some benefits more widely. Cooperatives and employee ownership offer additional paths. Each arrangement has trade-offs, and results matter more than labels.

Our goal shouldn't be to eliminate useful work. It should be to eliminate unnecessary insecurity.

A practical experiment for 3DVR

Let's take one open-source automation and measure it honestly. How many hours does it save? What does it cost to run, maintain and repair? How much value reaches the user rather than just the vendor? Could another person self-host it, modify it and build a livelihood around it?

That's a small experiment, not a universal economic revolution. But an economy of freedom will be built from testable improvements like these, alongside better institutions and fairer access to essentials.
