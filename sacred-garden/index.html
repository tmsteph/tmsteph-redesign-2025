Living world · civilization · spirituality

The Sacred Garden

Some plants seem to follow humanity everywhere. We eat them, wear them, burn them for light, build with them, heal with them, anoint with them, alter consciousness with them, and eventually make them symbols of the sacred.

A companion to The Garden Is Open Source.

More than useful

I keep noticing that certain plants and fungi feel almost holy. Not because every culture agrees on one sacred list, and not because every traditional use has been scientifically validated, but because some living organisms become woven through so many layers of human life that they stop feeling like mere commodities.

They become food and medicine, clothing and shelter, fragrance and fire, ceremony and memory. Sometimes they become a bridge between ordinary consciousness and experiences people interpret as spiritual.

Maybe a sacred plant is sometimes simply a living thing that humanity has discovered how to be in relationship with.

Olive: food, light, anointing

The olive is one of the clearest examples. Its fruit feeds us. Its oil cooks and preserves food. Historically, olive oil also fueled lamps, cared for skin, carried perfumes and medicines, and became economically important across the Mediterranean.

Then it crosses into the symbolic world. Olive branches become signs of peace. Olive oil appears in Jewish and Christian traditions of consecration and anointing. A substance used at the dinner table and in the lamp becomes a substance used to mark something—or someone—as set apart.

That connection between nourishment, light, healing, and blessing feels almost impossibly complete.

A small civilization in the garden

🌿 Rosemary

Food, fragrance and traditional herbal use; also associated with remembrance and ritual in parts of Europe and the Mediterranean.

🌾 Flax

Seeds and oil, but also linen: a plant transformed into thread, clothing, sails and household cloth.

🪻 Lavender

Fragrance, gardens and traditional preparations; strongly associated with cleanliness, calm and domestic life.

🌱 Hemp & cannabis

Fiber, cordage, seed and oil alongside psychoactive and medicinal uses of cannabis varieties.

🍄 Fungi

Not plants at all, but decomposers, foods, fermentation partners, medicines and—in some species—sources of psychedelic compounds.

🌾 Wheat

A foundation of settled agriculture in parts of the ancient world, transformed into bread and embedded deeply in religious symbolism.

🍇 Grapevine

Fruit, raisins, wine, trade and ceremony: agriculture intertwined with hospitality, celebration and religious imagery.

🌲 Cedar

Durable, aromatic timber used in buildings and objects, with ceremonial significance in multiple cultures.

🔥 Frankincense & myrrh

Aromatic resins whose trade, fragrance, ritual use and biblical traditions made them much more than pleasant smells.

🌿 Sage

A broad group spanning culinary and medicinal traditions. Some species hold specific ceremonial roles in Indigenous cultures that should not be collapsed into a generic idea of “smudging.”

Plants as technology

We tend to imagine technology as something that begins with metal, electricity, or silicon. But domesticated plants were among humanity's earliest powerful technologies.

Flax turns sunlight and soil into linen. Trees turn them into structural material and fuel. Grain stores solar energy in a form that can survive a winter. Hemp becomes cordage and cloth. Grapes become a fermentable sugar reservoir. Herbs concentrate aromatic chemicals that humans learned to use in food, fragrance and traditional medicine.

None of this makes the organism merely a machine. That is the beautiful part. The technology is alive before we ever touch it.

Plants as sacrament

Human beings repeatedly bring the living world into ceremony: oil for anointing, bread and wine at the Christian table, incense rising as smoke, flowers at weddings and funerals, sacred groves, ritual herbs, and psychoactive plants or fungi in some religious traditions.

These practices are not interchangeable. Their meanings belong to particular peoples, histories and religions. But together they reveal something about us: when humans try to make invisible ideas tangible, we very often reach for living things.

We use a seed to speak about death and rebirth. Oil to speak about blessing. Bread to speak about life. Wine to speak about covenant and celebration. Smoke to make prayer visible.

The fungal reminder

Mushrooms make the picture stranger. Fungi are their own kingdom, evolutionarily distinct from plants. Their underground networks and decomposing bodies help cycle nutrients through ecosystems. Yeasts participate in bread, beer and wine. Fungal compounds have also given medicine important drugs.

Some fungi produce psilocybin, capable of profoundly altering perception and subjective experience. Claims about ancient psychedelic use vary greatly in evidential strength, so I do not want to turn mystery into fake certainty. What is clear is that some cultures have used psychoactive organisms ceremonially, and modern research is again studying psychedelics under controlled conditions.

The spiritual interpretation remains a human one. The chemistry is real; what the experience ultimately means is a larger question.

Maybe holiness begins with relationship

I do not think we need to pretend every plant is magical medicine, or attach supernatural claims where evidence does not support them, to recover a sense of reverence.

A tree can already perform something astonishing: sunlight, air, water and minerals become wood, fruit, shade and habitat. A flax stem can become clothing. An olive can become food and light. A microscopic yeast can transform grain or fruit into entirely new foods. Fungi can turn death back into soil.

Science does not make that less sacred to me. Understanding the mechanism can make the relationship more astonishing.

The ancient world was not surrounded by “natural resources.” It was surrounded by other living beings—and civilization grew out of learning their names, seasons, gifts, dangers and needs.

Return to the garden, consciously

Modern life makes it easy to encounter the products while forgetting the organisms. Oil arrives in a bottle. Linen arrives as a shirt. Bread arrives sliced. Medicine arrives as a pill. Wood arrives as lumber.

Maybe part of becoming human again is tracing those things backward.

Plant rosemary. See flax flower. Touch an olive tree. Learn which fungi live under the trees near home. Bake bread. Notice where incense comes from. Grow something whose usefulness you cannot reduce to a price.

The living world should not be something we escape to. It is the older system inside which every human system still runs.
